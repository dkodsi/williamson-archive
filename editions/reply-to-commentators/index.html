Reply to commentators

Timothy Williamson

Philosophy and Phenomenological Research 57 (4) (1997): 945–953.

In their intriguing comments, Paul Horwich and Stephen Schiffer accept one main thesis of Vagueness, that vague sentences express true or false propositions in borderline cases. Neither of them denies the further thesis that we cannot know which truth-values those propositions have. Of course I will concentrate on their objections to more specific features of my view, hoping that this will not obscure the many points of agreement.

Borderline Cases

Vagueness is the capacity for borderline cases. On my view, the mark of a borderline case is a distinctive kind of obstacle to knowledge. Although we understand the word ‘bald’ and know the exact distribution of hairs on Jack’s scalp, we are not in a position to know whether Jack is bald. We can usually recognize whether the obstacle to knowledge is of the kind that constitutes vagueness; I discuss the underlying nature of this kind below. Horwich disputes this epistemic characterization of borderline cases:

[T]he essence of this phenomenon is not that borderline predications cannot be known to be correct. The essence of it […] is that in certain cases we are normally unwilling to apply the predicate, unwilling to deny that it applies, and confident that no further investigation could yield a decision. The problem of knowledge in such circumstances is a result of this paralysis of judgement.

Horwich notwithstanding, paralysis of judgement is quite unnecessary for vagueness. Consider an opinionated macho community, in which everyone applies the term ‘bald’ or its negation confidently and unhesitatingly on the basis of impressions gained from casual observation whenever the issue arises. There is no appeal to precise necessary and sufficient conditions. Speakers accept that the application of ‘bald’ depends only on the exact distribution of hairs on someone’s scalp, but vagaries of mood and perception cause them often to apply the term ‘bald’ when the distribution is exactly the same as in a previous case which they classified as ‘not bald’. When they disagree, each dogmatically insists that the other is clearly wrong. When inconsistencies are pointed out in a single speaker’s application of the term, they are denied ad hoc (‘I never said that!’, ‘His hair has grown since then!’). On Horwich’s account, ‘bald’ is not vague in the language of this community, because there are no cases in which speakers ‘are normally unwilling to apply the predicate, unwilling to deny that it applies, and confident that no further investigation could yield a decision’. But ‘bald’ is vague in the language of this community. If every speaker has about a 50% chance of dogmatically applying the term ‘bald’ to Jack and about a 50% chance of dogmatically applying the term ‘not bald’, then in the envisaged circumstances Jack is a borderline case for ‘bald’, even though no speaker recognizes him as such. Neither ‘Jack is bald’ nor ‘Jack is not bald’ is clearly true. Neither those who say ‘Jack is bald’ nor those who say ‘Jack is not bald’ know the truth of what they are saying. That is the point of characterizing borderline cases epistemically; Horwich’s non-epistemic characterization fastens onto an inessential accompaniment of vagueness.1

The members of the imagined community lack self-knowledge. They are spectacularly bad at knowing that they do not know. We are somewhat better in this respect, although obviously, inevitably and massively fallible. When one does not know whether something is the case, knowing that one does not know has survival value; it prevents rash action. The macho community may suffer for its epistemic overconfidence. In a borderline case, we know that we are not in a position to know whether Jack is bald, and do not waste time in sterile debate. Sometimes we know that we do not know whether Jack is bald because we know that we are not inclined to make a judgement either way; sometimes we are inclined to make such a judgement, but still know that we do not know, because we know that the inclination is too shaky for knowledge. We can recognize our ignorance in this way without conscious epistemological theorizing. Likewise, if one is inclined to judge that a passer-by is an Italian on the basis of her appearance, one need not be an epistemologist to reflect that, since judgements on such a basis are highly unreliable, one does not know whether she is an Italian. But the explanation of our knowledge that we do not know in borderline cases is in any case a secondary issue; they remain borderline even if we believe that we do know, for our belief is false.

Explanations of Ignorance

On the epistemic view, we can suppose that baldness is the property of having fewer than 3,832 hairs on one’s scalp.2 Furthermore, reference supervenes on nonintentional use-and-environment properties, so for some such property π\mathrm{\pi}:

(1)
Necessarily, whatever has π\mathrm{\pi} refers to the property of having fewer than 3,832 hairs on one’s scalp;
(2)
‘Bald’ has π\mathrm{\pi}.

As Schiffer notes, if we could know both (1) and (2), we could deduce that ‘bald’ refers to the property of having fewer than 3,832 hairs on one’s scalp.3 Since we already know that what ‘bald’ refers to is baldness, we could come to know that baldness is the property of having 3,832 hairs on one’s scalp, contrary to the epistemic view. Thus the view implies that we cannot know the conjunction of (1) and (2).4 What explains our ignorance? It is not the vagueness of ‘bald’, for that word is used in neither (1) nor (2); although it is mentioned in (2), that does not make (2) vague.5 In effect, I gave a brief account of our ignorance of (1) and (2) in an article. Schiffer objects on two grounds. First, that account is independent of my general theory of the ignorance that constitutes vagueness, and must be so because our ignorance of (1) and (2) does not involve vagueness, so the general theory is inadequate. Second, he finds the account of our ignorance of (1) and (2) unconvincing in itself.

Schiffer is right to be dissatisfied with the account in the article of our ignorance of (1) and (2). Although it is not false as far as it goes, it does not go far enough. For our ignorance of (1) does depend on vagueness: not vagueness in ‘bald’, of course, but vagueness in ‘refer’. It is vague whether having π\mathrm{\pi} is sufficient for referring to the property of having fewer than 3,832 hairs on one’s scalp. Having π\mathrm{\pi} is neither clearly sufficient nor clearly insufficient for having that property. The word ‘bald’ is a borderline case for the predicate ‘refers to the property of having fewer than 3,832 hairs on one’s scalp’. More generally, semantic ascent preserves vagueness. For example, since it is clear that something is bald if and only if it is in the extension of ‘bald’, ‘bald’ has the same borderline cases as ‘in the extension of “bald”’.

My general explanation of the ignorance that constitutes vagueness extends to semantic terms. Although someone may judge truly ‘Baldness is the property of having fewer than 3,832 hairs on one’s scalp’, the judgement does not express knowledge, for whatever produced a judgement in those words could very easily have done so even if the overall use of ‘bald’ had been very slightly shifted (as it could very easily have been) in such a way that it referred to the property of having fewer than 3,831 hairs on one’s scalp, in which case the judgement then made in those words would have been false. What produces the judgement does not produce true judgements reliably enough to produce knowledge. Thus we are not in a position to know that baldness is the property of having fewer than 3,832 hairs on one’s scalp.6 To extend this explanation of our non-semantic ignorance to an explanation of our semantic ignorance, note that in the envisaged counterfactual circumstances the sentence “‘Bald” refers to baldness’ naturally still commands assent (clearly, ‘bald’ refers to baldness). In those circumstances, the false judgement in the words ‘Baldness is the property of having fewer than 3,832 hairs on one’s scalp’ goes with a false judgement in the words “‘Baldness” refers to the property of having fewer than 3,832 hairs on one’s scalp’. Although someone may use the latter words to make a true judgement in the actual circumstances, the judgement does not express knowledge, for what produces it does not produce true judgements reliably enough to produce knowledge. Thus the account explains equally why we are not in a position to know that ‘baldness’ refers to the property of having fewer than 3,832 hairs on one’s scalp.

The effect of Schiffer’s challenge is to confirm the book’s original explanation of our ignorance. Horwich is sceptical about two aspects of that explanation.

First, Horwich does not see why small shifts in use should induce corresponding small shifts in reference. He is right that the postulated correspondence, although simple, natural and plausible, is not indubitable. But why should it be indubitable? In science, when we want to explain some effect, we look for simple, natural and plausible assumptions that would explain it; we do not require them to be indubitable. Their ability to explain the effect is itself a further reason to believe them, by inference to the best explanation. And what is the alternative to the postulated correspondence between shifts in use and shifts in reference? The reference of a vague term would be insensitive to most small shifts in use, but would change in large jerks as the pattern of use shifted across critical boundaries. The privileged extensions would mark out something like hidden natural kinds. Situations like the following would obtain. ‘Bald’ actually refers to the property of having fewer than 3,832 hairs on one’s scalp. If we had been as likely to apply ‘bald’ to someone with n hairs as we actually are to apply it to someone with n+100n+100 hairs (for all n), then ‘bald’ would still have referred to the property of having fewer than 3,832 hairs on one’s scalp. But if we had been as likely to apply ‘bald’ to someone with n hairs as we actually are to apply it to someone with n+1,000n+1{,}000 hairs (for all n), then ‘bald’ would have referred to the property of having fewer than 2,832 hairs on one’s scalp. 3,832 and 2,832 are natural boundaries of a sort; 3,732 is not. That is implausible, although not incoherent. What is implausible is the supposed naturalness of the boundaries, not their hiddenness. Of course, Horwich may offer an explanation of our ignorance compatible with the jerky hypothesis, but I argue below that he has not done so. If the only available explanation of our ignorance assumes the smooth hypothesis, that strengthens the case by inference to the best explanation for accepting the smooth hypothesis.

Second, Horwich asks why someone who makes a judgement in vague words might very easily still have made a judgement in those words even if the overall pattern of use had been slightly different. The book gives an answer to his question (p. 231). The overall use of a vague term has a sprawling quality: it involves dispositions to use the term in a wide variety of contexts. A change in my dispositions with respect to some contexts does not imply a change with respect to all. For example, if malevolent moods had made me more willing to call people ‘bald’ than they actually do, benevolent moods might not have made me less willing to call people ‘bald’ than they actually do. Moreover, interpersonal meaning supervenes on interpersonal use (and the environment); my use of a word might have been the same as my actual use even if other speakers’ use of it had been slightly different from their actual use. When I make a judgement in a given context, I might very easily still have made a judgement in the same words in that context even if other speakers’ use had been slightly different in ways such that my reference would have been different. That the overall use of a vague term has this sprawling quality is also not indubitable, but it is plausible, and we have further reason to accept it when it enables us to explain our ignorance in borderline cases, and other explanations fail.

Horwich purports to give an alternative explanation of our ignorance in borderline cases. He encodes the use of a predicate “H” in a function A(H).

[T]here are cases in which we are unwilling to confidently apply either “H” or “-H”. Moreover, since the regularity A(H) gives the full, basic explanation of the use of “H”, any decision about these borderline objects would have to be a consequence of it. But […] there appear to be no plausible auxiliary facts which don’t concern “H” but which, in conjunction with A(H), would explain such a decision. Therefore, insofar as A(H) is really the complete articulation of the basic regularity governing our use of “H”, then no matter what else is discovered, it cannot imply a confident application of either “H” or “-H” to the borderline objects. Thus we have explained our conviction that further information about the borderline objects will not help us to reach a decision about which are the ones that “H” applies to.

Of course, the further information that “H” applies to a particular borderline case or the further information that it does not would enable us to reach a decision in that case, and on Horwich’s view there is such a fact to be informed of. What is to be explained is our inability to be informed of it.

Does Horwich mean to explain our failure to decide the borderline cases at time t by the assumption that our use of “H” at t falls under the regularity A(H)? The explanation would be trivial, for the assumption includes what has to be explained; A(H) simply records our failure to decide the borderline cases, which is part of our use. Perhaps Horwich means to explain why A(H) is an equilibrium in use: if our use of “H” at t falls under A(H), then further reflection and investigation will not cause our use of “H” at a slightly later time t* to fall under a different regularity A*(H), on which “H” or “-H” would be confidently applied in the cases that were borderline at t. But Horwich has not explained even that. Whether “H” applies in a borderline case supervenes on A(H) and auxiliary facts not concerning “H”. If we could know the relevant supervenience conditional and A(H) (compare Schiffer’s (1) and (2)), we could work out whether “H” applies. On Horwich’s view, what we can’t know is the supervenience conditional (he does not deny that we can know A(H)). Why can’t we know the supervenience conditional? Horwich says only that ‘there appear to be no plausible auxiliary facts which don’t concern “H” but which, in conjunction with A(H), would explain such a decision’. But if we could know the supervenience conditional, that knowledge would explain such a decision. Presumably, Horwich counts the truth of the conditional as a fact but not a ‘plausible’ fact. That is merely to restate what has to be explained. He has not produced an alternative non-trivial explanation of our ignorance in borderline cases.

Our ignorance is not just a failure to decide. Although many speakers do not confidently apply “H” or “-H” in a given borderline case, some do. An eccentric speaker who confidently applies “H” to a borderline case that is H has a true belief; why does it not constitute knowledge? Horwich’s discussion suggests no answer.

Default Principles

As Horwich notes, my explanation of our ignorance does not extend automatically to cases not covered by the stipulations that wholly determine the meaning of an artificially introduced term. They are not paradigmatic borderline cases, not least because in principle they need not involve higher-order vagueness. They have a kind of cognitive transparency which might conceivably enable us to overcome the usual obstacles to knowledge. The epistemic view is not committed to the inevitability of ignorance in these special cases.

In the book (p. 213) and elsewhere, I consider default principles that might settle truth-values in such cases. Here are two simple possibilities:

DEFAULT-T
When everything else is equal, an atomic predication is true.
DEFAULT-F
When everything else is equal, an atomic predication is false.

Horwich agrees that “a is H” is true or false even when our attitudes to “a is H” and to “a is -H” are perfectly symmetrical. Thus default principles of some kind operate on his view too. They might be less general than DEFAULT-T and DEFAULT-F, each perhaps applicable only to a single predicate, although he offers no reason to think that there is such variation from case to case. At their most specific, default principles are simply supervenience conditionals. Presumably what Horwich finds ‘far-fetched’ is the idea that we might learn the default principles, although he offers no non-trivial explanation of our inability to learn them. I too am unsure that we can come to know them. Equally, we do not know that DEFAULT-T is false, and we do not know that DEFAULT-F is false. The epistemic view is consistent with each, and entails neither.7

If we cannot know the default principles, the problem may be that the default is truth in some kinds of case and falsity in others, cases of each kind being too close to cases of the other to enable us to discriminate between them. The explanation of our ignorance of the default principles would follow the same lines as the explanation of our ignorance in borderline cases.

Alternatively, perhaps we can know general default principles, by reflection on the concepts of truth and falsity. Even so, we may still be unable to know truth-values in particular cases. In order to apply the principles, we should need to know that everything else was equal, and we might be unable to know that. Cases of perfect symmetry might be too close to cases of slight asymmetry. Even when we make explicit stipulations, slight asymmetries in the use those stipulations caused us to make of the defined terms would tip the balance. Again, the explanation of our ignorance in these cases could be subsumed under my general explanation of our ignorance in borderline cases.

I see no special problem for the epistemic view here, but plenty of scope for further inquiry into the nature of stipulation.

Vague Communication

Schiffer raises a problem about communication. On my view, slight differences in the use of a vague term typically make slight differences in its meaning. But different speakers use a vague term in slightly different ways. We may therefore expect what they mean by it to be slightly different too. Moreover, the principles underlying my explanation of ignorance in borderline cases seem to imply that one speaker’s belief about what another means by the vague term would not count as knowledge, even if it happened to be true. Thus one speaker could not know what another meant by a vague term, and communication would break down.

Schiffer’s worry is addressed in the book (p. 211):

Perhaps no two speakers of English match exactly in their dispositions to use ‘thin’. It does not follow that no two speakers of English mean exactly the same by ‘thin’. For what individual speakers mean by a word can be parasitic on its meaning in a public language. The dispositions of all practitioners collectively determine a sense that is available to each.8

In a footnote, Schiffer expresses scepticism about a similar line, but gives no reason for his scepticism. Does he doubt that meaning can be socially determined? I do not say that all meaning is socially determined. Schiffer’s problem is solved if, in clear cases of successful communication, meaning is determined by use (and the environment) at the level of a community that includes both the speaker and the hearer.

The notion of a speech community is itself vague. What are the spatial and temporal boundaries of my present speech community? Some words differ in meaning between Britain and America; some words have changed in meaning over the last thirty years. A sorites paradox threatens the idea that meaning is determined by use in a community that extends over both space and time. But the paradox can be solved. Consider a particular episode of successful communication. The speaker says ‘Jack is bald’. The hearer thinks to himself ‘She said that Jack is bald’. ‘Say’ and ‘speech community’ vary slightly in content from context to context. In the context of the hearer’s thought, the speaker at the time of speaking and the hearer at the time of hearing count as members of the same community; what people count as saying in this context depends on which speech community they count as members of in this context. In this context, the hearer’s thought is true, and can even constitute an expression of knowledge. Of course, if the speaker and hearer used ‘bald’ in radically different ways, they might not count as members of the same speech community for the purpose of determining its meaning; but they can count as members of the same speech community for this purpose even if they use it in slightly different ways. The sorites paradox does not arise, because in a given context not all slight differences in use imply sameness in speech community: the implication holds only for some slight differences that are salient in that context.

The issue of communication arises equally for non-epistemic views of vagueness. Typically, they claim that the connection between meaning and use is cognitively more transparent than it is on the epistemic view; they have even more reason than the epistemic view has to accept that slight differences in the use of a vague term make slight differences in its meaning. For example, suppose that the meaning of ‘bald’ were given by a function B from natural numbers to real numbers in the interval [0,1], where B(n) is the degree to which ‘bald’ applies to someone with n hairs, conceived as something like the probability that a normal speaker would be willing to apply the term ‘bald’ to someone with n hairs in normal conditions of observation. Then a slight difference in these probabilities would constitute a slight difference in use; it would change B to a slightly different function B*. That would constitute a slight difference in meaning. How then is communication possible? Non-epistemic views of vagueness too could answer the question by appeal to the social determination of meaning.

Is communication possible when speaker and hearer do not belong to the same speech community? They can still know that they mean at least roughly the same by a word. That is enough for most practical purposes. Again, this answer is independent of the epistemic view of vagueness.

Vague Intentions

Schiffer’s final objection is that the epistemic account conflicts with the role of intention in demonstrative reference:

my intentions in uttering ‘she’ determine the person to whom I’m referring. In this case, of course, I must have some way of thinking of that person other than as “she”. I must, it would seem, have some way of identifying my referent, some way of picking her out.

Schiffer objects that when one uses the demonstrative ‘here’ with ordinary vagueness, one has no way of identifying or picking out the sharply demarcated region to which one is referring, on the epistemic view. I am not sure what Schiffer means by a way of ‘identifying’ or ‘picking out’ the referent. It would be unreasonable to require the speaker to be able to refer non-demonstratively to the thing in question. Nor need the speaker know how to trace its boundaries in practice (consider ‘this galaxy’). We should like an answer to the question ‘Why does the demonstrative refer to x?’. Presumably, a central part of the answer will often be that the speaker is perceptually attending to x. But Schiffer gives no reason to suppose that one can perceptually attend to x only if one can locate the exact boundaries of x. ‘Perceptually attend’ is a vague expression; it is vague just what information link is needed between me and a point of space for that point to be part of the region to which I am perceptually attending. The epistemic view applies to the vagueness of ‘perceptually attend’. I can perceptually attend to the region with exact boundaries b even if no one is in a position to know that I am perceptually attending to the region with exact boundaries b; what I know is that I am looking at this region here. The epistemic view is consistent with all reasonable constraints on the role of intention in demonstrative reference.
