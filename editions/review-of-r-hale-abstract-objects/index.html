Review of R. Hale, Abstract Objects

Timothy Williamson

Mind 97 (387) (1988): 487–490.

Bob Hale’s monograph continues the tradition of Fregean thought about abstract objects to be found in Michael Dummett’s Frege, Philosophy of Language and Crispin Wright’s Frege’s Conception of Numbers as Objects. All those should enjoy it who finished Wright’s book asking for more. The reader is required to take little on trust except the general Frege/Dummett framework (for example, the murky notion of a criterion of identity is used without defence enough to impress its critics). My comments will focus on points of criticism rather than praise, for the usual reasons; those who have read the author’s earlier work will not be surprised to learn that Abstract Objects is a thoughtful and sophisticated performance, in argument fair-minded, patient, and often acute, in style pleasantly concise. Much recent work in its area is put under sharp scrutiny: Field on nominalism, Maddy on mathematical intuition, Kitcher and Edidin on the a priori, Hodes on arithmetic, McIntosh on Skolem. There is a welcome preparedness to follow the argument where it leads without claiming for the point of arrival any more permanent status than it deserves.

Hale undertakes to defend a version of platonism, which he characterizes as the view that there are mind-independent abstract objects. His main argument for platonism is the Fregean one, that some true assertions contain singular terms which denote abstract objects if they denote anything; since the assertions are true, the singular terms denote. Mind-independence is a further issue; Hale takes it to mean that truths about the abstract objects hold independently of us; since its subsequent appearances are brief, it will not be discussed here. The relevant notions of singular term and abstract object are carefully examined in a chapter each. Both are given complicated, ad hoc analyses of the kind to which many of us react with the thought ‘Only lack of ingenuity will prevent me from finding an intuitive counter-example to this’. Hale’s analyses do indeed have counter-intuitive consequences, but there is a more serious point. We have no prior reason to suppose that philosophically significant concepts have interesting analyses into necessary and sufficient conditions. Our track record in finding them is poor. Hale follows the usual method of adding epicycles to a proposed analysis in the face of counter-examples until no more of the latter come to mind. What confidence is it rational to have in the result? One’s assurance that e (2.718…) is an abstract object if anything, and ‘e’ a singular term, will not be greatly increased if one learns that they fall under extremely tentative analyses of these notions. Even if one believed some messy analysis to be correct, the obvious conclusion to draw would be that the notion so defined had turned out to be unsuitable for systematic theorizing. It is true that, if the Fregean argument is not to beg the question, we must be able to recognize an expression as a singular term without first accepting that it denotes, and Hale’s modification of Dummett’s inferential criterion for singular termhood is intended to serve this purpose. However, platonists seem to have some other way of recognizing ‘7’ as a singular term—they are unlikely to have been using Hale’s criterion prior to reading his book—and this way will not presuppose that ‘7’ denotes 7, since they can recognize ‘7’ as a singular term in the same way as they can recognize ‘G’ as one, where G is defined to be the least even number greater than 2 which is not the sum of two primes; sensible platonists lack the belief that ‘G’ denotes G. Since the cues platonists tacitly but actually use carry more conviction than do recherché analyses, it might be worth finding out what they are. They seem to be linguistic rather than logical in character.

Hale’s criterion for singular termhood leads him into difficulty with candidates such as ‘the age of the Queen’. He is driven to the implausible view that it has quite different meanings in ‘The age of the Queen is less than that of the Poet Laureate’, where it refers to a quantity, and ‘The age of the Queen is known only by her closest associates’, where it refers to a proposition stating her age (p. 29; what does it refer to in ‘The age of the Queen is rising’?). Hale’s treatment of abstract nouns such as ‘wisdom’ and ‘sliminess’ is disappointing. The possibility that they denote what the corresponding adjectives express in some other way is dismissed by the invocation of Frege’s name (pp. 4, 33). Universals are claimed to lack general criteria of identity, without any consideration of the obvious candidate: necessary co-instantiation (e.g. p. 40). It is strangely assumed that one can recognize cases of the quality of mercy as such only if one has a description approximately synonymous with ‘merciful’ (ibid.). The analysis of the abstract/concrete distinction (pp. 59–63) seems to imply that the time but not the place of an event is abstract.

The middle chapters of the book contain useful treatments of knowledge and identifying thought about abstract objects, but I shall discuss only the long final chapter, on indeterminacy of reference as a threat to platonism. Hale takes the heroic course of defending a particular identification of numbers with sets as the correct one against Benacerraf’s familiar argument in ‘What Numbers Could Not Be’. He argues that numbers are equivalence classes of concepts under the relation of 1–1 correlation between their extensions—call these cardinality classes—on the grounds that these are the classes grasp of whose membership condition is in effect grasp of the Fregean criterion of identity for numbers (I omit much detail). Several things seem to have gone wrong. For instance, classes have many membership conditions (by extensionality); some membership conditions for cardinality classes do not involve 1–1 correlation (the Schröder–Bernstein theorem gives one example) and some membership conditions for non-cardinality classes do involve 1–1 correlation. So far as I can see, Hale says nothing to rule out alternative identifications of numbers with unions of cardinality classes or with their intersections with some class containing concepts with extensions of the relevant cardinalities. Although the issue is supposed to be the identity of the natural numbers, the argument would at most show that the cardinal numbers (including infinite ones) are the cardinality classes: but from this it would by no means follow that the finite cardinal numbers are the cardinality classes of finitely instantiated concepts, as Hale requires. Other difficulties could be mentioned.

If Hale’s identification is not necessary, is it at least possible? He discusses the question of its consistency against the background of standard set-theoretic axioms (pp. 217–19). An argument for inconsistency not considered is the following. Consider the set of singly instantiated concepts, a cardinality class. With each singly instantiated concept can be associated its single instance. Therefore the set of all things which uniquely instantiate some concept exists, by the Replacement Axiom of ZF set theory. Now every set x\mathrm{x} uniquely instantiates some concept, if only the concept of being identical with x\mathrm{x} (we need to be able to specify concepts by means of parameters in this way, since we need to be able to count classes specified by means of parameters and on Hale’s view ‘the number of’ applies in the first instance to concepts). Thus we have a set containing all sets, which leads to the usual contradiction. Hale does have a defence against this argument, since his official view (p. 269 n. 23) is that abstract objects form a hierarchy such that cardinality classes do not exist, and numbers are really classes all of whose elements have less than a given rank; however, he suggests that contradiction might be avoided without appeal to this point (p. 269 n. 25). The official view raises further questions. Is there such a thing as the number of objects falling under a given concept (e.g. being self-identical, being a set) when those objects do not form a set? If so, how are considerations of rank to be accommodated? If not, it looks as though the primary application of ‘the number of’ is to sets, not—as Hale would have it—to classes. A better-defined theory of concepts and classes is required.

Hale considers Harold Hodes’s argument that the platonist cannot say what the microstructure of reference is in virtue of which the standard numberer (taking each concept to the number of its instances) rather than some variant of it (for which the numbers have been permuted) is what we mean by ‘the number of’. He replies that Hodes can specify the non-standard numberers only in terms of the standard numberer, which suggests that the standard numberer is special after all (p. 223). This seems just to restate the difficulty. If there is a standard numberer then of course it is privileged, but Hodes is suggesting that since the platonist cannot explain why or how one particular function from concepts to numbers should get to have this privilege, one should conclude that there is no standard numberer. As an additional argument, Hale claims that only the standard numberer respects all equivalences of the type ‘The number of F\mathrm{F}s = 4 iff there are exactly 4 F\mathrm{F}s’ (pp. 223–4). However, although the right-hand side is equivalent to an unambiguous formula of quantification theory with identity, ‘4’ is not thereby disambiguated; non-standard numberers merely correspond to non-standard functions from numerical quantifiers to numbers. Finally, Hale suggests that Hodes’s argument is merely a special case of a general permutation argument used by Wallace, Davidson, and Putnam from Frege’s Context Principle to the indeterminacy of all reference; abstract objects would then be no worse off than concrete ones. The idea is that the reference of singular terms depends only on the truth-conditions of sentences (by the Context Principle); since permutations of reference can leave the truth-conditions fixed, reference is indeterminate. Much depends on how truth-conditions are individuated. If logical equivalence is sufficient for sameness of truth-conditions, the argument may be valid but the Context Principle is quite implausible; if truth-conditions are individuated in a way sensitive to sub-sentential structure, the permutations will not preserve truth-conditions. In particular, there would be room for independent causal constraints on reference which might determine it for concrete but not for abstract objects.

A few minor grumbles. There is some confusion between ‘All mental events are abstract’ and ‘All abstract objects are mental’ (pp. 49–50, my formulations). A principle of extensionality for concepts is presupposed without argument (p. 71). Hale argues in effect that any apriority-preserving inference rule will allow us to construct arbitrarily long deductions from a given piece of a priori knowledge (p. 139); this does not seem to apply to elimination rules. Hale dismisses mathematical intuition, but speaks of ‘direct discernment of fundamental conceptual relationships’ (p. 142) without much explanation. There is some discussion of whether ‘each abstract object is the value of some function for some suitable argument’ (pp. 184, 190); so stated the answer is trivially positive; needed refinements are introduced only much later. The putative indeterminacy of reference to sets is discussed under the assumption that the mapping from {x:Fx}\{\mathrm{x}: \mathrm{Fx}\} to {x:Fx∨Hx}\{\mathrm{x}: \mathrm{Fx} \vee \mathrm{Hx}\} (H\mathrm{H} fixed) is 1–1 (pp. 199, 225); consider the null set and {x:Hx}\{\mathrm{x}: \mathrm{Hx}\}. There is a quantifier-shift fallacy from ‘All F\mathrm{F}-identity involves some criterion of identity’ to ‘Some criterion of identity is involved in all F\mathrm{F}-identity’ (pp. 213–14, my formulations). Many formulae lack essential logical symbols (pp. 71–2, 99, 119, 203, 246–7) as a result of proof-reading oversights.

It is easy to stand on the sidelines and berate the players. Bob Hale is to be applauded for the seriousness and intelligence with which he has grappled with appallingly difficult issues.
