Pickel Against Second-Order Primitivism

Timothy Williamson

In Peter Fritz and Nicholas Jones (eds.), Higher-Order Metaphysics (2024), 504–513. Oxford: Oxford University Press.

In ‘Against Second-Order Primitivism’, Bryan Pickel questions the understanding of second-order languages defended in Modal Logic as Metaphysics (Williamson 2013). In particular, he opposes my refusal of reductionist readings of the second-order quantifiers as restrictions of an unrestricted first-order quantifier. In this chapter, I will explain why Pickel’s arguments are not responsive to the main case for an anti-reductionist higher-order approach, and note some troubles into which his reductionism draws him.

1. Paraphrase and Syntax

Let us start with a simple sentence, such as ‘Annabel sings’. In first-order logic, we can quantify into name position, and draw the conclusion ‘Someone sings’. In second-order logic, we can quantify into predicate position, and draw a conclusion which we can crudely render as ‘Annabel somethings’. However, someone may suspect that the corresponding second-order formula is just a fancy way of writing something which could be paraphrased more perspicuously by first-order quantification over entities of some special kind, such as properties, sets, or ways. Pickel offers these paraphrases (his numbering):

(10)
a. There is a property such that Annabel has it.
 b. There is a set such that Annabel belongs to it.
 c. There is a way Annabel is.

The trouble with paraphrases in the style of (10a–c) is that they are inconsistent with the unrestricted second-order comprehension principle.

The relevant second-order comprehension principle makes use of unrestricted first-order quantifiers. Pickel relies on such quantifiers through much of his paper, so they are not the bone of contention. I will write them as ‘everything’ and ‘something’. They subsume the restricted first-order quantifiers over properties, sets, and ways: every property is something; every set is something; every way is something.

For simplicity, we focus on the monadic case of the second-order comprehension principle. It applies the second-order analogue of ‘something’ to bind a monadic predicate variable ‘VV’ in the open sentence ‘(necessarily) for everything xx, VxVx if and only if φ\varphi’, where ‘φ\varphi’ is a schematic letter for any open or closed sentence of the language in which ‘VV’ does not occur free (Williamson 2013: 227). It enables us freely to make the sort of move that took us from ‘Annabel sings’ to ‘Annabel somethings’. More generally, we can use it to subsume the instances of a first-order schema under a single unifying second-order generalization from which they all follow, and which can serve as a hypothesis in its own right. On its intended interpretation, the generalization covers not just all the instances in the language, but also all the potential instances in extensions of the language. For example, Leibniz’s Law can be understood as a second-order generalization about identity, whose intended inferential power comes from the unrestricted comprehension principle. Nor is its relevance confined to logic and metaphysics. An unrestricted comprehension principle is needed to capture the intended strength of some key principles in mathematics too, such as the axiom of mathematical induction in Peano arithmetic and the axiom of replacement in Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory. Without the unrestricted comprehension principle, second-order generalizations cannot play their required unifying role.

If second-order quantifiers are paraphrased in terms of properties, as in (10a), the unrestricted second-order comprehension principle entails that there is a property pp such that for everything xx, xx has pp if and only if xx does not have xx (putting ‘xx does not have xx’ for ‘φ\varphi’). If second-order quantifiers are paraphrased in terms of sets, as in (10b), the unrestricted second-order comprehension principle entails that there is a set ss such that for everything xx, xx belongs to ss if and only if xx does not belong to xx (putting ‘xx does not belong to xx’ for ‘φ\varphi’). If second-order quantifiers are paraphrased in terms of ways, as in (10c), there is a way ww such that for everything xx, xx is the way ww if and only if xx is not the way xx (putting ‘xx is not the way xx’ for ‘φ\varphi’). We can then instantiate ‘everything xx’ by the first-order variable ‘pp’, ‘ss’, or ‘ww’, because ‘everything xx’ is an unrestricted first-order quantifier. In each case, the result is a contradiction, as in Russell’s paradox. Thus the unrestricted second-order comprehension principle is simply inconsistent with first-order paraphrases of second-order language in the style of (10a–c)—inconsistent, that is, with the generalized biconditionals linking second-order sentences to their first-order paraphrases. Since I accept the unrestricted second-order comprehension principle, I reject the first-order paraphrases.

Pickel uses the first-order paraphrases to raise two problems for my approach. They are central to his critique. Here is his first problem (verbatim):

Problem 1: It is natural to interpret the sentences of second-order logic along the lines of (10a-c). From this interpretation, it follows that the second-order variables are merely first-order variables over a restricted domain. The problem is to explain how to interpret the formulae of second-order logic without lapsing into one of these natural interpretations.

I do not explain how to interpret the formulae of second-order logic by paraphrasing them in first-order terms, or by giving an intended semantics for the second-order language in a first-order meta-language. Instead, as Pickel is aware, I propose that one can learn the second-order language by the direct method, through working in second-order logic. He may reply that, nevertheless, the understanding one thereby acquires of the second-order language can in fact be articulated by a first-order paraphrase, whether one likes it or not. On the face of it, that would be a quite uncharitable interpretation. For it invalidates the unrestricted second-order comprehension principle, which is at the heart of the envisaged practice of working in second-order logic. Moreover, the meaning of the second-order quantification at issue supervenes on that practice, precisely because it introduces the second-order vocabulary directly, not as paraphrasing something already understood. By normal standards of interpretation, one should seek an interpretation more charitable to the practice. Of course, Pickel might claim that no possible interpretation of the second-order language validates the unrestricted second-order comprehension principle, but he provides no evidence for such a bold claim.

Consider interpretation in science. When a scientific theory provides powerful, elegant, unifying explanations of the evidence, it would be quixotic for a philosopher to insist that, unfortunately, despite all the theory’s scientific successes, no possible interpretation validates its main principles. For example, giving up on quantum mechanics in view of its difficulties of interpretation would not be a progressive move. By comparison, the difficulties of interpreting unreduced second-order logic are far less pressing. Of course, second-order logic does not have predictive successes like those of quantum mechanics, but in its own way it provides a powerful, elegant, unifying framework for much of mathematics—for instance, it plays a key role in explaining how second-order Peano arithmetic singles out a unique mathematical structure (up to isomorphism). It plays a similar role in explaining how second-order Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory tightly constrains the structure of the set-theoretic hierarchy (see Shapiro 1991 for mathematical details). A more fruitful attitude is abductive with respect to meaning as well as truth: meaningless theories are not true, so abductive evidence for a theory’s truth is also evidence for its meaningfulness.

We have some evidence that second-order logic with unrestricted generality and unrestricted comprehension is a true theory as learnt by the direct method, and so is true on whatever interpretation it receives as learnt that way. By a Russellian paradox, that interpretation is irreducible to first-order terms. Thus we have some evidence that the currently intended interpretation of second-order logic is irreducible to first-order terms.

Most of Pickel’s critique would tell against me only if I were trying to settle the issue of interpreting a second-order language at the level of syntax, prior to doing any substantive theorizing in that language. In section 6, in summing up what he takes himself to have shown in sections 1–4, he writes:

Williamson argued that the syntactic features of the language of second-order logic rendered first- and second-order quantification incommensurable. I have argued that a language with these same syntactic features may be interpreted so that the second-order quantifiers are restricted first-order quantifiers. Thus, the syntactic features of the language of second-order logic are insufficient to render first- and second-order quantification incommensurable.

But I never claimed that the syntactic features of the language of second-order logic are sufficient to render first- and second-order quantification incommensurable. The insufficiency is obvious, since the model theory of second-order logic is often done in a first-order set-theoretic metalanguage. Later in his article, Pickel does criticize some semantic arguments for the incommensurability of first- and second-order quantification, but there he discusses mainly Florio and Jones (2021) and Magidor (2009, 2013). In short, Pickel never addresses my central case for taking first- and second-order quantification to be incommensurable.

Pickel may have been misled by a passage he quotes where I say that the distinction between first- and second-order quantification ‘must remain one of grammar’ (Williamson 2003: 458). In that passage I was rejecting the hope of distinguishing them by contrasting the different kinds of entity quantified over, since that would involve an implicit reduction of second-order quantification to first-order quantification over ‘entities’ of a special kind, just what must be avoided. But the main evidence that such reductions are wrong comes from logical and mathematical theorizing, in particular from the unrestricted second-order comprehension principle.

That is no change in my view. I have never thought that someone quite unacquainted with anything like Russell’s paradox would be well-placed on purely grammatical grounds to argue against a metaphysical reduction of second-order quantification to first-order quantification over properties. Of course, the type-theoretic structure is very natural at the level of syntax, and it is also a natural hypothesis that the syntactic structure reflects the semantic structure. If the semantic structure in turn reflects the metaphysical structure, so that the syntactic structure also reflects the metaphysical structure, then second-order quantification will not be metaphysically reducible to first-order quantification, irrespective of Russell’s paradox. Pickel has not shown such an anti-reductionist view to be wrong. It has its attractions, but so has the view that all generality can be metaphysically unified under first-order generality. What breaks that stand-off is the Russellian paradox. The type-theoretic metaphysics may explain why there is no such reduction to the first-order, but the paradox itself is a crucial part of the evidence in favour of the type-theoretic metaphysics.

Pickel’s focus on the issue of syntax emerges clearly in the second problem he raises (verbatim):

Problem 2: Whether or not the language of second-order logic is read as regimenting natural language constructions such as (10a–c) is not a syntactic matter. Therefore, the syntactic features of this language alone fail to reconcile Unrestricted Generality with the thesis that every predicate has an interpretation.

This requires some explaining. By ‘Unrestricted Generality’, Pickel means the schema ‘∀α𝑇ℎ𝑖𝑛𝑔(α)\forall \alpha\,\mathit{Thing}(\alpha)’, where ‘α\alpha’ can be replaced by any style of variable, and ‘Thing’ is a trivially universal predicate satisfied by everything. Pickel provisionally attributes Unrestricted Generality to me as the ‘most natural way to interpret Williamson’s claim’ in a passage in which I say ‘Any value of a variable is a thing’ (2003: 420). He provisionally attributes ‘the thesis that every predicate has an interpretation’ to me because I treat the predicates of the object-language as interpreted and my approach to interpretation is referentialist in spirit. Together, the schema and the thesis threaten to collapse second-order quantifiers into restricted first-order quantifiers, thereby making second-order logic with the unrestricted comprehension principle inconsistent.

Fortunately, I have no need ‘to reconcile Unrestricted Generality with the thesis that every predicate has an interpretation’, because I reject both sides, as Pickel is aware. I reject the Unrestricted Generality schema because ‘𝑇ℎ𝑖𝑛𝑔(α)\mathit{Thing}(\alpha)’ is not even well-formed when ‘α\alpha’ is replaced by a predicate variable; the result is something like ‘Sings is a thing’. That is quite consistent with my claim ‘Any value of a variable is a thing’, for ‘value’ is just an ordinary noun, like ‘thing’. Strictly speaking, second-order variables do not have values (in using the word ‘any’, I did not presuppose that there are such values). When ‘α\alpha’ is replaced by a higher-order variable, the semantic clause for sentences of the form ‘∀αA\forall \alpha\,A’ will use a variable of the same higher-order type in the meta-language. For a similar reason, the thesis that every predicate has an interpretation is false, for ‘interpretation’ is just an ordinary noun, like ‘thing’. Strictly speaking, second-order variables do not have interpretations. There is a true claim in the vicinity of the false thesis, but it replaces the first-order ‘has an interpretation’ by a second-order analogue. Thus Problem 2 presents no difficulty for my view.

Of course, in giving that account, I have assumed my own view, and will surely not convince Pickel. But I was not trying to convince Pickel. My aim was just to show that my view has a clear answer to his second challenge. If second-order quantification were somehow reducible to first-order quantification, that clear answer would be wrong, but, just as before, the abductive success of the unrestricted second-order comprehension principle is evidence that second-order quantification is not reducible to first-order quantification.

In short, Pickel’s two problems pass my view harmlessly by, for reasons which he recognizes elsewhere in his article. Basing his critique on them was never a promising strategy.

In the remainder of this response, I will turn to two more specific themes in Pickel’s discussion.

2. Constants and Variables

In the course of his dialectic with my account, Pickel argues that second-order variables are not syntactically and semantically analogous to first-order predicates; according to him, the differences between them go well beyond those between variables and constants of a given type (section 4.1 of his paper). That is quite a surprising claim. The predicate constants of a formal first-order language are modelled on predicates in natural language, perhaps tidied up and simplified. Moreover, the predicate variables (second-order variables) of a formal second-order language are supposed to stand to the predicate constants of the language as variables of any type stand to constants of the same type. The semantic clause for a simple formula made up of an nn-place second-order variable followed by a sequence of nn singular terms (constants or variables) is modelled as closely as possible on the semantic clause for a simple formula made up of an actual or hypothetical predicate constant followed by a sequence of nn singular terms. I followed that standard approach in Modal Logic as Metaphysics. How can a significant disanalogy have crept in?

Pickel notes a distributional difference. In a well-formed formula, a second-order variable VV can occur immediately after a quantifier symbol, as in ‘∀V\forall V’; a predicate constant cannot. That is a genuine difference, though its importance is unclear. On a very strict distributional criterion of identity for types, it implies that predicate variables are not of the same type as the corresponding predicate constants.

As we read in Pickel’s paper, the editors of this volume pointed out that a parallel argument would show that individual variables are not of the same type as individual constants, an implausible conclusion which strongly suggests that the criterion of identity for types was set too tight. In response, Pickel proposes that we could in principle allow a first-order quantifier ‘∀n\forall n’, where ‘nn’ is an individual constant, as well as the first-order quantifier ‘∀v\forall v’, where ‘vv’ is an individual variable; he notes that Carnap once did so.1 The semantics of quantification could be uniform for both bound individual variables and bound individual constants, since we could treat individual constants just as individual variables except for their unbound occurrences (pronouns in natural language work rather like that). That would restore the distributional symmetry between individual variables and individual constants.

The trouble with Pickel’s suggestion is that it works just as well for predicates as for singular terms. For the same reason as before, we could in principle allow a second-order quantifier ‘∀F\forall F’, where ‘FF’ is a predicate constant, as well as the second-order quantifier ‘∀V\forall V’, where ‘VV’ is a predicate variable. The semantics of quantification could be uniform for both bound predicate variables and bound predicate constants, since we could treat predicate constants just as predicate variables except for their unbound occurrences. That would restore the distributional symmetry between predicate variables and predicate constants. Thus Pickel’s argument does not point to any deep difference between second-order (predicate) variables and predicate constants, beyond the usual obvious difference between constants and variables of any type when they occur unbound.

3. Logical Truth

In Modal Logic as Metaphysics, I point out a tight connection between Tarski’s early account of logical truth and higher-order logic: a formula is logically true if and only if its higher-order universal generalization is simply true (Williamson 2013: 93–94). Tarski defined logical truth as truth on all interpretations which keep the interpretation of the logical constants fixed, as a special case of his definition of logical consequence (Tarski 1936). At that stage, he seems to have understood languages and interpretations type-theoretically, with a base type of absolutely all individuals; that is significantly different from the later set-theoretic conception of a model, based on an arbitrary set domain (Mancosu 2006). He was not offering a model-theoretic definition of logical consequence in quite the modern sense. His 1936 conception fits better with higher-order logic and unrestricted first-order quantification. For reasons explained above, the noun ‘interpretation’ needs to be read with a pinch of salt. The higher-order universal generalization of a formula is the result of replacing all its non-logical constants of any type by variables of the same type, and then prefixing universal quantifiers of the appropriate types to bind all the variables. Obviously, this account relies on the close semantic connection between variables and constants of a given type defended in the previous section. On the resulting view, logical truth is a matter of very abstract universal patterns, and so of clear scientific interest, in a sense of ‘science’ broad enough to include mathematics as well as the natural sciences. The account has the virtues of austerity and simplicity: it does not invoke any metaphysical idea of necessity, or epistemological idea of apriority or obviousness, or semantic idea of analyticity.

Pickel’s approach is very different. Working within a first-order framework susceptible to Russellian paradoxes, he proposes ‘to restrict the comprehension schema and thereby deny that every predicate has an interpretation’ (section 6). The predicates which lack interpretations are not meaningless by ordinary standards; they are just the predicates which lead to paradoxes when inserted into the comprehension schema. For example, by ordinary standards, the predicate ‘is not a member of itself’ is perfectly meaningful; it occurs in normal set-theoretic discourse (for any set xx, xx is not a member of itself). One can reason validly or invalidly with such a predicate, yet logical consequence, defined as truth-preservation under all interpretations, takes no account of whether such arguments are actually truth-preserving, because no interpretation fits the way we use the predicate.

In his very brief remarks on logical truth, Pickel seems to hint at an epistemological approach: he writes of a logical truth following ‘from visibly sound axioms’. That is disappointingly vague. Is visibility a matter of human psychology? Anyway, what does the word ‘sound’ mean here? Pickel takes the phrase ‘visibly sound’ from a passage he quotes by Quine, where it is glossed as ‘visibly such as to generate only schemata that come out true under all substitutions’ (Quine 1986: 54). This does not expand on ‘visibly’, but suggests that ‘sound’ is being used to mean ‘true under all substitutions’. Alternatively, one might read Pickel as proposing truth under all substitutions as the standard of logical truth, at least for formulas understood as schemata, while visibility just concerns our epistemic access to logical truth.

However, as I understand from Pickel (p.c.), he is not proposing a revision of standard semantic accounts of logical truth and consequence, but only indicating how we can legitimately extend the standard proof theory of first-order logic to arguments involving meaningful predicates with no interpretation, where all interpretations are set-sized. In section 6, he writes:

part of the interest of Tarski’s definition of logical truth (and consequence) is that it supports a completeness theorem for the language of first-order logic: a sentence can be “proved” if it is true on all interpretations. Here ‘proof’ refers to using the familiar methods of first-order logic.

After giving one elementary example, Pickel concludes:

we don’t need to suppose that every possible predicate has an interpretation to deliver a satisfactory account of logical truth or consequence.

The situation is trickier than Pickel suggests. He allows that our language may contain an unrestricted first-order quantifier ∃\exists. He gives no reason not to count it as a logical constant. It meets Tarski’s later criterion of invariance under permutations of individuals for being a logical notion (Tarski 1986). A question naturally arises: which arguments with ∃\exists are always truth-preserving? The question is of obvious logical interest, and the way to bring it under Tarski’s account of logical consequence is by treating ∃\exists as a logical constant, as Tarski implicitly does in his 1936. I will follow him in doing so. Thus the sentence ∃x∃yx≠y\exists x\,\exists y\,x \ne y contains no non-logical constants. As Tarski notes in his 1936 paper, on his account, an argument without non-logical constants is materially truth-preserving if and only if its conclusion is a logical consequence of its premise set. But the argument from the empty premise set to the conclusion ∃x∃yx≠y\exists x\,\exists y\,x \ne y contains no non-logical constant and is materially truth-preserving, since its conclusion is true: there are at least two things, for example the editors of this volume (remember, ∃\exists is unrestricted). Thus ∃x∃yx≠y\exists x\,\exists y\,x \ne y is a logical consequence of the empty set, and so a logical truth. But ∃x∃yx≠y\exists x\,\exists y\,x \ne y is of course not provable ‘using the familiar methods of first-order logic’, since it is false in a domain with only one member. Thus, on Tarski’s 1936 account, the usual completeness theorem for first-order logic does not generalize to first-order languages with unrestricted quantifiers.

We might respond to that specific type of example by adding the formalization of ‘There are at least nn things’ as an axiom for each natural number nn. Are such axioms ‘visibly sound’? The formalization of ‘There are at least nn things’ does contain nn variables, tokens of which will be visible when it is written down, so the axiom is in some sense visibly true, but we surely need a less ad hoc approach.

Consider a standard formalization of ‘RR does not linearly order everything’, with ‘everything’ an unrestricted first-order quantifier and ‘RR’ a dyadic atomic predicate, treated as a non-logical constant (the example is from Friedman 1999). It is of course not a theorem of standard first-order logic, since it is false in models of arithmetic when ‘RR’ is interpreted as ≤\leq. But is it logically true in Tarski’s sense? No interpretation of ‘RR’ linearly orders everything, for interpretations are set-sized by hypothesis, but that does not show that ‘RR linearly orders everything’ cannot be meaningful and true on some use of ‘RR’. If our account of logical consequence deals only in set-sized interpretations, we have no adequate framework in which to address such questions systematically.

Such questions can be systematically addressed in a framework of higher-order metalogic (Rayo and Williamson 2003). Pickel’s rejection of unrestricted second-order comprehension principles in order to maintain a first-order framework has prevented him from giving an adequate account of logical truth for languages with unrestricted quantification, whose meaningfulness he acknowledges. That is more evidence in favour of an anti-reductionist higher-order approach.
