Review of P. Simons, Parts: A Study in Ontology

Timothy Williamson

Grazer Philosophische Studien 38 (1990): 201–210.

0. Background

Some writers take the relation of limb to body as paradigmatic for that of part to whole, regarding talk of non-spatial parts as shaky metaphor. Others take the notion of a part to be as formal as the notion of a subclass. But on either view there is room for both a formal theory of parts and wholes – mereology – and an informal account of its application. Peter Simons provides both in his authoritative study.

Mereology has lived as the poor relation of set theory. In the earliest systems, developed by Lesniewski and, somewhat later, by Leonard and Goodman, the algebraic structure of the part-whole relation was that of the subset-set relation made ugly by the omission of the null set.1 Complete assimilation was impossible, given that ordinary individuals are not sets; indeed, the motivation for mereology was often the nominalist hope of substituting it for set theory, in order to avoid the latter’s commitment to abstract objects. But this seemed to require mereology to mirror characteristic features of set theory. Sets are extensional; they are identical if and only if they have the same members. Similarly, mereology took wholes to be identical if and only if they have the same parts. Both principles were applied even to identity over time or across different possibilities, if only by default; change or contingency were not contemplated in either the members of a set or the parts of a whole. Sets are also promiscuous; any random individuals are collected by some set as its members. Similarly, mereology summed any random individuals into a whole. Simons uses the phrase ‘extensional mereology’ broadly to describe approaches of this kind. His opening chapters are a valuable guide through the maze of systems into which they have proliferated.

Extensional mereology had a place within a certain metaphysical picture. To simplify, there was a primal medium, sometimes the four-dimensional space-time continuum, sometimes the private world of phenomenal experience. Individual objects were carved out of this medium. Since the medium could be treated as though it were composed of points, each object corresponded to a unique non-empty set of points and each non-empty set of points corresponded to a unique object. On this conception, mereological relations between objects are isomorphic to set-theoretic relations between non-empty sets of points in the medium. The picture was always at odds with the everyday ontology of three-dimensional continuants having spatial but not temporal parts, on which arbitrary mereological sums are not permitted. Still, it could represent itself as the scientific world-view. It had a perspicuous simplicity; the absence of a distinction between essential and accidental properties made reputable truth-functional logic sufficient for its needs; the four-dimensional view gained prestige from its association with Einstein.

Things have changed. A revival of Aristotelian metaphysics and a better understanding of the semantics of modal logic have combined to make a distinction between essential and accidental properties seem both necessary and possible. The restriction to truth-functional logic feels correspondingly oppressive. The supposed inconsistency between the four-dimensional space-time continuum of relativistic physics and the three-dimensional objects of common sense has proved elusive. Perspicuous simplicity is not far from simple-mindedness. This metaphysical turn leaves the natural habitat of extensional mereology behind.2 Yet it does not diminish the need for a systematic theory of part and whole. The chief aim of Simons’s book is to develop a mereology in tune with, and contributory to, neo-Aristotelian metaphysics. Such a theory will distinguish between essential and accidental parts, making the appropriate background logic a modal one; it will assign ordinary things spatial parts at times but not spatio-temporal parts; it will not permit arbitrary mereological sums.

1. Occurrents, Classes, Masses

Although Simons criticizes extensional mereology as inadequate for continuants, he allows that some form of it may apply to certain other categories. He considers the cases of occurrents, classes and masses, arguing in Aristotelian tenor that the terms ‘part’ and ‘whole’ have a variety of analogous senses, related but distinct.

(a) Occurrents. These include events, processes, activities and performances. Unlike continuants, they have temporal as well as spatial parts. Since the metaphysical picture behind extensional mereology assimilates continuants to occurrents, it might be expected to fit the latter reasonably well.3

Simons associates the possession of temporal parts with an incapacity for change. We may say that a sea battle moved from the Channel to the Atlantic, but that is because one phase (temporal part) was in the Channel and a later one in the Atlantic; unlike a continuant, it is not the whole battle that had first one property and then a contrary one (p. 135). Simons follows tradition in using italicization of ‘whole’ to make the point, but ordinary language can be slippery: someone might say that the whole battle had moved because all the ships had. This may be to treat the battle as a kind of continuant (metaphysical error or stylistic variation?); in any case, the line between continuants and occurrents is not always easy to draw.

Having developed a mereology for occurrents, Simons uses it to distinguish between activity verbs and performance verbs. ‘Laugh’ is an activity verb; if one is laughing then one has laughed. ‘Dress’ is a performance verbs; if one is dressing then one has not yet dressed. Simons argues that the nominals of activity verbs are phase-dissective; any reasonably long phase of a laughing is itself a laughing. In contrast, the nominals of performance verbs are phase anti-dissective; no phase of a dressing less than the whole is itself a dressing. The relation between continuous and perfective aspects of the verbs is then explained in terms of this contrast.

Simons presents the distinction as an ontological one between two kinds of occurrent, activities and performances (pp. 137-8). That is dubious. By the tests on which he relies, ‘sail from Ireland’ is an activity verb phrase; ‘sail to Byzantium’ is a performance verb phrase. If single words are required, we could introduce the verbs ‘sailfromireland’ and ‘sailtobyzantium’ with corresponding senses. Now if a man has sailed from Ireland to Byzantium, are there two occurrents, his activity of sailing from Ireland and his performance of sailing to Byzantium? Of course, his leaving Ireland was not his arriving at Byzantium, and the property of sailing from Ireland is not the property of sailing to Byzantium, and the fact that he sailed from Ireland is not the fact that he sailed to Byzantium. Nevertheless, there seems to be a perfectly good sense in which just one sailing from Ireland to Byzantium occurred, a voyage which began and ended on specifiable dates. It might be objected that a strong wind could cause his sailing to Byzantium without causing his sailing from Ireland. In the same sense, a famine could cause his sailing from Ireland without causing his sailing slowly from Ireland, where both ‘sail from Ireland’ and ‘sail slowly from Ireland’ are activity verb phrases. Similarly, curiosity could cause his sailing to Byzantium without causing his sailing slowly to Byzantium, where both ‘sail to Byzantium’ and ‘sail slowly to Byzantium’ are performance verb phrases. Thus the objection requires a sense in which his sailing from Ireland was not his sailing slowly from Ireland, and his sailing to Byzantium was not his sailing slowly to Byzantium. But, so taken, the gerunds do not seem to deliver what Simons’s explanation requires, occurrents with genuine mereological structure. In the needed sense, the man’s sailing from Ireland was his sailing slowly from Ireland, and his sailing to Byzantium was his sailing slowly to Byzantium; but in a corresponding sense his sailing from Ireland was his sailing to Byzantium. The logical differences between activity and performance expressions do not prevent them from picking out the same occurrent. This point is quite compatible with the rest of Simons’s discussion, for ‘phase-dissective’ and ‘phase anti-dissective’ are defined for predicates of occurrents, not for occurrents themselves.4

(b) Classes. Simons distinguishes between high-brow and low-brow classes. A high-brow class is one abstract individual, such as the set of Danes; a low-brow class is many concrete individuals, such as the Danes. Low-brow class theory makes no provision for classes of classes; ‘the Danes and the Norwegians’ refers to a class whose members are people, not classes. May one say that the low-brow class of Danes is not the high-brow class? The singular is misleading. The claim is simply that the Danes are not the high-brow class: indeed, they are not any one thing, and no one thing is the Danes. For classes, ‘part of’ may be read as ‘some of’ or ‘a subclass of’: the Danes are some of the Europeans, a subclass of them, and in that sense part of them. Not surprisingly, extensional mereology suffices. But it would be wrong to suppose that parthood or anything else is a two-place relation on low-brow classes; if there is a relation between the Danes and the Europeans, it has several million terms.

(c) Masses. ‘Part of’ should be read as ‘some of’ for mass terms too: in Richard Sharvy’s example, some of my whisky-and-water is the water (although it is not some whisky-and-water). Like Sharvy, Simons resists the assimilation of masses to individuals. Mass terms cannot be analysed as class terms; ‘Most of the world’s gold is under water’ is not equivalent to ‘Most of the world’s portions of gold are under water’. What this argument does not close off is the possibility that ‘the world’s gold’ denotes a single scattered Quinean individual, the terrestrial part of the single individual denoted by ‘gold’. ‘Most of the world’s gold is under water’ might then be compared to ‘Most of Krakatoa is under water’, with ‘most’ meaning ‘the greater part’.

For Simons, masses stand to individuals in the relation of constitution, and thereby play a fundamental role in his neo-Aristotelian metaphysics. Nevertheless, extensional mereology may apply to masses. Indeed, he follows Sharvy in suggesting that they may provide its most plausible, if unintended, interpretation.

2. Identity, Coincidence, Superposition

According to extensional mereology, objects are determined by their parts, for they are identical if they have the same parts. According to Simons, individual continuants are not determined by their parts. The issue is subtler than it looks, for the operative part-whole relation is reflexive. Each object is quite legitimately taken as a limiting case to be part of itself. Thus objects with the same parts are parts of each other: Simons argues that, if continuants, they may nevertheless be distinct. Some clarification is required.

A directed relation RR may be said to yield extensionality just in case no two things have RR from exactly the same things in the domain (∀x∀y(∀z(zRx↔zRy)→x=y)\forall x\,\forall y\,(\forall z\,(zRx \leftrightarrow zRy) \to x = y)); the converse holds for every relation by Leibniz’s Law. Thus the relation of element to set yields extensionality on the domain of pure sets; so does its converse. It is easy to show that if RR is reflexive and anti-symmetric (∀x∀y((xRy&yRx)→x=y)\forall x\,\forall y\,((xRy \mathbin{\&} yRx) \to x = y)) then it yields extensionality. Conversely, if RR is transitive and yields extensionality then RR is anti-symmetric. Thus a reflexive transitive relation yields extensionality if and only if it is anti-symmetric. The part-whole relation is reflexive and transitive, making it closer to the relation of subset to set than that of element to set. Simons deals convincingly with objections to its reflexivity and transitivity. So the part-whole relation yields extensionality if and only if it is anti-symmetric. By contraposition, two objects have exactly the same parts if and only if each is part of the other.

How could two objects each be part of the other? For Simons, the basic notion is that of a proper part (≪\ll; pp. 9-10). A proper part of a whole is in some sense less than the whole. ≪\ll is a transitive and asymmetric relation; it is therefore also irreflexive. From ≪\ll, a reflexive notion of part is to be defined. The simplest definition is:

x<y:x≪v∨x=yx < y: x \ll v \vee x = y

The reflexivity, anti-symmetry and transitivity of << follows from its definition and the asymmetry and transitivity of ≪\ll. Thus << uncontroversially yields extensionality. No two objects have << to exactly the same objects (p. 11). However, << is not the only reflexive notion of part definable from ≪\ll. Another is:

x≤y:(∃zz≪x→∀z(z≪x→z≪y))&(∼∃zz≪x→(x≪y∨x=y))\begin{aligned}x \leq y:\quad &(\exists z\, z \ll x \to \forall z\,(z \ll x \to z \ll y)) \mathbin{\&}\\ &(\mathord{\sim}\exists z\, z \ll x \to (x \ll y \vee x = y))\end{aligned}

The reflexivity and transitivity of ≤\leq follows from its definition and the transitivity of ≪\ll, but ≤\leq cannot be shown to be anti-symmetric without the use of special assumptions. Thus it is ≤\leq which does not yield extensionality, according to Simons. For him, it is a weaker relation than <<; x<yx < y entails x≤yx \leq y but not conversely (pp. 112, 248).5

It can be shown that where distinct objects have << to each other, the second conjunct of the definition of ≤\leq drops out as irrelevant. That is, both objects have proper parts, and every proper part of one is a proper part of the other. Simons argues that two continuants can stand in this relation.

Some more terminology will help. Objects coincide if they have exactly the same parts (in the sense of ≤\leq), and are superposed if they occupy exactly the same place. Both notions are relative to a time.6 Given that the location of an object is determined by the locations of its parts, coincidence entails superposition. Thus Simons argues for coincidence and superposition without identity. He appeals to three cases:

(A) The cat Tibbles consists of her tail, Tail, and the rest of her body, Tib. Tibbles is nevertheless distinct from the sum Tib + Tail, for she but not it is capable of Tail-less existence. According to Simons, Tibbles and Tib + Tail coincide and are superposed when Tail is in place.7

(B) The ship of Theseus is continually repaired, until none of the original material is left. However, someone collects the original material and makes it into a ship of exactly the original form. Which of the two ships is the original? Simons divides the original ship into two, a form-constant ship under continual repair and a matter-constant ship that is taken to pieces and reassembled. According to Simons, the two ships coincide and are superposed when first built.8

(C) I am not my body, since the latter will be in a coffin when I am not. According to Simons, I and my body coincide and are superposed when I am alive.9

It may be granted, at least for the sake of argument, that each case involves superposition without identity. For there is reason to doubt that any involves coincidence. In each case, one continuant seems to have a proper part the other lacks.

(A) One proper part of Tibbles is her nervous system. Is it a proper part of Tib + Tail? It is natural to suppose that any part of Tib + Tail consists of the sum of that part of it (if any) in Tib and that part of it (if any) in Tail. Thus Tibbles’s nervous system is part of Tib + Tail only if it is the nervous system in Tib + the nervous system in Tail. But these are distinct for the same sort of reason that Tibbles and Tib + Tail are distinct; the former but not the latter is capable of nervous-system-in-Tail-less existence. Thus Tibbles’s nervous system is not part of Tib + Tail. By transitivity, Tibbles too is not part of Tib + Tail.

(B) The form-constant ship has a form-constant hull and the matter-constant ship has a matter-constant hull. The hulls are distinct for the same reason as the ships: they will later be in different places. Since neither ship ever has more than one hull, the form-constant hull is a proper part of the form-constant ship but not of the matter-constant one and the matter-constant hull is a proper part of the matter-constant ship but not of the form-constant one. By transitivity, neither the form-constant ship nor the matter-constant ship is part of the other.

(C) My mind is not my brain, for the same reason that I am not my body: the latter will be in a coffin when the former is not. This no more makes my mind a Cartesian spirit than it does me. I exist in virtue of the functioning of my body; my mind exists in virtue of the functioning of my brain. My mind is a proper part of me, but not of my body. By transitivity, I too am not part of my body.

None of the above is a knockdown objection, but each has some plausibility. No answer to any of them is discernible in Simons’s discussion. Thus he has not refuted the principle that coincident continuants are identical. The examples may instead refute the principle that superposed continuants coincide. Simons himself rejects this principle, for independent and admittedly inconclusive reasons (pp. 248-9). As a substitute for it, he suggests that superposed continuants weakly coincide, in the sense that they overlap the same individuals (p. 250). The objections to (A)-(C) seem to leave this weaker principle standing.

3. Substance and Accident

Simons uses modal mereology to define an impressive variety of concepts and principles for metaphysical theorizing. They cover amongst much else normal and essential parts, different kinds of dependence and integrity, and the distinction between substance and accident. Only the latter will be discussed here.

A substance is anything of which something is an accident but which is not itself an accident of anything. Simons defines xx to be an accident of yy if and only if (i) it is necessarily always necessary that if xx exists then yy exists without being part (≤\leq) of xx and (ii) yy does not necessarily sometimes exist (p. 306).

Are persons substances? Simons wants his definitions to have them so, but considers the objection that they make persons accidents of the Universe, and therefore not substances. He replies that ‘the Universe’ either names a necessary existent or describes different things in different possible situations. The former case is ruled out by clause (ii) of the definition of accident; the latter leaves it open that, in some possible situation in which this person would exist, this Universe would not exist, contrary to clause (i) (p. 309). However, the example can be varied. ‘Space’ may rigidly designate a container capable of existing with different contents. It is necessarily always necessary that if I exist then space (this space) exists, certainly without being part of me. Moreover, space does not necessarily sometimes exist. There could have been nothing at all, and there could have been a space of a different number of dimensions, in which case this space would not have existed. Thus the definitions make me an accident of space, and therefore not a substance. Again, ‘the human race’ may rigidly designate our species, homo sapiens (not to be confused with the class or sum of its actually existing members). It is necessarily always necessary that if I exist then the human race exists, certainly without being part of me. Moreover, the human race does not necessarily sometimes exist. It might never have evolved at all. Thus the definitions make me an accident of the human race, and again not a substance. Yet the cited considerations just do not seem enough to make me an accident of space or the human race, let alone both.

The definition of ‘accident’ is defective. Can it be repaired? It is not obvious how. Even if an extra clause could be found to rule out the examples above, it risks being ad hoc. Indeed, clause (ii) is already ad hoc; the intention is to prevent ordinary substances from being counted as accidents of all necessary existents, but the effect is to prevent necessary existents from having any accidents at all. Gods would have no accidents, and would therefore not be substances. The relation of substance to accident may be simply too close to be captured by considerations of the sort used in Simons’s definition.

4. Conclusion

By the end of the book, Simons has reduced mereological theory to a core of four principles about ≪\ll, the relation of a proper part to a whole. Three say that ≪\ll is an asymmetric transitive relation holding only between existents. Simons calls the fourth the Weak Supplementation Principle: if xx is a proper part of yy then yy has a proper part zz disjoint from xx (i.e. xx and zz have no proper part in common, they are distinct, and neither is a proper part of the other; p. 362). Objects of special kinds may satisfy stronger principles, but only these four are formally or topic-neutrally valid.

Simons expresses the hope that his account will enable parthood to take a central place in ontology beside identity and existence (p. 363). That seems too much to ask. It is arguable that identity and existence are uniquely characterized by the logical principles which govern them.10 Thus any relation identity* governed by Leibniz’s Law and the principle of reflexivity is logically equivalent to identity. Nothing similar is true of parthood. For example, let pp be any permutation of existents, and let xx have ≪*\mathrel{\ll\text{*}} to yy just in case p(x)p(x) has ≪\ll to p(y)p(y). ≪*\mathrel{\ll\text{*}} will satisfy exactly the same logical principles as ≪\ll; in particular, it should satisfy Simons’s core mereological theory. Nevertheless, ≪*\mathrel{\ll\text{*}} is not equivalent to ≪\ll; the moon may have ≪*\mathrel{\ll\text{*}} to my nose. Parthood is not a logical notion in the way identity and existence are, for its logic does not determine its content in the way theirs do. This can hardly be irrelevant to their role in ontology. However, parthood does have more of a logic than, say, the notion of gold has. In this respect one might compare parthood to similarity, causal necessity and temporal order. If it matters to ontology as much as they do, that is importance enough.

For the sake of interest, this article has concentrated on points of disagreement. These points are isolated ones. Simons’s discussion is almost always marked by fair-mindedness, formal rigour and subtle common sense. Parts could easily be the standard book on mereology for the next twenty or thirty years. It puts previous work in manageable order, purges it of unwanted assumptions, extends it, and points the way to further extensions. Metaphysics may be in danger of becoming a sober science.
