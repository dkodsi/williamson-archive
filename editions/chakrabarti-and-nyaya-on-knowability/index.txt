# Chakrabarti and Nyāya on Knowability

Timothy Williamson

In Matthew MacKenzie, Amy Donahue, and Anand Vaidya (eds.), Thinking without Borders: Essays in Honor of Arindam Chakrabarti (2025), 19–32. London: Bloomsbury Academic.

In Realisms Interlinked, Arindam Chakrabarti engagingly and resourcefully demonstrates through many connected examples the scope for fruitful dialogue in epistemology, metaphysics, philosophy of language, and philosophy of mind between the classical Indian tradition and contemporary analytic philosophy.1 Contrary to the old stereotype of incommensurable cultural differences, this reader came away with a strong impression of a shared philosophical project, using our common human cognitive faculties to make very general sense of reality and our place in it, grappling with similar difficulties in ways not at all alien to each other. This discussion note aims to contribute another round to one such dialogue, from the perspective of contemporary analytic philosophy and modern logic.

# 1. Knowability, Nameability, and Realism

Chapter Five of Realisms Interlinked is mischievously entitled “Truth, Recognition of Truth, and Thoughtless Realism,” but written in a thoughtful spirit deeply sympathetic to realism. Much of it concerns the implications of a Nyāya slogan sometimes translated as “Whatever exists is knowable and nameable.” How far does it compromise realism?

One might wonder why anyone would treat the slogan as a threat to realism. After all, as Chakrabarti points out, it does not say that existing consists in or depends on being knowable or nameable, still less that it consists in or depends on being actually known or named. It is quite consistent with the realist view that what exists does so independently of mind and language. After all, realism does not entail pessimism about knowledge or reference. Why should an optimistic realist not suppose that we can in principle know or name whatever exists?

Troubles start when we ask optimistic realists for evidence that the cognitive capacities of one short-lived species on one minor planet are as all-encompassing as they suppose. In particular, if they adopt the strategy of challenging the questioner to give an example of something unknowable or unnameable—which would involve knowing and naming that very thing—and treat the self-defeating nature of the task as vindicating the original slogan, they give the game away. For the task of giving an example of something that one will never know or name is equally self-defeating, so an analogous argument would pretend to vindicate the overweening claim to eventually know and name everything oneself. That claim is utterly implausible without the support of a solipsistic metaphysics, and such a metaphysics is equally implausible. In any case, it is not the metaphysics the realist wants.

To go further, we need to get clearer about what the slogan means. Chakrabarti considers a variety of interpretations, some of which avoid assigning implausible epistemic or semantic privileges to the human species. For instance, he reports, “Indian Logicians interpreted ‘Everything is knowable’ as ‘Everything is actually known by God’ ” (2020, 60). If the knowability and nameability conditions are already satisfied by the knowledge and reference of an omniscient God, then the slogan requires nothing of human knowledge and human reference. Alternatively, if knowing something about a plurality counts as knowing each of its members, and naming the plurality counts as naming each of its members, then what the slogan requires of us humans is trivial, even without divine assistance. I can name the unnamed things “the Unnamed,” and thereby automatically count as naming each of them. In a more modern vein, when I generalize about the Unnamed, each of them is the value of my variable on some assignment of values to variables. Likewise, in knowing that many things belong to the Unknown, I thereby automatically count as knowing each of them.

Needless to say, I am in no position to interpret ancient Indian philosophers autonomously. Even so, I cannot help feeling that interpretations of the sort just considered will have difficulty explaining why the slogan was so famous and often invoked by evidently skilled philosophers (Chakrabarti 2020, 52; Perrett 1999, 401). If the point was that God knows everything and names everything, why not say so directly? And why water down “known” and “named” to “knowable” and “nameable”? Alternatively, if the point was as trivial as the plural reading makes it, why bother to invoke the slogan?

On Roy Perrett’s interpretation, the Nyāya slogan has consequences for human cognitive capacities too (1999, 402). Consequently, in what follows, I will discuss less disappointing interpretations such as his.

# 2. The Modal Church–Fitch Argument

On one salient reading, the claim of universal knowability is that whatever is so can be known to be so. In order to make the claim informative about creatures like us, we may understand “known” as “known by some human” (or “known by some finite creature”), corresponding to the operator “some human knows that ….’ The modalized form “can be known” is the result of prefixing a possibility operator to “known.” That yields the following schema:

$\mathrm{KNOWABLE}\qquad A \supset \lozenge KA$

Here “A” is to be replaced by declarative sentences, ⊃ is material implication, $\lozenge$ is a possibility operator, and K abbreviates “some human knows that.” Hence we can informally paraphrase KNOWABLE thus: if things are so, it is possible for some human to know that they are so.

The schematic letter “A” is replaceable by any meaningful declarative sentence, on pain of not taking the universality of the slogan “Whatever exists is knowable and nameable” with full seriousness. Even very complicated true sentences with many iterations of operators say how things are. The natural default assumption is that where no restriction on a universal generalization is signaled, none is intended. Positing a tacit restriction on “A” with no specific textual basis would be ad hoc and hermeneutically unwarranted.

Philosophers such as Michael Dummett have endorsed KNOWABLE on anti-realist grounds derived from reflection on meaning (e.g., Dummett 1978, 23–4). For reasons already noted, optimistic realists might also endorse KNOWABLE, but on what grounds is unclear, beyond wishful thinking.

A famous argument devised by Alonzo Church but first published by Frederic Fitch—thanks to Church’s role as an anonymous referee for the Journal of Symbolic Logic—reduces KNOWABLE to absurdity (Fitch 1963; Williamson 2000, 270–301; Salerno 2009). The trick is to substitute the Moore-paradoxical conjunction “A ∧ ¬KA” in the schema, which yields this special case of KNOWABLE:

$[1]\quad (A \wedge \neg KA) \supset \lozenge K(A \wedge \neg KA)$

But K(A ∧ ¬KA) is inconsistent, for it entails both A ∧ ¬KA (given that knowledge is factive) and KA ∧ K¬KA (given that knowledge of a conjunction entails knowledge of its conjuncts). Thus, we have:

$[2]\quad \neg\lozenge K(A \wedge \neg KA)$

By classical propositional logic, [1] and [2] together entail [3], and therefore the schema KNOWN:

$[3]\quad \neg(A \wedge \neg KA)$

$\mathrm{KNOWN}\qquad A \supset KA$

We can informally paraphrase KNOWN thus: if things are so, some human knows that they are so. That is utterly implausible. For example, the number of books in my office on New Year’s Day 2010 was either odd or even, but they were never counted, and now it is too late—there has been too much coming and going since then for any future inquiry to settle the matter. Thus, either the specified number is odd and no human will ever know that the specified number is odd, or the specified number is even and no human will ever know that the specified number is even. Either way, there is a counterexample to KNOWN. Although KNOWN is obviously implausible while KNOWABLE is not, KNOWABLE unobviously reduces to KNOWN, and so is itself unobviously implausible.

Perrett (1999) has applied the Church–Fitch argument to the Nyāya slogan. On his first pass, he formalizes its knowability half as KNOWABILITY, so that it entails the disastrous consequence KNOWN. However, he worries that “the proof uses the modal notion of logical possibility, even though Indian logic recognizes no such notion of modality” (1999, 406). Chakrabarti seems to endorse the doubt: “Perrett acknowledges that it is unfair to graft modal logic onto Nyāya because there simply is not any room for possible worlds in Nyāya thinking” (2020, 60).

Confusingly, Perrett’s doubt concerns logical possibility, while Chakrabarti’s concerns possible worlds: these two points raise quite different issues. One need not understand logical possibility in terms of possible worlds; for example, one can treat logical possibility as syntactic consistency. Conversely, one need not understand possible worlds in terms of logical possibility; for example, one can treat worlds as metaphysically or physically possible rather than just logically possible. Moreover, the Church–Fitch argument depends on neither logical possibility nor possible worlds. One can simply propound it as above, reading $\lozenge$ in terms of a pre-theoretically understood distinction, perhaps implicitly metaphysical or physical, between possibility and impossibility. Most humans understand such a distinction without having heard of modal logic or possible worlds semantics. A pre-theoretic understanding of “possible” and “impossible” is quite adequate for the Church–Fitch argument.

It may be objected that metaphysical and physical possibility are still ultimately explained in terms of possible worlds, so Chakrabarti’s criticism stands. But we must distinguish different levels of explanation. Probably, all natural languages contain modal expressions such as “can” and “must,” and the suffix “-able.” In particular, the translation of the Nyāya slogan as “Whatever exists is knowable and nameable” rather than “Whatever exists is known and named” already attributes modal distinctions to Sanskrit. The best available semantic treatment of modality in natural language relies on the framework of possible world semantics (Kratzer 1977, 2012; Portner 2009). If such accounts are on the right lines, modal expressions in Sanskrit are to be explained in terms of possible worlds, irrespective of the conscious theoretical commitments of Nyāya philosophers, just as “can” and “must” are to be explained in terms of possible worlds, even when used by English-speaking philosophers who rant against possible world semantics. On the other hand, if the possible worlds framework is radically misconceived, then the pre-theoretic distinction between “possible” and “impossible” does not depend on possible worlds, so the original objection lapses anyway.

In short, dismissing the properly modal reading of the Nyāya slogan as somehow anachronistic is much too quick. On that reading, it is vulnerable to the Church–Fitch argument.2

# 3. The Temporal Church–Fitch Argument

Although Perrett’s reason for dismissing the properly modal interpretation of the slogan is inadequate, we can also consider his alternative “Diodorean” interpretation (following Diodorus Cronus, who defined the possible as what either is or will be). It simply involves rereading $\lozenge$ in KNOWABLE in temporal rather than modal terms, as “at some time.” Hence, we can informally paraphrase the temporal version of KNOWABLE thus: if things are so, at some time some human knows that they are so. This makes a much stronger claim than the properly modal version of KNOWABLE. It is already quite close to KNOWN, whose reading remains unchanged because it does not contain $\lozenge$. Indeed, the original pair of alternative counterexamples to KNOWN already constitutes a pair of alternative counterexamples to the temporal version of KNOWABLE: either the specified number is odd and no human will ever know that the specified number is odd, or the specified number is even and no human will ever know that the specified number is even. Thus, we hardly need the Church–Fitch argument to refute the temporal version of KNOWABLE. But the Church–Fitch argument still applies to the temporal version. Just as the modal version constructs from any actually unknown truth a necessarily unknown truth, so the temporal version constructs from any presently unknown truth an eternally unknown truth.

According to Chakrabarti, Perrett’s temporal version of the Church–Fitch argument is fallacious. However, his objection to it depends on misreading the conjunction A ∧ ¬KA as though it were invisibly prefixed by “at some time” (2020, 61). It is not, any more than it is invisibly prefixed by $\lozenge$ in the modal version of the argument. It is simply left bare, as a nontrivial potential input to a temporal operator, just as in the original argument it is left bare as a nontrivial potential input to a modal operator. This exactly fits Arthur Prior’s conception of temporal (tense) logic as analogous to modal logic, with truth normally changing rather than timeless, just as truth is normally contingent rather than necessary (Prior 1957). Prior roots his conception deep in an older Western tradition, scholastic and ancient. By contrast, Chakrabarti’s apparent assumption that we must add a fixed time reference to get a fully defined supposition is hard to justify except on the assumption that truth is timeless.

It is surprising that Chakrabarti should assume that truth is timeless given that he has just explained that, for the Nyāya, truth is not timeless. He adds: “This close connection between truth and time is difficult to understand from a classical Western realist standpoint where truth is often timeless” (2020, 58). Prior defends a classical Western standpoint on which truth is not timeless; in that respect, and perhaps in others, he is closer to Nyāya. By contrast, Chakrabarti’s objection to Perrett’s temporal version of the Church–Fitch argument seems to require the assumption, alien to Nyāya, that truth is timeless.3

Of course, Prior’s analogy between tense and modality may turn out to be mistaken (King 2003). But it is hard to see how that would help Naiyāyikas.

# 4. Cognitions and Propositions

Chakrabarti raises a further difficulty for interpretations of the knowability claim on the lines of KNOWABLE. They seem to quantify over all truths, in other words all true propositions, but Nyāya had no category of propositions in their theoretical apparatus. Instead, they discussed cognitions or awarenesses, cognitive episodes occurring in time (Chakrabarti 2020, 61). The worry is that the type of generality implicit in KNOWABLE is anachronistic.4

The schema KNOWABLE does not explicitly involve the category of propositions. It is more like a pattern, whose instances are suitable sentences. One can reason according to such patterns without employing a category of propositions in one’s theoretical apparatus. Chakrabarti makes the generality explicit by using what are often called “propositional quantifiers,” as in $\forall p\,(p \supset \lozenge Kp)$ (to use present notation). He describes such formulations as using “universal quantification over propositions” (2020, 61). But that characterization of ∀ p is misleading. To see this, observe that universal quantification over propositions is formally analogous to universal quantification over penguins. For example, “Some propositions entail others” is grammatically analogous to “Some penguins feed others.” They can be formalized by the same formula, differently interpreted: $\exists x\,(Px \wedge \exists y\,(Py \wedge x \ne y \wedge Fxy)$. The bound variables “x” and “y” occupy name position; in one case the variables are restricted to propositions, in the other to penguins. By contrast, in $\forall p\,(p \supset \lozenge Kp)$ the bound variable “p” occupies sentence position, since it forms the antecedent of the conditional operator ⊃. That is where Chakrabarti himself places the relevant variable. Thus, what is at issue is not first-order quantification over propositions but second-order quantification into sentence position. To assume that such second-order quantification must in turn be analysed in terms of first-order quantification over propositions in a meta-language is in effect to help oneself to the dubious Quinean theory that first-order quantification is basic (see Williamson 2013, 235–61, for more discussion). Thus, it is far from clear that the kind of generality in $\forall p\,(p \supset \lozenge Kp)$ requires commitment to an ontology of propositions.

Of course, none of this shows that the Nyāya slogan should be understood in terms of higher-order logic; that sounds equally anachronistic. But interpretations of it along the lines of a schema such as KNOWABLE cannot be refuted just by taking for granted that the generality implicit in the schema involves theoretical commitment to a category of propositions.

The mechanism of generality is not the only way in which a principle might carry implicit commitment to an ontology of propositions. The commitment might come from the underlying semantics of the sentences which instantiate the principle, just as the semantics of modal expressions might carry implicit commitment to an ontology of possible worlds. On the simplest way for this to happen, the semantic content of a sentence in a context would just be a proposition. Without a proposition, the sentence would crash semantically. Such a basic feature of the semantics will presumably be universal to natural languages; in particular, there is no reason to expect a difference between Sanskrit and English in that respect. Again, this has little to do with the explicit theoretical commitments of philosophers writing in a natural language. They may avoid all explicit commitment to propositions, and yet be implicitly committed to them every time they utter a sentence of English or Sanskrit, as a precondition for having said anything. What matters there is what the semantics of their language really is, not what they take it to be. Equally, if it is false that the semantic content of a sentence in a context is a proposition, speakers are not implicitly committed to propositions by that means, although they may be explicitly committed to them by endorsing such a semantic theory as theoreticians.

A more specific feature of KNOWABLE is that it involves potential ascriptions of propositional knowledge, since the K operator in it is read “some human knows that” (emphasis added). Once again, explicit theoretical commitments must be distinguished from implicit commitments incurred from the actual semantics of the natural language one is speaking. But there is also a difference between a general implicit commitment incurred just by using sentences of a natural language at all and a special implicit commitment incurred by using specific words or phrases of the natural language, such as the “knows that” construction. In the latter case, a theoretician who frowns on the implicit commitment can in principle avoid it by refusing to use the specific words or phrases at issue. However, Chakrabarti does not suggest that Naiyāyikas went to the length of avoiding propositional knowledge constructions, at least when on duty as philosophers. Quite the opposite: he characterizes them as defending propositional proof and knowledge, for example in these passages:

In ancient India, the Nyāya Sūtras tried to construct systematic philosophy with the three avowed steps of enumerating, defining, and examining the definitions and defending the definienda against actual and imagined counterattacks. Unless you can define what perception or inference or knowledge or substance or a universal is, how can you go on to prove that such things exist, and if you cannot prove that such things exist, how can you claim that they exist at all? (Chakrabarti 2020, 51, his italics, my underlining)

It is clear that the Nyāya Vaiśeşika meant the following: so long as the human liberation-seeker knows what makes something a substance, a quality, a cause, an action, a universal etc., and thereby attains self-knowledge with the proper moral preparation, he can hope to be liberated. (Chakrabarti 2020, 60)

In the second passage, knowing what makes something a substance (…) is clearly a matter of knowing that such-and-such makes something a substance. In any case, Naiyāyikas were happy to express the content of cognitions in sentences, which provide the material for that-clauses (Chakrabarti 2020, 58; Perrett 1999, 403).

None of this shows that the Nyāya did intend their knowability claim along the lines of KNOWABLE, on either its modal or its temporal reading. In particular, it does not exclude an interpretation in terms of something more like knowledge by acquaintance, as Chakrabarti discusses. The point is just this: for all that has been seen, the expressive resources needed to articulate something tantamount to a schema such as KNOWABLE were available to the Nyāya, even if they did not explicitly conceive it as a schema.

# 5. Generalizing over Properties

Chakrabarti discusses Nyāya theories of properties (2020, 57–8). The Nyāya divide properties into what he calls “real universals” and “titular properties,” in a way reminiscent of David Lewis’s distinction between natural and unnatural properties (Lewis 1983).5 One can use quantification over properties to approximate the effect of quantification over propositions.

Recall Chakrabarti’s explicit universal generalization of KNOWABLE, with quantification into sentence position:

$\mathrm{KNOWABLE}_{p}\qquad \forall p\,(p \supset \lozenge Kp)$

For purposes of comparison, we continue this higher-order approach. Let “x” be a variable that takes name position, and “f” a variable that takes (monadic) predicate position. As usual, concatenation indicates predication. Thus, in place of KNOWABLE_{p}, we can try this:

$\mathrm{KNOWABLE}_{f,x}\qquad \forall f\,\forall x\,(fx \supset \lozenge Kfx)$

A crude paraphrase of KNOWABLE_{f,x} is this: for any property and anything which has that property, it is possible for some human to know that the thing has the property. The properties here include both real universals and titular properties, because no property, natural or unnatural, is barred from being expressed by a predicate.

The paraphrase is crude because it treats second-order quantification into predicate position as if it were first-order quantification into name position restricted to objects of a special sort, namely properties. That in turn requires a special relation of “having” between things and properties, with the consequent danger of an infinite regress. As a second-order formula, KNOWABLE_{f,x} avoids those problems, but it is hard to translate faithfully into natural language. Presumably, the English paraphrase is closer than KNOWABLE_{f,x} itself to what was available to the Nyāya in Sanskrit, so its unfaithfulness to the second-order formula does not matter too much for present purposes. In what follows, we can ignore such subtleties.

How far does KNOWABLE_{f,x} fall short of KNOWABLE_{p}? The obvious concern is that not every proposition is to the effect that a given object has a given property: for example, pure universal and existential generalizations seem to lack that form. However, given a second-order variable “p” taking sentence position and a first-order variable “x” taking name position, we can form the conjunction x=x ∧ p, with the same truth-value as “p.” We can then abstract the complex predicate $\mathrm{\lambda}y(y = y \wedge p)$, which we can read as “is self-identical while p”; the predicate expresses a mere titular property, but predicates are allowed to do that.6 Applying that predicate to the variable “x” gives the open sentence $\mathrm{\lambda}y(y = y \wedge p)x$, which should be equivalent to the original conjunction. By substituting the complex predicate for the predicate variable “f” in KNOWABLE_{f,x}, we obtain this special case of KNOWABLE_{f,x}:

$\mathrm{KNOWABLE}_{p,x}\qquad \forall p\,\forall x\,(\mathrm{\lambda} y\,(y = y \wedge p)x \supset \lozenge K\mathrm{\lambda} y\,(y = y \wedge p)x)$

Crudely paraphrased, this principle says that for any proposition and anything, if the thing has the property of being self-identical while the proposition holds, then it is possible for some human to know that the thing has the property of being self-identical while the proposition holds. But then, on very plausible assumptions, KNOWABLE_{f,x} is equivalent to the original universally quantified principle KNOWABLE_{p}. For if a proposition holds, then something has the property of being self-identical while the proposition holds, so by KNOWABLE_{p,x} (which KNOWABLE_{f,x} entails) it is possible for some human to know that the thing has the property of being self-identical while the proposition holds, which in turn requires knowing that the proposition holds. More formally, p entails $\mathrm{\lambda}y(y = y \wedge p)x$ (given that the value of “x” is in the domain of first-order quantification) and $K\mathrm{\lambda}y(y = y \wedge p)x$ entails Kp (given that knowledge of a conjunction entails knowledge of the conjuncts). In effect, one can strip away the redundant subject-predicate verbiage from KNOWABLE_{p,x} to leave KNOWABLE_{p}. Conversely, KNOWABLE_{p} entails KNOWABLE_{f,x} because we can substitute the complex subject-predicate open sentence fx for the sentence variable “p” in KNOWABLE_{p}. Thus, on plausible assumptions, the new knowability principle for properties and things, KNOWABLE_{f,x}, is equivalent to the old knowability principle for propositions, KNOWABILITY_{p}.

In one respect, this is very bad news for KNOWABILITY_{f,x}. For the Church–Fitch argument refutes KNOWABILITY_{p}; since KNOWABILITY_{f,x} is equivalent to KNOWABILITY_{p}, the Church–Fitch argument refutes KNOWABILITY_{f,x} too. Of course, one can evade the equivalence by the simple expedient of banning sentence variables from the language, so that KNOWABILITY_{p} cannot even be formulated. But that achieves little, for one can simulate the Church–Fitch argument directly in the language of KNOWABILITY_{f,x}, with the kind of reasoning just explained. The details are slightly messy, but the guiding idea is clear. This is another way to see that keeping propositions out of their theoretical apparatus will not by itself save Naiyāyikas from the Church–Fitch argument.

# 6. Concluding Reflections

As already emphasized, the considerations in this essay by no means show that the knowability half of the Nyāya slogan should be interpreted as something like KNOWABILITY, KNOWABILITY_{p}, or KNOWABILITY_{f,x}. Their aim is only to clear away some apparent obstacles to such interpretations. But is the Church–Fitch argument itself an obstacle to those interpretations? It might be thought so, on the grounds that it makes them uncharitable. Could the Nyāya slogan really have meant something that can be so briefly and decisively refuted?

In response, it is helpful to look back at the reception of the Church–Fitch argument in recent Western philosophy. It came as a bolt from the blue. Arguably, it refuted signature claims by various highly distinguished, brilliantly ingenious, logically-minded philosophers, including Charles Sanders Peirce and Michael Dummett (see Perrett 1999, 404 for apt quotations). Of course, one can debate whether it really hit the bull’s eye. To switch metaphors, friends of knowability scrutinize the small print in search of loopholes. But what is clear is that Peirce, Dummett, and the rest had not seen it coming. For if they had, they would have sought to preempt the objection, by explaining why they were not vulnerable to it. They would have strengthened and displayed their defenses, not passively hoped that no one else would notice the gap. The problem simply never occurred to them—in Dummett’s case, it was brought to his attention only long after he had first published his anti-realist claims (see Dummett 2001, which significantly restricts his anti-realism). The proper moral is not that Peirce and Dummett were not as smart as they were thought to be. Rather, the moral is that philosophy is even harder than it seems. Often, once you have seen a point, it seems obvious, but that makes it almost unimaginable how hard it was to see for the first time.

A more specific mechanism is also at work. Mathematical logic has greatly facilitated the identification, computation, and verification of relevant examples and counterexamples, by massive increases not only in the scope of formal deductive reasoning but also in the expressive power of formal languages with fully explicit formation rules, which help one survey the full range of substitution instances of a given principle. Unsurprisingly, the Church–Fitch argument was devised by a leading figure in the rise of mathematical logic. Modern technology has facilitated many tasks: finding philosophical counterexamples is no exception to the trend. In that respect, philosophy, though harder than it seems, is easier than it was.

In this light, it is simply anachronistic to treat the Church–Fitch argument as a serious objection to interpreting the knowability half of the Nyāya slogan on the lines of KNOWABILITY, KNOWABILITY_{p}, or KNOWABILITY_{f,x}. It would have been no easier for the Nyāya to spot the problem than it was for Peirce, Dummett, and the rest. Presumably, if the Nyāya had spotted the possibility of such an argument against a knowability principle that sounded like their own slogan, they would have taken care to show why their position was not vulnerable to such an attack, which they did not do. There may be serious objections to interpreting the slogan that way; if so, the Church–Fitch argument is not one of them.

# Notes

[1] By “analytic philosophy” I mean the diverse philosophical tradition stemming from such contrasted figures as Frege, Russell, Moore, Wittgenstein, and Carnap, not something defined by adherence to specific doctrines, methods, or aims. Chakrabarti describes the two sides of the comparison as “contemporary Anglo-American” and “classical Indian” (2020, x), but analytic philosophy has never been exclusively Anglo-American, as that list of dramatis personae shows; indeed, the philosophers most often cited in the book are Frege and Kant. One of the most welcome developments in philosophy over recent decades has been the growth of analytic philosophy in regions from which it had been largely absent, notably in Asia.

[2] For a discussion of modality in Sanskritic philosophy see Chakrabarti and Nicolay (2023).

[3] Even granted that truth is timeless, existentially quantifying over times is not the only way of fixing the time reference, so more work would still be needed to defeat Perrett’s temporal version of the Church–Fitch argument.

[4] Chakrabarti raises the further concern that speaking of “all true propositions” may generate paradoxes (2020, 61). However, the analogous concern can be raised that speaking of “everything” or “all things” (as in the original slogan) may also generate paradoxes (Williamson 2003). In what follows, I ignore such concerns. Even if some restriction were ultimately needed to avoid paradox, it would still permit enough generality for the issues discussed here to retain their interest.

[5] Chakrabarti explains that real universals in Nyāya Vaiśeṣika are subject to the constraint that any pair of them are related by inclusion or exclusion (either the first includes the second, or the second includes the first, or they exclude each other). If so, real universals form at least a minimally treelike taxonomic structure. On some views of Lewisian natural properties, the analogous constraint may be plausible for them too (see Thomason 1969, for this constraint applied to species, determinates, and natural kinds, and Wiggins 1980, 202–3, for a proposed counterexample). However, Chakrabarti interprets the constraint as making the disjunction of inclusion and exclusion as sufficient as well as necessary for both properties to be real universals: “substancehood and qualityhood are two natural kinds [real universals] because no substance is a quality” and “Substancehood and earthenness are also both natural [real] universals because all earthen things are substances,” but “the extensions of two [real] universals cannot partially overlap or intersect” (2020, 57). However, this interpretation makes the criterion inconsistent. For example, it implies that the properties of being gold and being silver are both real universals, because they exclude each other, and the properties of being a cube and being a sphere are also both real universals, for the same reason. But being gold and being a cube partially overlap: some spheres are gold, some spheres are not gold, and some gold things are not spheres. A more charitable understanding of the criterion makes the disjunction of inclusion and exclusion necessary but not sufficient for a pair of properties to both be real universals. If real universals constitute a treelike taxonomy, they satisfy this less extreme version of the constraint.

[6] The only purpose of the conjunct y=y is to make the predicate abstraction nonvacuous. Readers who (like the author) are comfortable with vacuous predicate abstraction can simplify the argument by deleting this conjunct.
