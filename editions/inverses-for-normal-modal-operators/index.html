Inverses for Normal Modal Operators

Lloyd Humberstone and Timothy Williamson

Studia Logica 59 (1997): 33–64.

Abstract. Given a 1-ary sentence operator ○\bigcirc, we describe LL – another 1-ary operator – as a left inverse of ○\bigcirc in a given logic if in that logic every formula φ\varphi is provably equivalent to L○φL\bigcirc\varphi. Similarly RR is a right inverse of ○\bigcirc if φ\varphi is always provably equivalent to ○Rφ\bigcirc R\varphi. We investigate the behaviour of left and right inverses for ○\bigcirc taken as the □□ operator of various normal modal logics, paying particular attention to the conditions under which these logics are conservatively extended by the addition of such inverses, as well as to the question of when, in such extensions, the inverses behave as normal modal operators in their own right.

Key words: modal logic, Kripke models, inverses, conservative extension.

1. Introduction

The n-modal language comprises all formulas obtained from a denumerable set of propositional variables (‘sentence letters’) p0,…pk,…p_0, \ldots p_k, \ldots by means of the usual boolean connectives →,↔,∧,∨,¬\to, \leftrightarrow, \wedge, \vee, \neg, and (so that we can have formulas containing no propositional variables) ⊤,⊥\top, \bot, as well as additional primitive singulary connectives (‘modal operators’) ○1,…,○n\bigcirc_1, \ldots, \bigcirc_n. An n-modal logic is a set of formulas in this language containing all truth-functional tautologies and closed under Uniform Substitution (of arbitrary formulas for propositional variables) as well as under Modus Ponens. 1-modal and 2-modal logics are referred to as ‘monomodal’ and ‘bimodal’, respectively. A primitive modal operator ○i\bigcirc_i is congruential in an n-modal logic 𝐒{𝐒} (where 1≤i≤n1 \leq i \leq n) if for all formulas φ,ψ\varphi, \psi, with φ↔ψ∈𝐒\varphi \leftrightarrow \psi \in {𝐒}, we have ○iφ↔○iψ∈𝐒\bigcirc_i\varphi \leftrightarrow \bigcirc_i\psi \in {𝐒} and normal in 𝐒{𝐒} if for all formulas φ1,…,φm,ψ\varphi_1, \ldots, \varphi_m, \psi, if (φ1∧…∧φm)→ψ∈𝐒(\varphi_1 \wedge \ldots \wedge \varphi_m) \to \psi \in {𝐒}, then (○iφ1∧…∧○iφm)→○iψ∈𝐒(\bigcirc_i\varphi_1 \wedge \ldots \wedge \bigcirc_i\varphi_m) \to \bigcirc_i\psi \in {𝐒}. (When m=0m=0, this is to be understood as saying that if ψ∈𝐒\psi \in {𝐒} then ○iψ∈S\bigcirc_i\psi \in S: the ‘rule of necessitation’ for ○i\bigcirc_i.) Note that normality implies congruentiality but not conversely. 𝐒{𝐒} itself is congruential (normal) when each of ○1,…,○n\bigcirc_1, \ldots, \bigcirc_n is congruential (normal) in 𝐒{𝐒}. Where i,j≤ni,j \leq n, ○i\bigcirc_i is a left inverse of ○j\bigcirc_j, and ○j\bigcirc_j is a right inverse of ○i\bigcirc_i, just in case for all formulas φ\varphi (in the language of 𝐒{𝐒}) we have ○i○jφ↔φ∈𝐒\bigcirc_i\bigcirc_j\varphi \leftrightarrow \varphi \in {𝐒}. We sometimes write ‘⊢𝐒φ\vdash_{{𝐒}} \varphi’ in place of ‘φ∈𝐒\varphi \in {𝐒}’, and read this as ‘φ\varphi is provable in 𝐒{𝐒}’.

We shall be concerned with extending normal monomodal logics (a) to bimodal logics in which ○2\bigcirc_2 is a left inverse of ○1\bigcirc_1, and (b) to bimodal logics in which ○2\bigcirc_2 is a right inverse of ○1\bigcirc_1. We write ○1\bigcirc_1 as ‘□□’ throughout, writing ○2\bigcirc_2 as ‘LL’ in case (a) and as ‘RR’ in case (b). The letters here are of course mnemonic for Left and Right, respectively. We abbreviate ¬□¬φ\neg□\neg\varphi to ◇φ◇\varphi, ¬L¬φ\neg L\neg\varphi to MφM\varphi, and ¬R¬φ\neg R\neg\varphi to 𝐒φ{𝐒}\varphi. Note that in the latter pair of cases, we have simply chosen the letters alphabetically following ‘LL’ and ‘RR’ to denote the dual operators. In particular, the resulting use of ‘LL’ and ‘MM’ is to be distinguished from their usage in some quarters as variants on ‘□□’ and ‘◇◇’. While on the subject of notation, we remark that it would not have been a good idea to employ the perhaps obvious notation ‘□−1□^{-1}’ for an inverse of □□, in the first place because of the desirability of distinguishing between left and right inverses, and secondly because that notation already has a usage (in connexion with tense logic) for symbolising an operator interpreted in Kripke models by the converse of the accessibility relation interpreting □□: we avail ourselves of that usage in Section 6 below, in fact. Familiarity is assumed with the Kripke semantics for normal (n-)modal logics, and in particular with the canonical model construction and its role in proving completeness with respect to a class of models. (See [6].) If 𝐒{𝐒} is a normal monomodal logic then 𝐒(𝐋){𝐒}({𝐋}) is the smallest bimodal logic extending 𝐒{𝐒} in which ○2\bigcirc_2 is a congruential left inverse of ○1\bigcirc_1, while 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋} is the smallest bimodal logic extending 𝐒{𝐒} in which ○2\bigcirc_2 is a normal left inverse of ○1\bigcirc_1; in both 𝐒(𝐋){𝐒}({𝐋}) and 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋}, we require ○1\bigcirc_1 to be normal. That is, in terms of the notation introduced above, 𝐒(𝐋){𝐒}({𝐋}) and 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋} both contain all formulas of the form 𝐋□φ↔φ{𝐋}□\varphi \leftrightarrow \varphi and allow the ‘substitutivity of provable equivalents’, while in the latter case we also have closure under the passage from (φ1∧…∧φm)→ψ(\varphi_1 \wedge \ldots \wedge \varphi_m) \to \psi to (𝐋φ1∧…∧𝐋φm)→𝐋ψ({𝐋}\varphi_1 \wedge \ldots \wedge {𝐋}\varphi_m) \to {𝐋}\psi. Mutatis mutandis for the notations 𝐒(𝐑){𝐒}({𝐑}) and 𝐒𝐑{𝐒}{𝐑}; for these cases, the relevant schema is □𝐑φ↔φ□ {𝐑}\varphi \leftrightarrow \varphi. We refer to 𝐋□φ↔φ{𝐋}□\varphi \leftrightarrow \varphi and □𝐑φ↔φ□ {𝐑}\varphi \leftrightarrow \varphi as the left inverse schema and the right inverse schema, respectively.

The talk of left and right inverses here of course derives from the corresponding terminology in the context of semigroups with a (two-sided) identity element (alias monoids). Recall that if an element a of such a semigroup possesses a left inverse l(a) and a right inverse r(a), then these inverses coincide, since, denoting the identity element by e and the semigroup operation by ·, we have, for the given a:

a⋅r(a)=eso l(a)⋅(a⋅r(a))=l(a)⋅eso l(a)⋅(a⋅r(a))=l(a)so (l(a)⋅a)⋅r(a)=l(a)so e⋅r(a)=l(a)so r(a)=l(a).\begin{aligned} a\cdot r(a)&=e\\ \text{so }l(a)\cdot(a\cdot r(a))&=l(a)\cdot e\\ \text{so }l(a)\cdot(a\cdot r(a))&=l(a)\\ \text{so }(l(a)\cdot a)\cdot r(a)&=l(a)\\ \text{so }e\cdot r(a)&=l(a)\\ \text{so }r(a)&=l(a). \end{aligned}

It is for this reason that we do not also consider 3-modal logics in which there are separate left and right inverses for an originally given operator □□. (However, in subsection 6.1 below, we do consider the case in which the given operator has a two-sided inverse: a left-inverse which is also a right-inverse.) The corresponding reasoning in this case takes the following form, with □□ playing the role of the aa above:

(1)
□Rφ↔φ□ R\varphi \leftrightarrow \varphi
(2)
L□Rφ↔LφL□ R\varphi \leftrightarrow L\varphi
(3)
Rφ↔LφR\varphi \leftrightarrow L\varphi

Here we have had to indicate explicitly an arbitrary ‘argument’ φ\varphi for the operators concerned, and the derivation – which should be understood as saying that if (1) belongs to the logic concerned, so does (2), and if (2) does, so does (3) – is shorter because there is no explicit notation for the identity operator, and so no separate steps in which it is deleted, as well as no separate appeal to associativity (passing from, as it were, L(□R)φ↔LφL(□ R)\varphi \leftrightarrow L\varphi to (L□)Rφ↔Lφ(L□)R\varphi \leftrightarrow L\varphi). Note the exploitation of LL’s congruentiality in the transition from (1) to (2). (The connexion between the semigroup reasoning and this modal logical version is of course given by the semigroup of 1-ary operations induced by the operators in the Lindenbaum-Tarski algebra of the envisaged logic; see [2], p. 205, for example. Warning: semigroup theorists themselves tend to mean something weaker when they speak of inverses – see [5], Chapter 5.)

Our interest, in what follows, is in the modal analogue of another part of the received wisdom concerning inverses, to the effect that a function has a left inverse in the semigroup of all mappings from a set into itself (with composition as the semigroup operation) if and only if that function is injective, and likewise with ‘right’ replacing ‘left’ and ‘surjective’ replacing ‘injective’. Concentrating initially on the ‘left inverse’ part of the story, this suggests that we consider the relationship between, on the one hand, the logics 𝐒(𝐋){𝐒}({𝐋}) and 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋}, for a normal monomodal logic 𝐒{𝐒}, and the closure of 𝐒{𝐒} under what we shall call the ‘Cancellation Rule’:

Cancellation

𝐹𝑟𝑜𝑚:□φ↔□ψ𝑡𝑜:φ↔ψ\begin{aligned} \mathit{From}:&\quad□\varphi\leftrightarrow□\psi\\ \mathit{to}:&\quad\varphi\leftrightarrow\psi \end{aligned}

We take the name ‘Cancellation Rule’ from [14], which considers some variants also, as does [15]. (These papers, as well as [16], may be consulted for some of the philosophical interest behind studying left and right inverses. We restrict ourselves to presenting – rather than motivating – such a study here.) For 𝐒{𝐒} to be closed under this rule is for the operation corresponding to □□ in the Lindenbaum-Tarski algebra of 𝐒{𝐒} to be injective (‘one-one’); and clearly, whatever the status of 𝐒{𝐒} in this regard, 𝐒(𝐋){𝐒}({𝐋}) and 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋} are closed under Cancellation, since if □φ↔□ψ□\varphi\leftrightarrow□\psi belongs to either of these logics, for a pair of formulas φ,ψ\varphi,\psi, then we may prefix ‘LL’ to each side and delete the resulting ‘L□L□’ sequence. We look further into the Cancellation Rule in Section 2, returning to the connection with left inverses in Section 3. Section 4 addresses the topic of right inverses. (To complete this prospectus: in Section 5 an issue related to the right inverse theme is raised, and Section 6 collects together sundry additional material.) The topic of right inverses naturally suggests consideration of the case of logics in which the operation corresponding to □□ in the Lindenbaum-Tarski algebra is surjective (‘onto’), which amounts to saying that □□ itself is universally representative in the logic, meaning: every formula φ\varphi is provably equivalent to one of the form □ψ□\psi. This is clearly so for the logics 𝐒(𝐑){𝐒}({𝐑}) and 𝐒𝐑{𝐒}{𝐑}, since for a given φ\varphi we can take ψ\psi as RφR\varphi. In some cases □□ is even universally representative in 𝐒{𝐒} itself (see Section 6.2); in the general case this is not so, even if the extension to 𝐒(𝐑){𝐒}({𝐑}) (or indeed to the stronger 𝐒𝐑{𝐒}{𝐑}) is conservative – and we shall want to know under what conditions the extension is conservative. As is stressed in [1], if a connective with prescribed inferential properties – in our case, those of being a left or a right inverse of □□ – cannot be defined within a logic, there is still a sense in which a proponent of that logic is committed to the intelligibility (to the ‘existence’, as [1] has it) of such a connective provided the logic in question can be extended conservatively to accommodate a new connective with the inferential powers concerned.

To conclude these introductory remarks, notice that while the definitions of left and right inverses for a given operator were given specifically for the primitive modal operators, the notions make precisely analogous sense for arbitrary primitive or defined modal or non-modal operators. Thus, taking up the ‘non-modal’, we observe that negation (¬\neg) is its own left (and right) inverse, and, taking up the ‘or defined’, we observe that MM is a left inverse of ◇◇ in 𝐒(𝐋){𝐒}({𝐋}), and so a fortiori in 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋} (since 𝐒(𝐋)⊆𝐒𝐋{𝐒}({𝐋})\subseteq {𝐒}{𝐋}): for we have, where φ\varphi is arbitrary, L□¬φ↔¬φ∈𝐒(𝐋)L□\neg\varphi\leftrightarrow\neg\varphi\in {𝐒}({𝐋}), and thus ¬L□¬φ↔φ∈𝐒(𝐋)\neg L□\neg\varphi\leftrightarrow\varphi\in {𝐒}({𝐋}), and so (appealing to the congruentiality of LL) ¬L¬¬□¬φ↔φ∈𝐒(𝐋)\neg L\neg\neg□\neg\varphi\leftrightarrow\varphi\in {𝐒}({𝐋}), which is to say M◇φ↔φ∈𝐒(𝐋)M◇\varphi\leftrightarrow\varphi\in {𝐒}({𝐋}). Similarly, in the case of 𝐒(𝐑){𝐒}({𝐑}) we have: □R¬φ↔¬φ∈𝐒(𝐑)□ R\neg\varphi\leftrightarrow\neg\varphi\in {𝐒}({𝐑}), so ¬□R¬φ↔φ∈𝐒(𝐑)\neg□ R\neg\varphi\leftrightarrow\varphi\in {𝐒}({𝐑}), so (this time by the normality – and hence congruentiality – of □□ itself) ¬□¬¬R¬φ↔φ∈𝐒(𝐑)\neg□\neg\neg R\neg\varphi\leftrightarrow\varphi\in {𝐒}({𝐑}), i.e., ◇Sφ↔φ∈𝐒(𝐑)◇ S\varphi\leftrightarrow\varphi\in {𝐒}({𝐑}): thus SS is a right inverse of ◇◇. [16] provides a detailed study of the properties a right inverse of ◇◇, the latter being the dual of a normal □□ for which the 𝐓{𝐓} schema (see Section 3 below) holds, the inverse itself not being assumed to be congruential. (All references to duality are to boolean duality rather than semigroup duality.) In what follows, we attend only to inverses which are at least congruential (as in the systems 𝐒(𝐋){𝐒}({𝐋}) and 𝐒(𝐑){𝐒}({𝐑}), for various 𝐒{𝐒}), if not actually normal (the systems 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋} and 𝐒𝐑{𝐒}{𝐑}).

2. The Cancellation Rule

We begin by noting some rules which are equivalent to the Cancellation Rule in the sense of

Proposition 2.1. A modal logic in which □□ is normal is closed under any of these rules if and only if it is closed under all of them:

(i)
the Cancellation Rule
(ii)
the rule: From ◇φ↔◇ψ◇\varphi\leftrightarrow◇\psi to φ↔ψ\varphi\leftrightarrow\psi
(iii)
the rule: From □φ→□ψ□\varphi\to□\psi to φ→ψ\varphi\to\psi
(iv)
the rule: From □φ→□¬φ□\varphi\to□\neg\varphi to ¬φ\neg\varphi

Proof. For the equivalence of (i), (ii), (iii), see [14], p.304. We address the equivalence of (iii) and (iv). Suppose 𝐒{𝐒} is a modal logic in which □□ is normal, and that 𝐒{𝐒} is closed under the rule mentioned in (iii). Then if □φ→□¬φ∈𝐒□\varphi\to□\neg\varphi\in {𝐒}, we may invoke that rule to conclude that φ→¬φ∈𝐒\varphi\to\neg\varphi\in {𝐒} and hence that ¬φ∈𝐒\neg\varphi\in {𝐒} (since (φ→¬φ)→¬φ(\varphi\to\neg\varphi)\to\neg\varphi is a substitution instance of a truth-functional tautology and 𝐒{𝐒} is closed under Modus Ponens). Thus closure under the rule in (iii) implies closure under the rule in (iv). Conversely, suppose that 𝐒{𝐒} is closed under the latter rule and that we have a premiss for the application of the rule in (iii): □φ→□ψ□\varphi\to□\psi. From the hypothesis that □□ is normal in 𝐒{𝐒} it follows that this formula is then also in 𝐒{𝐒}: □(φ∧¬ψ)→□¬(φ∧¬ψ)□(\varphi\wedge\neg\psi)\to□\neg(\varphi\wedge\neg\psi). Thus by the rule in (iv) we get ¬(φ∧¬ψ)∈𝐒\neg(\varphi\wedge\neg\psi)\in {𝐒}, and so φ→ψ∈𝐒\varphi\to\psi\in {𝐒}, as required. ■

Next, we want a semantic characterization of the Cancellation Rule. We say that a model ⟨W,R,V⟩\langle W,R,V\rangle satisfies the Predecessor Condition when for all y∈Wy\in W there exists x∈Wx\in W such that xRyxRy and for all z∈Wz\in W, xRzxRz implies y=zy=z. This is really a condition on the frame ⟨W,R⟩\langle W,R\rangle of the model, but we shall often prefer model-based rather than frame-based formulations, since we rely heavily on the fact that the canonical model for a normal modal logic 𝐒{𝐒} is a characteristic model for 𝐒{𝐒} – a model verifying all and only those φ∈𝐒\varphi\in {𝐒}, that is – even though the frame of that model may not be a characteristic frame for 𝐒{𝐒} – a frame validating precisely such φ\varphi, that is. (As usual, in saying that a frame validates a formula we mean that every model on that frame verifies the formula.) Now it is not hard to check that a normal monomodal logic 𝐒{𝐒} is closed under the Cancellation Rule if and only if 𝐒{𝐒} is determined by some class of models all of which satisfy the Predecessor Condition. (Note that this is not to say that every class of models which determines 𝐒{𝐒} consists exclusively of models satisfying the condition.) For present purposes, however, it suffices to give a restricted form of this observation:

Proposition 2.2. A normal monomodal logic 𝐒{𝐒} is closed under the Cancellation Rule if and only if the canonical model for 𝐒{𝐒} satisfies the Predecessor Condition.

Proof. ‘If’: We take the implicational version of the Cancellation Rule given in (iii) of Prop. 2.1, showing that if the canonical model ⟨W,R,V⟩\langle W,R,V\rangle for 𝐒{𝐒} satisfies the Predecessor Condition, then 𝐒{𝐒} is closed under this rule. Suppose that the conclusion φ→ψ\varphi\to\psi of an application of the rule is not provable in 𝐒{𝐒}. Then at some point y∈Wy\in W, φ→ψ\varphi\to\psi is false, so φ\varphi is true at yy while ψ\psi is false. Since the model satisfies the Predecessor Condition, there is some x∈Wx\in W which bears the relation RR to yy, making □ψ□\psi false at xx, and only to yy, making □φ□\varphi true at xx. Since □φ→□ψ□\varphi\to□\psi is therefore false at xx, this implication is not provable in 𝐒{𝐒}.

‘Only if’: We take the form of the Cancellation Rule given in (iv) of Prop. 2.1, showing that, on the assumption that 𝐒{𝐒} is closed under this rule, the canonical model ⟨W,R,V⟩\langle W,R,V\rangle for 𝐒{𝐒} must satisfy the Predecessor Condition. Making this assumption, then, take y∈Wy\in W and note that if the set {◇φ∧□φ∣φ∈y}\{◇\varphi\wedge□\varphi\mid\varphi\in y\} is 𝐒{𝐒}-consistent, then any maximal 𝐒{𝐒}-consistent superset of this set will be an x∈Wx\in W such that xRyxRy and for all z∈Wz\in W, xRzxRz only if z=yz=y. So it remains only to verify that the indicated set is indeed SS-consistent. Suppose otherwise; that is, that for some φ1,…,φn\varphi_1,\ldots,\varphi_n with each φi∈y\varphi_i\in y, we have ¬((◇φ1∧□φ1)∧…∧(◇φn∧□φn))\neg((◇\varphi_1\wedge□\varphi_1)\wedge\ldots\wedge(◇\varphi_n\wedge□\varphi_n)) provable in 𝐒{𝐒}. Thus (◇φ1∧…∧◇φn)→¬(□φ1∧…∧□φn)∈𝐒(◇\varphi_1\wedge\ldots\wedge◇\varphi_n)\to\neg(□\varphi_1\wedge\ldots\wedge□\varphi_n)\in {𝐒}, and hence ◇(φ1∧…∧φn)→¬□(φ1∧…∧φn)∈𝐒◇(\varphi_1\wedge\ldots\wedge\varphi_n)\to\neg□(\varphi_1\wedge\ldots\wedge\varphi_n)\in {𝐒}, and therefore (contraposing): □(φ1∧…∧φn)→□¬(φ1∧…∧φn)∈𝐒□(\varphi_1\wedge\ldots\wedge\varphi_n)\to□\neg(\varphi_1\wedge\ldots\wedge\varphi_n)\in {𝐒}. These manoevres are all justified by the normality (in 𝐒{𝐒}) of □□. We have ended up with a premiss for the rule mentioned in Prop. 2.1(iv), so we conclude that the conclusion, ¬(φ1∧…∧φn)\neg(\varphi_1\wedge\ldots\wedge\varphi_n), is provable in 𝐒{𝐒}: but this contradicts the fact that φ1,…,φn\varphi_1,\ldots,\varphi_n are all drawn from the 𝐒{𝐒}-consistent set yy. We conclude from this contradiction that the set {◇φ∧□φ∣φ∈y}\{◇\varphi\wedge□\varphi\mid\varphi\in y\} is indeed 𝐒{𝐒}-consistent, and so that yy has an RR-predecessor bearing RR only to yy, and thus, since yy was arbitrary, that ⟨W,R,V⟩\langle W,R,V\rangle satisfies the Predecessor Condition. ■

At the end of this section we shall see that several familiar modal logics are not closed under the Cancellation Rule. Before proceeding, we pause to observe that infinitely many normal modal logics are so closed. Let 𝐂n{𝐂}_n be the schema □nφ↔φ□^n\varphi\leftrightarrow\varphi. (As usual, ‘□n□^n’ indicates nn-fold iteration of ‘□□’; the ‘𝐂{𝐂}’ is for ‘cyclic’: see the top left hand diagram of Figure 2 in [12], putting k=0k=0 there.) 𝐊𝐂n{𝐊}{𝐂}_n is to be the smallest normal (monomodal) logic containing all instances of the schema 𝐂n{𝐂}_n. (This makes 𝐊𝐂1{𝐊}{𝐂}_1 the logic called 𝐊𝐓!{𝐊}{𝐓}! in the nomenclature of [3].)

Proposition 2.3. The logics 𝐊𝐂n{𝐊}{𝐂}_n are all closed under the Cancellation Rule; the logics 𝐊𝐂m{𝐊}{𝐂}_m and 𝐊𝐂n{𝐊}{𝐂}_n are distinct whenever m≠nm\ne n.

Proof. The second assertion follows from the unprovability of the instance of 𝐂m{𝐂}_m with φ\varphi as a propositional variable in the logic 𝐊𝐂n{𝐊}{𝐂}_n whenever m<nm<n. (One can check this by an easy semantic argument.) For the first assertion, suppose □φ↔□ψ∈𝐊𝐂n□\varphi\leftrightarrow□\psi\in {𝐊}{𝐂}_n. By normality, we can prefix □n−1□^{n-1} to each side of this biconditional, concluding that □nφ↔□nψ∈𝐊𝐂n□^n\varphi\leftrightarrow□^n\psi\in {𝐊}{𝐂}_n; hence, by appeal to the schema 𝐂n{𝐂}_n we have φ↔ψ∈𝐊𝐂n\varphi\leftrightarrow\psi\in {𝐊}{𝐂}_n. ■

We shall hear more of these logics 𝐊𝐂n{𝐊}{𝐂}_n in subsection 6.2.

Proposition 2.2 is reminiscent of a similar result for the rule of Denecessitation (from: □φ□\varphi to: φ\varphi); when a reference to this rule replaces the reference to the Cancellation Rule, the corresponding result replaces the Predecessor Condition by a weaker condition – that the model in question is ‘converse serial’ in the sense that for all y∈Wy\in W there exists x∈Wx\in W with xRyxRy. (And again, we can further replace the talk of the canonical model’s satisfying this condition by talk of being determined by some class of models all of which satisfy it). Syntactically, the closure of a normal monomodal logic under Denecessitation follows from its closure under the Cancellation Rule by putting ψ=⊤\psi=\top.

The above semantic characterization of Denecessitation is a special case of the results proved in [9] concerning what Lemmon and Scott call the ‘Rule of nn-ary Disjunction’, which they describe a modal logic S\mathrm{S} as possessing when for any φ1,…,φn\varphi_1,\ldots,\varphi_n, if □φ1∨…∨□φn∈𝐒□\varphi_1\vee\ldots\vee□\varphi_n\in {𝐒} then φ1∈𝐒\varphi_1\in {𝐒} or … or φn∈𝐒\varphi_n\in {𝐒}. (Actually, Lemmon and Scott say simply that a logic possesses the Rule of Disjunction when the above condition is satisfied for all nn.) Because of the ‘or’s here, when n>1n>1 we prefer to describe this as a generalized rule rather than as a rule simpliciter. (There is no such thing as the closure of a set of formulas under this ‘rule’.) The corresponding semantic condition is an obvious variation on converse seriality: namely that for any y1,…,yn∈Wy_1,\ldots,y_n\in W there exists x∈Wx\in W with xRy1xRy_1 and … and xRynxRy_n. It may be of some interest to note a similarly generalized version of the Cancellation Rule. Just as the (generalized) Rule of nn-ary Disjunction is the Denecessitation Rule when n=1n=1, so the Cancellation Rule – in the ‘implicational’ form given under Proposition 2.1(iii) – is the n=1n=1 case of the following principle:

If □(φ1∨…∨φn)→(□ψ1∨…∨□ψn)∈𝐒 thenφ1→ψ1∈𝐒 or … or φn→ψn∈𝐒\begin{aligned}&\text{If }□(\varphi_1\vee\ldots\vee\varphi_n)\to(□\psi_1\vee\ldots\vee□\psi_n)\in {𝐒}\text{ then}\\&\qquad\qquad\varphi_1\to\psi_1\in {𝐒}\text{ or }\ldots\text{ or }\varphi_n\to\psi_n\in {𝐒}\end{aligned}

or, equivalently, of the following:

If □φ→(□ψ1∨…∨□ψn)∈𝐒□\varphi\to(□\psi_1\vee\ldots\vee□\psi_n)\in {𝐒} then φ→ψ1∈𝐒\varphi\to\psi_1\in {𝐒} or … or φ→ψn∈𝐒\varphi\to\psi_n\in {𝐒}

To describe the effect of this condition on the canonical model for a system 𝐒{𝐒} satisfying it, it is convenient to use the notation R(w)R(w) for {z∈W∣wRz}\{z\in W\mid wRz\} (understood relative a to a model ⟨W,R,V⟩\langle W,R,V\rangle with w∈Ww\in W), in terms of which the Predecessor Condition itself becomes: for all y∈Wy\in W there exists x∈Wx\in W with R(x)={y}R(x)=\{y\}. Then the corresponding condition for the above generalized Cancellation Rule is: for all y1,…,yn∈Wy_1,\ldots,y_n\in W there exists x∈Wx\in W with R(x)={y1,…,yn}R(x)=\{y_1,\ldots,y_n\}. (Compare this with the condition given above for the nn-ary Disjunction Rule, which replaces ‘==’ by ‘⊇\supseteq’.)

Finally, on the subject of this comparison between the Rule of Disjunction and the generalized Rule of Cancellation, we recall that Lemmon and Scott showed, using a simple ‘adjunction of points’ argument that the smallest normal (mono)modal logic 𝐊{𝐊} satisfies the nn-ary Rule of Disjunction for all n∈▲n\in▲. ([9], proof of Theorem 3.5. This style of argument goes back to [8]. ) The same argument shows – since the point adjoined bears the accessibility relation of the new model to just the selected nn points falsifying φ1,…,φn\varphi_1,\ldots,\varphi_n – that 𝐊{𝐊} satisfies, for each nn, the nn-ary generalized Cancellation Rule above. The corresponding arguments by adjunction of points showing that 𝐊𝟒{𝐊}\mathbf{4}, 𝐊𝐓{𝐊}{𝐓}, and 𝐒𝟒{𝐒}\mathbf{4} (= 𝐊𝐓𝟒{𝐊}{𝐓}\mathbf{4}) have the Rule of Disjunction lack this feature, since we have to keep the new model in the class of transitive, reflexive, and reflexive and transitive models respectively: not surprisingly, since these logics are not closed under the Cancellation Rule. In the case of 𝐊𝟒{𝐊}\mathbf{4}, we can illustrate the failure of closure by noting that □p→□□p∈𝐊𝟒□ p\to□□ p\in {𝐊}\mathbf{4} while p→□p∉𝐊𝟒p\to□ p\notin {𝐊}\mathbf{4}; for the case of 𝐊𝐓{𝐊}{𝐓} we have □(p∧¬□p)→□⊥∈𝐊𝐓□(p\wedge\neg□ p)\to□\bot\in {𝐊}{𝐓} while (p∧¬□p)→⊥∉𝐊𝐓(p\wedge\neg□ p)\to\bot\notin {𝐊}{𝐓}. Either illustration makes the point for the case of 𝐒𝟒{𝐒}\mathbf{4}. (Note that we have used the version of the Cancellation Rule given in Prop 2.1(iii), and that – here and below – we abbreviate (say) ‘p0p_0’ to ‘pp’. For a variation – due to de Jongh – on this rule, under which 𝐊𝟒{𝐊}\mathbf{4} is closed, see [13], p.70, p.97f., and p. 105.)

We adapt the system of nomenclature for normal modal logics of [3] in the following way. Example 3.1. (i) 𝐊𝐓c𝐋=𝐊𝐋𝐓{𝐊}{𝐓}_c{𝐋}={𝐊}{𝐋}{𝐓}; (ii) 𝐊𝐓𝐋=𝐊𝐋𝐓c{𝐊}{𝐓}{𝐋}={𝐊}{𝐋}{𝐓}_c.

Example 3.2. 𝐊𝐓!𝐋=𝐊𝟒!𝐋=𝐊𝟒𝐋=𝐊𝐋𝟒=𝐊𝐋𝟒!=𝐊𝐋𝐓!{𝐊}{𝐓}!{𝐋}={𝐊}\mathbf{4}!{𝐋}={𝐊}\mathbf{4}{𝐋}={𝐊}{𝐋}\mathbf{4}={𝐊}{𝐋}\mathbf{4}!={𝐊}{𝐋}{𝐓}!

The logic featured in Example 3.2 is a Post-complete normal bimodal logic (a dual atom in the lattice – ordered by inclusion – of all such logics, that is): since the operators □□ and LL are both vacuous in this logic (which has the redundancy – involving label 𝐊𝐓!𝐋𝐓!{𝐊}{𝐓}!{𝐋}{𝐓}!), any unprovable formula is provably equivalent to a non-tautologous formula free of modal operators, so that its addition would yield the inconsistent logic. In fact, by an argument which we shall not give here, this is the only Post-complete extension of any normal extension of 𝐊𝐋{𝐊}{𝐋} which has a finite model (note: we are not saying ‘which has a finite characteristic model’); however – as again we shall not show here – there are normal extensions of 𝐊𝐋{𝐊}{𝐋} which lack finite models, and uncountably many of these are Post-complete. The same example illustrates something else: since the monomodal logic 𝐊𝟒!{𝐊}\mathbf{4}! is a proper subsystem of 𝐊𝐓!{𝐊}{𝐓}!, while 𝐊𝐓!𝐋=𝐊𝟒!𝐋{𝐊}{𝐓}!{𝐋}={𝐊}\mathbf{4}!{𝐋}, the extension to a normal bimodal logic with a left inverse for □□ is non-conservative. This is not surprising, of course, since 𝐊𝟒!{𝐊}\mathbf{4}! (and for that matter 𝐊𝟒{𝐊}\mathbf{4} itself, as was mentioned at the end of the preceding section) is not closed under the Cancellation Rule. We make the considerations at work here explicit in Theorem 3.4 below.

To this end, we need a lemma dealing with what we call expanded models. Where ⟨W,R,V⟩\langle W,R,V\rangle is any model satisfying the Predecessor Condition, we call ⟨W,R,f,V⟩\langle W,R,f,V\rangle an expanded model if ff is a function (from WW to WW) assigning to each y∈Wy\in W some element of the (ex hypothesi, non-empty) set {x∈W∣R(x)={y}}\{x\in W\mid R(x)=\{y\}\}. We call ⟨W,R,f,V⟩\langle W,R,f,V\rangle an expansion of the original model ⟨W,R,V⟩\langle W,R,V\rangle. (Typically, a model which satisfies the Predecessor Condition will have more than one expansion, since there will be more than one selection function ff available to choose for each element some element bearing RR to precisely that element.) Truth in an expanded model is given the usual inductive definition supplemented by the following clause for LL: a formula LφL\varphi is true at ww just in case φ\varphi is true at f(w)f(w). We make an obvious observation using the following terminology. A model (an expanded model) throughout which every formula in some set is true is called a model for (expanded model for) that set of formulas. A frame for (expanded frame for) a set of formulas – typically a logic – is a frame (expanded frame) every model (expanded model) on which is a model (an expanded model) for that set of formulas.

Lemma 3.3. If ⟨W,R⟩\langle W,R\rangle is a frame satisfying the Predecessor Condition for a normal monomodal logic 𝐒{𝐒}, then any expansion ⟨W,R,f⟩\langle W,R,f\rangle (of ⟨W,R⟩\langle W,R\rangle) is a frame for 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋}.

Proof. It suffices to check that LL’s normality and left-inverse properties give only formulas valid on the expanded model. ■

As usual, we call a normal monomodal logic canonical when the frame of the canonical model for that logic is a frame for the logic.

Theorem 3.4. For any canonical normal monomodal logic 𝐒{𝐒}: 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋} is a conservative extension of 𝐒{𝐒} if and only if 𝐒{𝐒} is closed under the Rule of Cancellation.

Proof. ‘Only if’: Suppose that 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋} is a conservative extension of 𝐒{𝐒} and that □φ↔□ψ∈𝐒□\varphi\leftrightarrow□\psi\in {𝐒}. Since 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋} is closed under the Rule of Cancellation, φ↔ψ∈𝐒𝐋\varphi\leftrightarrow\psi\in {𝐒}{𝐋}. But as □φ↔□ψ∈𝐒□\varphi\leftrightarrow□\psi\in {𝐒}, φ\varphi and ψ\psi (and therefore also φ↔ψ\varphi\leftrightarrow\psi) do not contain LL, so since 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋} is a conservative extension of 𝐒{𝐒}, φ↔ψ∈𝐒\varphi\leftrightarrow\psi\in {𝐒}.

‘If’: Suppose that (canonical) 𝐒{𝐒} is closed under the Rule of Cancellation and that φ∈𝐒𝐋\varphi\in {𝐒}{𝐋}, for LL-free φ\varphi. We must show that φ∈𝐒\varphi\in {𝐒}. So suppose otherwise. Then φ\varphi is false at some point ww in the canonical model ⟨W,R,V⟩\langle W,R,V\rangle for 𝐒{𝐒}, which model, by Proposition 2.2, satisfies the Predecessor Condition. In any expansion ⟨W,R,f,V⟩\langle W,R,f,V\rangle of this model, φ\varphi is false at ww, since φ\varphi does not contain LL and the definition of truth for such formulas makes no reference to ff and so agrees with truth at the given point in the original model. Thus ⟨W,R,f⟩\langle W,R,f\rangle is not an expanded frame for 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋}, which contradicts Lemma 3.3, since, 𝐒{𝐒} being canonical, ⟨W,R⟩\langle W,R\rangle is a frame for 𝐒{𝐒}. ■

The expanded models in play here verify not just the provable formulas of 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋} but of the stronger logic 𝐒𝐋𝐃!{𝐒}{𝐋}{𝐃}!. As a result, we have for example:

Proposition 3.5. 𝐊𝐋𝐃!{𝐊}{𝐋}{𝐃}! is determined by the class of all expanded models.

Proof. Soundness: Clear.

Completeness: By a canonical model construction. Specifically, for a 𝐊𝐋𝐃!{𝐊}{𝐋}{𝐃}! maximal consistent set yy, define f(y)f(y) to be {ψ∣Lψ∈y}\{\psi\mid L\psi\in y\}; by 𝐃{𝐃}! (for LL) this set is maximal consistent, and automatically settles the case of φ=Lψ\varphi=L\psi in the inductive part of the proof that φ\varphi is true at ww in the model if and only if φ∈w\varphi\in w. It remains only to check that, with ff so defined, and RR (as well as WW and VV) defined as usual, ⟨W,R,f,V⟩\langle W,R,f,V\rangle is an expanded model: that is, that R(f(y))={y}R(f(y))=\{y\}. It is left to the reader to exploit the left inverse schema to show this. ■

The leap from 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋} to 𝐒𝐋𝐃!{𝐒}{𝐋}{𝐃}! is, incidentally, not so great as it may appear from this labelling, because already 𝐒𝐋=𝐒𝐋𝐃{𝐒}{𝐋}={𝐒}{𝐋}{𝐃} (so 𝐒𝐋𝐃!=𝐒𝐋𝐃c{𝐒}{𝐋}{𝐃}!={𝐒}{𝐋}{𝐃}_c). To substantiate this claim, it suffices to show that 𝐃{𝐃} holds for LL in 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋}, in the sense that the equivalent form ¬L⊥\neg L\bot, is provable in 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋}: but this is immediate from a special case of the left inverse schema, namely L□⊥↔⊥L□\bot\leftrightarrow\bot.

Given that the expanded models above provide a semantic treatment specifically for the systems 𝐒𝐋𝐃!{𝐒}{𝐋}{𝐃}! (and their extensions), what can be offered in a similar vein for the weaker 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋} (alias 𝐒𝐋𝐃{𝐒}{𝐋}{𝐃})? As usual for a normal bimodal logic, we need two accessibility relations in our models, and we denote the additional relation (for interpreting LL) by R′R'. That is, we count LφL\varphi as true at ww in a (bi-relational) model ⟨W,R,R′,V⟩\langle W,R,R',V\rangle just in case φ\varphi is true at every xx such that wR′xwR'x. For the basic system, 𝐊𝐋{𝐊}{𝐋}, the conditions we need to impose on such models are the following (using some obvious abbreviative notation, and taking variables to range over WW):

∀u∃v(uR′vRu)\forall u\,\exists v\,(uR'vRu)(1)

∀u,v,w(uR′vRw⇒w=u)\forall u,v,w\,(uR'vRw\Rightarrow w=u)(2)

For any normal monomodal logic 𝐒{𝐒} closed under the Cancellation Rule, the logic 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋} is determined by some class of such models; for the weakest such logic, we have the following (predictable) result.

Proposition 3.6. 𝐊𝐋{𝐊}{𝐋} is determined by the class of all bi-relational models satisfying conditions (1) and (2) above.

In a notation which is more suggestive in the light of the considerations reviewed in Section 1, we can rewrite (1) as I⊆R′∘RI\subseteq R'\circ R, where II is the identity relation on WW and ∘\circ denotes composition of relations (‘relative product’). Similarly, (2) amounts to the converse inclusion, so that together we have R′∘R=IR'\circ R=I, stating that R′R' is a left inverse of RR in the semigroup of binary relations (under ∘\circ) on WW. Let us say that a relation R⊆W×WR\subseteq W\times W is left-invertible if the following condition is satisfied:

∃R′(R′∘R=I)\exists R'\,(R'\circ R=I)(∗)

More explicitly, we should say ‘left-invertible over WW’, but let us take some fixed WW as a background domain. Now (*) is a second-order condition on R\mathrm{R}, because it quantifies over relations on, rather than just elements of, this domain. If we ask whether there is a purely first-order condition equivalent to (*), we answer with following, which may be thought of as a ‘modal-logic’ – free version of Theorem 3.4.

Proposition 3.7. For any set WW with R⊆W×WR\subseteq W\times W, RR is left-invertible if and only if ⟨W,R⟩\langle W,R\rangle satisfies the Predecessor Condition: ∀y∃x∀z(xRz⇔z=y)\forall y\,\exists x\,\forall z\,(xRz\Leftrightarrow z=y).

Proof. ‘If’: Suppose the Predecessor Condition is satisfied, so that for each y∈Wy\in W there exists x∈Wx\in W with R(x)={y}R(x)=\{y\}. Let f(y)f(y) be some such xx, and define R′={<y,f(y)>∣y∈W}R'=\{<y,f(y)>\mid y\in W\}, noting that R′∘R=IR'\circ R=I.

‘Only if’: Let R′R' be a left inverse of RR, so that (1) and (2) are satisfied. Then for any u∈Wu\in W a vv as promised by (1) bears RR to uu and, by (2), only to uu: so the Predecessor Condition is satisfied. ■

Our discussion of left inverses has concentrated on the case of normal rather than merely congruential such inverses. The notation for logics here would be an obvious adaptation of that given above: for example, 𝐊𝐃(𝐋𝟒){𝐊}{𝐃}({𝐋}\mathbf{4}) would be the bimodal logic with normal □□ satisfying the 𝐃{𝐃} schema and congruential LL satisfying the left inverse schema and the 4 schema. We address such congruential left inverse extensions of monomodal 𝐒{𝐒} with the following observations. (The straightforward proof of (i) is omitted; (ii) follows from (i) and Thm. 3.4.)

Proposition 3.8. For any normal monomodal logic 𝐒{𝐒}:

(i)
𝐒{𝐒} is closed under Cancellation if and only if 𝐒(𝐋){𝐒}({𝐋}) extends 𝐒{𝐒} conservatively
(ii)
If 𝐒{𝐒} is canonical, then 𝐒(𝐋){𝐒}({𝐋}) extends 𝐒{𝐒} conservatively if and only if 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋} extends 𝐒{𝐒} conservatively.

We conclude this section with an open problem: how to prove or refute the claim resulting from Thm. 3.4 by deleting the word ‘canonical’. Such a claim would allow a similar deletion from Prop. 3.8(ii) and would follow from a version of Lemma 3.3 in terms of models rather than frames, which read instead: ‘If ⟨W,R,V⟩\langle W,R,V\rangle is a model, satisfying the Predecessor Condition, for a normal monomodal logic 𝐒{𝐒}, then any expansion ⟨W,R,f,V⟩\langle W,R,f,V\rangle (of ⟨W,R,V⟩\langle W,R,V\rangle) is a model for 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋}’; however, the proof sketched for Lemma 3.3 itself would not justify this new version (there being no reason to suppose that substitution instances, in the language of 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋}, of theorems of 𝐒{𝐒}, were all true throughout the expanded model). The only progress we have been able to make in the direction of showing that ‘canonical’ can be deleted from the statement of Thm. 3.4 has been to show (by an argument we do not give here) that such a deletion is possible for the case of normal monomodal 𝐒{𝐒} for which 𝐒⊈𝐊𝐃𝟒{𝐒}\nsubseteq {𝐊}{𝐃}\mathbf{4}: for all such 𝐒{𝐒}, that is, closure under Cancellation guarantees that extension to SL\mathrm{SL} is conservative.

4. Right Inverses

Because ○i\bigcirc_i is a left inverse of ○j\bigcirc_j just in case ○j\bigcirc_j is a right inverse of ○i\bigcirc_i, some attention has in effect already been given to issues concerning right inverses for □□ in the course of the above discussion of left inverses. If we denote by φ#\varphi^\# the result of replacing, simultaneously, all occurrences of ‘□□’ by ‘RR’ and all occurrences of ‘LL’ by ‘□□’ in a formula φ\varphi of the language (with modal operators LL and □□) of the preceding section, and put, for a set Φ\mathrm{\Phi} of such formulas, Φ#={φ#∣φ∈Φ}\mathrm{\Phi}^\#=\{\varphi^\#\mid\varphi\in\mathrm{\Phi}\}, then 𝐊𝐑=(𝐊𝐋)#{𝐊}{𝐑}=({𝐊}{𝐋})^\#, for example. (Here we assume the labelling policy described at the start of Section 3 in terms of the notation ‘𝐊_𝐋_{𝐊}\_{{𝐋}}\_’ taken over mutatis mutandis for ‘𝐊_𝐑_{𝐊}\_{{𝐑}}\_’.) Similarly, where 𝐗,𝐘{𝐗},{𝐘}, are any schema-labels, 𝐊𝐗𝐑𝐘=(𝐊𝐘𝐋𝐗)#{𝐊}{𝐗}{𝐑}{𝐘}=({𝐊}{𝐘}{𝐋}{𝐗})^\#. By this correspondence, we have, for example, that 𝐊𝐑{𝐊}{𝐑} is closed under the Cancellation Rule (not for □□ but) for RR, i.e. the rule From: Rφ↔RψR\varphi\leftrightarrow R\psi to: φ↔ψ\varphi\leftrightarrow\psi. Although viewed ‘statically’, the correspondence means that logics with a normal modal operator having a normal right inverse do not raise issues not already raised by logics with a normal modal operator having a normal left inverse, the dynamics of extending a given normal modal logic to one with a normal right inverse are not the same as those for the analogous extension with a left inverse – to say nothing of the case in which we ask only for a congruential inverse. In the first place, the above rule of Cancellation for R\mathrm{R} is a rule under which 𝐒𝐑{𝐒}{𝐑}, and indeed 𝐒(𝐑){𝐒}({𝐑}), is closed, whereas we should ideally like, given Prop. 3.8 and the discussion following it, a syntactic condition on the original monomodal 𝐒{𝐒} which is necessary and sufficient for those systems to extend 𝐒{𝐒} conservatively. A second complication arises from the fact that the question of whether the extension of 𝐒{𝐒} to 𝐒(𝐑){𝐒}({𝐑}) is conservative and that of whether the extension to 𝐒𝐑{𝐒}{𝐑} is conservative questions need no longer have the same answer for a given canonical 𝐒{𝐒}, by contrast with the left inverse case (Prop. 3.8):

Example 4.1. 𝐊𝐓(𝐑){𝐊}{𝐓}({𝐑}) is a conservative extension of 𝐊𝐓{𝐊}{𝐓}; but 𝐊𝐓𝐑{𝐊}{𝐓}{𝐑} is not a conservative extension of 𝐊𝐓{𝐊}{𝐓}.

The first assertion here can be extracted from [16]; the second (also implicit in [16]) is justified as follows. The right inverse schema and 𝐓{𝐓} give φ→Rφ∈𝐊𝐓𝐑\varphi\to R\varphi\in {𝐊}{𝐓}{𝐑}, for all formulas φ\varphi, so taking φ\varphi as Rp→pRp\to p, we have

(1)
⊢𝐊𝐓𝐑(Rp→p)→R(Rp→p)\vdash_{{𝐊}{𝐓}{𝐑}}(Rp\to p)\to R(Rp\to p).

The normality of RR gives Rp→R(ψ→p)Rp\to R(\psi\to p) for all ψ\psi; so taking ψ\psi as RpRp, we have

(2)
⊢𝐊𝐓𝐑Rp→R(Rp→p)\vdash_{{𝐊}{𝐓}{𝐑}}Rp\to R(Rp\to p)

By truth-functional reasoning from (1) and (2), we have

(3)
⊢𝐊𝐓𝐑R(Rp→p)\vdash_{{𝐊}{𝐓}{𝐑}}R(Rp\to p)

So by Necessitation (with □□) and the right inverse schema:

(4)
⊢𝐊𝐓𝐑Rp→p\vdash_{{𝐊}{𝐓}{𝐑}}Rp\to p

Then, since □□ is normal,

(5)
⊢𝐊𝐓𝐑□Rp→□p\vdash_{{𝐊}{𝐓}{𝐑}}□ Rp\to□ p

Finally, by appeal to the right inverse schema, we conclude that p→□p∈𝐊𝐓𝐑p\to□ p\in {𝐊}{𝐓}{𝐑}. Since p→□p∉𝐊𝐓p\to□ p\notin {𝐊}{𝐓}, the extension is non-conservative.

Since 𝐒(𝐑)⊆𝐒𝐑{𝐒}({𝐑})\subseteq {𝐒}{𝐑}, whenever 𝐒𝐑{𝐒}{𝐑} extends 𝐒{𝐒} conservatively, 𝐒(𝐑){𝐒}({𝐑}) does; but as the above example shows, one can have 𝐒(𝐑){𝐒}({𝐑}) conservatively extending 𝐒{𝐒} without 𝐒𝐑{𝐒}{𝐑} doing so. We should like to have syntactic conditions on 𝐒{𝐒} which reliably indicate the conservativeness of the extensions in each case. We only possess such information in the former case (the extension of 𝐒{𝐒} to 𝐒(𝐑){𝐒}({𝐑})), however. For this case, it is necessary to adjust the rule mentioned under the name ‘Cancellation for RR’ above. As was pointed out, closure under this rule is not an appropriate syntactic condition on the RR-free system 𝐒{𝐒} to be extended. What is wanted is, rather, a condition of closure under a rule we shall call Inner Cancellation (or more explicitly: Inner Cancellation for □□). To formulate the rule, we use the notation s□s_□ to denote the substitution which replaces each propositional variable pip_i by □pi□ p_i. In fact, more generally, for any kk-ary connective κ\kappa, we shall understand by sκs_\kappa the substitution which maps each pip_i to κ(pi,…,pi)\kappa(p_i,\ldots,p_i) (kk occurrences of pip_i). In what follows we exploit this notation only for cases in which k=0k=0 or k=1k=1.

Inner Cancellation

𝐹𝑟𝑜𝑚:s□(φ)𝑇𝑜:φ.\begin{aligned} \mathit{From}:&\quad s_□(\varphi)\\ \mathit{To}:&\quad\varphi. \end{aligned}

Thus, for example, if □◇□p→◇□p∈𝐒□◇□ p\to◇□ p\in {𝐒} and 𝐒{𝐒} is closed under Inner Cancellation, then □◇p→◇p∈𝐒□◇ p\to◇ p\in {𝐒}. As this example illustrates, Inner Cancellation deletes innermost occurrences of □□, while our familiar Cancellation rule for □□ – which might accordingly be called ‘Outer’ Cancellation – deletes outermost occurrences. In some cases the results are the same. For example, as applied to the premiss □p→□□p□ p\to□□ p both yield the same conclusion: p→□pp\to□ p. It follows, by the reasoning of the following paragraph, that 𝐊𝟒{𝐊}\mathbf{4} and 𝐒𝟒{𝐒}\mathbf{4} are extended non-conservatively when they are extended to include a congruential right inverse for □□, no less than – as the last paragraph of Section 2 (together with Prop. 3.8) showed – when they are extended to include a congruential left inverse.

It is not hard to see that closure of 𝐒{𝐒} under Inner Cancellation is a necessary condition for the extension to 𝐒(𝐑){𝐒}({𝐑}) to be conservative. For suppose that s□(φ)∈𝐒s_□(\varphi)\in {𝐒}; then s□(φ)∈𝐒(𝐑)s_□(\varphi)\in {𝐒}({𝐑}), so by Uniform Substitution, sRs□(φ)∈𝐒(𝐑)s_Rs_□(\varphi)\in {𝐒}({𝐑}). Now, sRs□(p)=□Rps_Rs_□(p)=□ Rp, so for all φ\varphi, φ↔sRs□(φ)∈𝐒(𝐑)\varphi\leftrightarrow s_Rs_□(\varphi)\in {𝐒}({𝐑}). Therefore φ∈𝐒(𝐑)\varphi\in {𝐒}({𝐑}), and φ\varphi is RR-free, so unless 𝐒{𝐒} is closed under Inner Cancellation, the passage to 𝐒(𝐑){𝐒}({𝐑}) will be non-conservative. In fact, closure (of 𝐒{𝐒}) under Inner Cancellation is not only necessary, but also sufficient for 𝐒(𝐑){𝐒}({𝐑}) to be a conservative extension. Our proof of this consists in the construction, for an arbitrary normal monomodal 𝐒{𝐒} (assumed closed under Inner Cancellation), of a system 𝐒+{𝐒}^+ with 𝐒(𝐑)⊆𝐒+{𝐒}({𝐑})\subseteq {𝐒}^+ and with 𝐒+{𝐒}^+ demonstrably a conservatively extension of 𝐒{𝐒}. It follows that 𝐒(𝐑){𝐒}({𝐑}) conservatively extends 𝐒{𝐒}. As the construction in question is somewhat elaborate, we economize on space by omitting a proof for the ‘if’ direction:

Theorem 4.2. For any normal monomodal logic 𝐒{𝐒}, 𝐒(𝐑){𝐒}({𝐑}) is a conservative extension of 𝐒{𝐒} if and only if 𝐒{𝐒} is closed under Inner Cancellation.

We recall the concept of a universally representative connective, introduced for the case of the connective □□ in Section 1: a connective κ\kappa is universally representative in a logic if in that logic every formula is provably equivalent to a formula of the form κ(φ1,…,φk)\kappa(\varphi_1,\ldots,\varphi_k). Here κ\kappa is assumed kk-ary and for notational convenience in what follows we consider the case of k=1k=1. Universal representativeness is related to the Inner Cancellation rule for κ\kappa by the following considerations. (To make the following points in full generality, allowing k>1k>1, we should need to modify our convention as to what the substitutions sκs_\kappa do.) To show that a connective is not universally representative in a logic – not necessarily an nn-modal logic by the definition of Section 1, but assumed closed under Uniform Substitution and to be congruential in an obvious enough sense – it suffices to show that the logic treats that connective in what we may call a special way: meaning that there is a feature the logic accords to all formulas with the given connective as main connective, but not to all formulas whatever. For example, negation is ‘special’ in intuitionistic logic because negated formulas have the following feature not shared by all formulas: they are provably implied by their own double negations. The fact that negated formulas are thus special shows that negation is not universally representative in intuitionistic logic, since this would make every formula equivalent to a formula having the feature in question, whence, by congruentiality, every formula would have that feature after all. Similarly, in 𝐊𝟒{𝐊}\mathbf{4} (or 𝐒𝟒{𝐒}\mathbf{4}) □□ is special because □□-formulas provably imply their own necessitations while not all formulas do, and in the Verum system (K+□⊥)(K+□\bot) □□ is special because because all □□-formulas are provable while not all formulas are. Accordingly, by the same reasoning as before, □□ is not universally representative in these modal logics. Contraposing the general statement here – that being special implies not being universally representative – we get: any universally representative connective is non-special. But this is just to say (given closure under Uniform Substitution) that if κ\kappa is universally representative in a logic, then that logic is closed under Inner Cancellation for κ\kappa: the rule for passing, for arbitrary φ\varphi, from sκ(φ)s_\kappa(\varphi) to φ\varphi. The converse is, incidentally, false. For example, 𝐊𝐃{𝐊}{𝐃} is closed under Inner Cancellation for □□ – this connective is non-special in 𝐊𝐃{𝐊}{𝐃}, in the other terminology – though □□ is not universally representative in that logic. The latter claim follows from the fact that □□ is not universally representative in 𝐒𝟒{𝐒}\mathbf{4} (by the argument above) and 𝐒𝟒⊇𝐊𝐃{𝐒}\mathbf{4}\supseteq {𝐊}{𝐃}. The former claim follows from Thm. 4.2 and Prop. 5.2 together with a remark about 𝐊𝐃{𝐊}{𝐃} which precedes the statement, below, of Prop. 5.2. Bibliographical note: the examples of non-universality in intuitionistic logic and modal logic given above may be found in [7], $$12.4 and 27.2, respectively; an earlier source for these and several other examples of non-universality in virtue of ‘specialness’ is [10].

Lemma 4.3. If 𝐒{𝐒} is a normal monomodal logic with ⊢𝐒p→□◇kp\vdash_{{𝐒}} p\to□◇^k p for some k≥0k\ge0, then if 𝐒(𝐑){𝐒}({𝐑}) is a conservative extension of 𝐒{𝐒}, we have ⊢𝐒□k◇p→p\vdash_{{𝐒}}□^k◇ p\to p.

Proof. Given the hypothesis of the Lemma, we show by induction on i≤ki\le k that ⊢𝐒□i◇□k−ip→p\vdash_{{𝐒}}□^i◇□^{k-i}p\to p. For i=0i=0, this follows from normality. For the induction step, suppose that i<ki<k and ⊢𝐒□i◇□k−ip→p\vdash_{{𝐒}}□^i◇□^{k-i}p\to p. By Uniform Substitution, ⊢𝐒(𝐑)□i◇□k−iRp→Rp\vdash_{{𝐒}({𝐑})}□^i◇□^{k-i}Rp\to Rp, so by the normality of □□, ⊢𝐒(𝐑)□i+1◇□k−iRp→□Rp\vdash_{{𝐒}({𝐑})}□^{i+1}◇□^{k-i}Rp\to□ Rp. Since RR is a right inverse of □□, we have (using the congruentiality of □□ to make the replacement in the antecedent) ⊢𝐒(𝐑)□i+1◇□k−(i+1)p→p\vdash_{{𝐒}({𝐑})}□^{i+1}◇□^{k-(i+1)}p\to p. Since 𝐒(𝐑){𝐒}({𝐑}) is a conservative extension of 𝐒{𝐒}, ⊢𝐒□i+1◇□k−(i+1)p→p\vdash_{{𝐒}}□^{i+1}◇□^{k-(i+1)}p\to p, completing the induction. The Lemma then follows by taking i=ki=k. ■

Proposition 4.4. Let 𝐒{𝐒} be a normal monomodal system with ⊢𝐒p→□◇kp\vdash_{{𝐒}} p\to□◇^k p for some k≥0k\ge0. Then 𝐒(𝐑){𝐒}({𝐑}) is a conservative extension of 𝐒{𝐒} if and only if ⊢𝐒p↔□k+1p\vdash_{{𝐒}} p\leftrightarrow□^{k+1}p.

Proof. ‘If’. Even without the assumption that p→□◇kp∈𝐒p\to□◇^k p\in {𝐒}, if p↔□k+1p∈𝐒p\leftrightarrow□^{k+1}p\in {𝐒}, then □k□^k is a congruential right inverse of □□ within 𝐒{𝐒}, so the extension of 𝐒{𝐒} by Rφ↔□kφR\varphi\leftrightarrow□^k\varphi (all formulas φ\varphi) is a conservative extension of 𝐒{𝐒}, which itself includes the logic 𝐒(𝐑){𝐒}({𝐑}).

‘Only if’. Suppose 𝐒(𝐑){𝐒}({𝐑}) extends 𝐒{𝐒} conservatively and ⊢𝐒p→□◇kp\vdash_{{𝐒}} p\to□◇^k p. By Lemma 4.3, we have (1) ⊢𝐒□k◇p→p\vdash_{{𝐒}}□^k◇ p\to p. The supposition also gives us that 𝐒⊇𝐊𝐃{𝐒}\supseteq {𝐊}{𝐃} (see below), so, since ⊢𝐊𝐃□k+1p→□k◇p\vdash_{{𝐊}{𝐃}}□^{k+1}p\to□^k◇ p, we have (2) ⊢𝐒□k+1p→□k◇p\vdash_{{𝐒}}□^{k+1}p\to□^k◇ p. From (1) and (2), we conclude: (3) ⊢𝐒□k+1p→p\vdash_{{𝐒}}□^{k+1}p\to p. For the converse implication, we note that (3) yields, by normality, ⊢𝐒¬□k+1(p∧¬□k+1p)\vdash_{{𝐒}}\neg□^{k+1}(p\wedge\neg□^{k+1}p), so: (4) ⊢𝐒◇k+1(p→□k+1p)\vdash_{{𝐒}}◇^{k+1}(p\to□^{k+1}p). Now (1) implies that for all φ\varphi, if ◇φ∈𝐒◇\varphi\in {𝐒} then φ∈𝐒\varphi\in {𝐒}; for by normality if ◇φ∈𝐒◇\varphi\in {𝐒} then □k◇φ∈𝐒□^k◇\varphi\in {𝐒}, so Uniform Substitution (of φ\varphi for pp) in (1) and Modus Ponens give φ∈𝐒\varphi\in {𝐒}. Repeated appeals to this rule of ‘Depossibilitation’ deliver the conclusion that ⊢𝐒p→□k+1p\vdash_{{𝐒}} p\to□^{k+1}p, from (4). Putting this together with (3) then gives the desired result: ⊢𝐒p↔□k+1p\vdash_{{𝐒}} p\leftrightarrow□^{k+1}p. ■

5. The Successor Condition

Although, as we have remarked, we do not know of a syntactic condition on 𝐒{𝐒} which is necessary and sufficient for the normal right inverse extension 𝐒𝐑{𝐒}{𝐑} to be conservative, unlike the case of conservative extension to 𝐒(𝐑){𝐒}({𝐑}) (Thm. 4.2) and to 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋} (for canonical 𝐒{𝐒} at least) and 𝐒(𝐋){𝐒}({𝐋}) (Thm. 3.4, Prop. 3.8), a consideration of the route taken to the latter results especially – the route via the Predecessor Condition – turns out to connect fruitfully with the topics we have been exploring.

According to Prop. 3.7, which we described as a (modal) logic – free version of the main result for the systems 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋}, Theorem 3.4, the second-order condition of left-invertibility had a first order equivalent in the shape of: the Predecessor Condition – a condition satisfied by the canonical models of precisely those monomodal logics which were closed under the (‘outer’) Cancellation Rule. The question naturally arises, then, as to what the analogue of the Predecessor Condition might be when we turn our attention from left-invertibility to right-invertibility. By parity with the ‘left’ case, we are understanding the claim that R⊆W×WR\subseteq W\times W is right-invertible to mean that

∃R′(R∘R′=I)\exists R'\,(R\circ R'=I)(∗∗)

We can obtain a first-order condition equivalent to (**) by considering the following well-known property of composition of relations and their converses (symbolized here by the superscript ‘−1{}^{-1}’):

  • For any binary relations S,TS,T: (S∘T)−1=T−1∘S−1(S\circ T)^{-1}=T^{-1}\circ S^{-1}

Thus (**) is equivalent to the condition that ∃R′(R′−1∘R−1=I)\exists R'\,(R'^{-1}\circ R^{-1}=I). (Here we have tacitly used the fact that I−1=II^{-1}=I.) The right-invertibility of RR is therefore equivalent to the left-invertibility of R−1R^{-1}, so we can give a first order characterization of the right-invertibility of RR by appealing to Proposition 3.7. Accordingly, let us say that a frame ⟨W,R⟩\langle W,R\rangle – or, for later, a model ⟨W,R,V⟩\langle W,R,V\rangle – satisfies the Successor Condition just in case for all x∈Wx\in W there exists y∈Wy\in W such that xRyxRy and for all z∈Wz\in W, zRyzRy implies x=zx=z. Here we have adapted the definition given for the Predecessor Condition following Proposition 2.1. More concisely, since the Predecessor Condition can be formulated as: ∀u∃v.R(v)={u}\forall u\,\exists v.R(v)=\{u\} (quantifiers ranging over WW), so the Successor Condition can be put as: ∀u∃v.R−1(v)={u}\forall u\,\exists v.R^{-1}(v)=\{u\}. What Proposition 3.7 delivers then, is the following.

Proposition 5.1. For any set WW with R⊆W×WR\subseteq W\times W, RR is right-invertible if and only if ⟨W,R⟩\langle W,R\rangle satisfies the Successor Condition.

The Successor Condition, as one might expect, is intimately connected with the conservativeness of 𝐒𝐑{𝐒}{𝐑} over 𝐒{𝐒}, though not in quite the way the Predecessor Condition was connected with the conservativeness of 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋} over 𝐒{𝐒}. Proposition 2.2 and Theorem 3.4 can be combined into this claim:

For any canonical normal monomodal logic 𝐒{𝐒} the following are equivalent:

(i)
𝐒{𝐒} is closed under the Rule of (Outer) Cancellation
(ii)
𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋} is a conservative extension of 𝐒{𝐒}
(iii)
The canonical model for 𝐒{𝐒} satisfies the Predecessor Condition.

As was mentioned in Section 2, we could extend this list by adding:

(iv)
𝐒{𝐒} is determined by some class of models satisfying the Predecessor Condition.

The point is that (iv) implies (i) by essentially the same argument as that supplied in the proof of the ‘If’ direction of Prop. 2.2; conversely, (iii) obviously implies (iv).

In the case of the Successor Condition, however, the analogues of (iii) and (iv) are not equivalent: a canonical logic can be determined by a class of models satisfying the condition without the canonical model’s satisfying that condition. 𝐊𝐃{𝐊}{𝐃} provides an example: its canonical model does not satisfy the condition, while various other classes of models determining the logic – models on the unravelled frames of [11], for instance, or on the ‘canonical’ subordination frame of [6], p. 115 – do satisfy it. It is the analogue of (iv), rather than of (iii), that turns out to be equivalent to the right-handed analogue of (ii), as we now show. (The ‘successor’ analogue of (iii) will appear in the following section – see especially Thm. 6.10.)

Proposition 5.2. For any normal monomodal logic 𝐒{𝐒}:

(i)
𝐒𝐑{𝐒}{𝐑} is a conservative extension of 𝐒{𝐒} if 𝐒{𝐒} is determined by some class of frames satisfying the Successor Condition.
(ii)
𝐒𝐑{𝐒}{𝐑} is a conservative extension of 𝐒{𝐒} only if 𝐒{𝐒} is determined by some class of models satisfying the Successor Condition.

Proof. (i) If S\mathrm{S} is determined by a class of frames satisfying the Successor Condition then each frame ⟨W,R⟩\langle W,R\rangle in the class can be expanded to a frame ⟨W,R,g⟩\langle W,R,g\rangle as with the expanded frames introduced for Lemma 3.3 except that gg now assigns to each element of WW some point to which it and only it bears the relation RR. Reasoning parallel to that of the proof of Lemma 3.3 and Theorem 3.4 (‘if’ direction) then gives the result. (ii) Since 𝐒𝐑{𝐒}{𝐑} is a normal bimodal logic its canonical (bi-relational) model can be defined in the usual way. Appealing to the ‘□Rφ→φ□ R\varphi\to\varphi’ direction of the right inverse schema gives, for any point xx, some point yy to which it bears the accessibility relation associated with □□ which in turn bears the accessibility relation associated with RR to xx; appealing to the ‘φ→□Rφ\varphi\to□ R\varphi’ direction of the schema then shows that no point other than xx bears the former relation to yy. The reduct of this model obtained by discarding the latter relation thus meets the Successor Condition, and is a characteristic model for 𝐒{𝐒} on the assumption that 𝐒𝐑{𝐒}{𝐑} conservatively extends 𝐒{𝐒}. (The unit class of this model provides the class of models promised by (ii).) ■

We conclude our discussion of the Successor Condition with a technical observation concerning a restricted class of frames satisfying it. We shall need to appeal to this result in the following section. We call a frame functional when each element of the frame bears the accessibility relation of the frame to exactly one element. (These are the ‘totally functional’ frames of [12].)

Lemma 5.3.

(i)

6. Three Additional Topics

We treat this section as an Appendix in which to discuss three special topics arising out of the foregoing material: two-sided inverses, what we call ‘indigenous’ inverses, and the status of inverses in systems of tense logic (temporal logic).

6.1 Two-Sided Inverses

We recall from Section 1 that a two-sided inverse for □□ is an operator ○\bigcirc with ○□φ\bigcirc□\varphi, φ\varphi, and □○φ□\bigcirc\varphi all provably equivalent in the logic under discussion. To avoid new notation, we write ‘LL’ for ‘○\bigcirc’ (though we could equally suitably have written ‘RR’). We assume in addition only that LL is congruential. Thus we are considering the congruential logics extending 𝐒(𝐋){𝐒}({𝐋}) by the addition of all instances of the right-inverse schema (for LL): □Lφ↔φ□ L\varphi\leftrightarrow\varphi. For a given monomodal 𝐒{𝐒}, we refer to the least such extension of 𝐒(𝐋){𝐒}({𝐋}) as two-sided 𝐒(𝐋){𝐒}({𝐋}).

Lemma 6.1. If two-sided 𝐒(𝐋){𝐒}({𝐋}) extends (normal monomodal) 𝐒{𝐒} conservatively then 𝐒⊇𝐊𝐃!{𝐒}\supseteq {𝐊}{𝐃}!.

Proof. It suffices to show that □p↔◇p∈𝐒(𝐋)□ p\leftrightarrow◇ p\in {𝐒}({𝐋}), since this is an LL-free formula. The forward (‘𝐃{𝐃}’) direction of this equivalence follows from the fact that LL is a right inverse of □□ (via the right inverse property □L⊥↔⊥□ L\bot\leftrightarrow\bot). Since LL is also a left inverse of □□, □□ is a right inverse of LL, so LL satisfies the 𝐃{𝐃} schema Lφ→MφL\varphi\to M\varphi. We can now show that the backward direction of the above equivalence is provable by consideration of the following sequence of formulas provable in two-sided 𝐒(𝐋){𝐒}({𝐋}):

(1) p→L□pp\to L□ p (LL is a left inverse)
(2) L□p→M□pL□ p\to M□ p (𝐃{𝐃} for LL)
(3) p→M□pp\to M□ p (1,2 truth-functional logic)
(4) ◇p→◇M□p◇ p\to◇ M□ p (3, normality of □□)
(5) ◇M□p→□p◇ M□ p\to□ p (LL is a right inverse)
(6) ◇p→□p◇ p\to□ p (4, 5 truth-functional logic) ■

Although we have assumed only congruentiality for LL in two-sided 𝐒(𝐋){𝐒}({𝐋}), in fact normality follows – so this logic turns out to be ‘two-sided 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋}’. For the proof we note in advance that two-sided 𝐒(𝐋){𝐒}({𝐋}) is always closed under Necessitation for LL and under the (Outer) Cancellation Rule (for □□). As to the first point: for φ∈𝐒(𝐋)\varphi\in {𝐒}({𝐋}) we have □φ∈𝐒(𝐋)□\varphi\in {𝐒}({𝐋}) since □□ is normal, making φ\varphi and □φ□\varphi provably equivalent, thereby (since LL is congruential), making LφL\varphi and L□φL□\varphi provably equivalent; but, as LL is a left inverse φ\varphi and L□φL□\varphi are equivalent. Thus LφL\varphi and φ\varphi are equivalent and since φ∈𝐒(𝐋)\varphi\in {𝐒}({𝐋}), we have Lφ∈𝐒(𝐋)L\varphi\in {𝐒}({𝐋}). Since we have here derived LφL\varphi from φ\varphi using only the resources of 𝐒(𝐋){𝐒}({𝐋}), it follows that not only 𝐒(𝐋){𝐒}({𝐋}) itself, but any □□-normal extension thereof, such as two-sided 𝐒(𝐋){𝐒}({𝐋}), is closed under Necessitation. We turn to the Cancellation Rule. For any normal monomodal 𝐒{𝐒}, we know from the proof of Lemma 6.1 that two-sided 𝐒(𝐋){𝐒}({𝐋}) contains all formulas of the form ◇φ→□φ◇\varphi\to□\varphi. Thus if □φ→□ψ□\varphi\to□\psi is provable so is ◇φ→□ψ◇\varphi\to□\psi and hence □¬φ∨□ψ□\neg\varphi\vee□\psi, and hence □(φ→ψ)□(\varphi\to\psi). We now apply Necessitation for LL, available by the argument just given, and then delete the ‘L□L□’ by the left inverse schema, concluding that φ→ψ\varphi\to\psi belongs to two-sided 𝐒(𝐋){𝐒}({𝐋}). So this logic is closed under the Cancellation Rule (taken here in the form given in Prop. 2.1(iii)).

Lemma 6.2. LL is normal in two-sided 𝐒(𝐋){𝐒}({𝐋}).

Proof. Suppose that
(1) (φ1∧…∧φm)→ψ(\varphi_1\wedge\ldots\wedge\varphi_m)\to\psi is provable in two-sided 𝐒(𝐋){𝐒}({𝐋}). We must show that (Lφ1∧…∧Lφm)→Lψ(L\varphi_1\wedge\ldots\wedge L\varphi_m)\to L\psi is provable. (2) (□Lφ1∧…∧□Lφm)→□Lψ(□ L\varphi_1\wedge\ldots\wedge□ L\varphi_m)\to□ L\psi (From 1, since LL is a right inverse)
(3) □(Lφ1∧…∧Lφm)→□Lψ□(L\varphi_1\wedge\ldots\wedge L\varphi_m)\to□ L\psi (From 2, by the normality of □□)

Thus the proof of Lemma 6.1 goes through, mutatis mutandis, to show that Lp↔MpLp\leftrightarrow Mp (= 𝐃{𝐃}! for LL) is provable in two-sided 𝐒(𝐋){𝐒}({𝐋}), for all 𝐒{𝐒}; so for example two-sided 𝐊(𝐋)⊇𝐊𝐃!𝐋𝐃!{𝐊}({𝐋})\supseteq {𝐊}{𝐃}!{𝐋}{𝐃}!.

Lemma 6.1 can be strengthened to give a necessary and sufficient condition for two-sided 𝐒(𝐋){𝐒}({𝐋}) to extend 𝐒{𝐒} conservatively; by the foregoing remarks, it also gives a necessary and sufficient condition for two-sided 𝐒𝐋{𝐒}{𝐋} to extend 𝐒{𝐒} conservatively.

6.2 Indigenous Inverses

In the proof of Prop. 4.4 (‘If’ direction) we had occasion to remark that “□k□^k is a congruential right inverse of □□ within 𝐒{𝐒}” , in saying which we are exploiting the observation (from Section 1) that “the notions [of left and right inverse] make precisely analogous sense for arbitrary primitive or defined modal or non-modal operators.” The term ‘indigenous’, here borrowed from its use in [4], in particular, seems apposite for this case. Thus when there is already, in the monomodal language, a context ψ(⋅)\psi(\cdot), such that for the result, ψ(φ)\psi(\varphi) of putting any formula φ\varphi into that context, we have the right inverse equivalence

□ψ(φ)↔φ□\psi(\varphi)\leftrightarrow\varphi

provable in (monomodal) 𝐒{𝐒}, then the context ψ(⋅)\psi(\cdot) is an indigenous right inverse (in 𝐒{𝐒}). Similarly, ψ(⋅)\psi(\cdot) is an indigenous left inverse in 𝐒{𝐒} if 𝐒{𝐒} proves the left inverse equivalence

ψ(□φ)↔φ\psi(□\varphi)\leftrightarrow\varphi

for all formulas φ\varphi. Here we have used the functional notation ‘ψ(⋅)\psi(\cdot)’ to denote a context, thought of as a formula with gaps in it; when these gaps are filled by φ\varphi, we get, as value of the function, the formula ψ(φ)\psi(\varphi). We can alternatively represent this function by singling out a propositional variable in ψ\psi – an ordinary formula with no ‘gaps’ – and regarding ψ(φ)\psi(\varphi) as the result of uniformly substituting φ\varphi for that variable throughout ψ\psi. Let us choose pp (=p0p_0) to play this gap-indicating role. Then in particular, taking φ\varphi as pp itself, the right inverse equivalence above becomes

(1)
□ψ↔p□\psi\leftrightarrow p

Similarly, the left inverse equivalence becomes, using the substitution notation introduced in Section 4:

(2)
⊢𝐒s□(ψ)↔p\vdash_{{𝐒}} s_□(\psi)\leftrightarrow p

6.3 Inverses in Tense Logic

Adding a single left or right inverse to a tense logic produces a 3-modal logic, in the terminology of Section 1, because the logic to be extended is already bimodal. However, as we shall see, when the extension is conservative, it is unnecessary: the inverse in question is indigenously definable. We view the tense logic to be extended as already the result of extending a normal monomodal logic 𝐒{𝐒}; its tense-logical extension (always, as it happens, conservative) is the logic we denote by 𝐒t{𝐒}_t: the smallest normal bimodal extension of 𝐒{𝐒} by all instances of the following schemata, in which the new operator is written (recalling Section 1) as ‘□−1□^{-1}’, with dual ‘◇−1◇^{-1}’: φ→□◇−1φ\varphi\to□◇^{-1}\varphi; φ→□−1◇φ\varphi\to□^{-1}◇\varphi. Such logics are called normal tense logics. The operator □−1□^{-1} is interpreted in Kripke models using the converse of the accessibility relation interpreting □□. The ‘tense’ terminology merely records the historical motivation for studying such logics, which arises from thinking of the points of a model as moments of time, and the accessibility relation as the earlier than relation. (Then □□ and □−1□^{-1} emerge as means of simulating certain future and past tense constructions, respectively.) There is no suggestion of any preoccupation with such temporal interpretations in describing 𝐒t{𝐒}_t as the tense-logical extension of 𝐒{𝐒}. Before turning to the question of inverses, we remark on the considerable increase of expressive power attendant on the passage from the language of 𝐒{𝐒} to that of 𝐒t{𝐒}_t. In particular, there are formulas of the latter language which directly express the Predecessor and Successor Conditions of Sections 3 and 5 above, namely (1) and (2) respectively – that is, which modally define (in the sense of [2]) the classes of frames satisfying these conditions – and which are such that the canonical models of normal tense logics in which these formulas (and therefore all their substitution instances) are provable meet the respective conditions:

(1)
p→◇−1□pp\to◇^{-1}□ p
(2)
p→◇□−1pp\to◇□^{-1}p

(Note that when we refer to the Predecessor and Successor Conditions, we have in mind those conditions as stated for the relation RR interpreting □□, not the relation R−1R^{-1} interpreting □−1□^{-1}.) It follows from this that, by contrast with the monomodal case, the canonical model for any logic determined by a class of models satisfying the Successor Condition itself satisfies that condition.

The crucial observation for what follows is that in every normal tense logic, the following is provable:

(3)
□p↔□◇−1□p□ p\leftrightarrow□◇^{-1}□ p

Now applying the rule of Outer Cancellation (for □□) to (3) would yield a biconditional form of (1), and hence (1) itself. (The converse conditional belongs to every normal tense logic.) And applying the rule of Inner Cancellation (for □□) to (3) yields the formula p↔□◇−1pp\leftrightarrow□◇^{-1}p, which (in every normal tense logic) yields (2). Thus since neither (1) nor (2) is provable in 𝐊t{𝐊}_t, the smallest normal tense logic, while (3) is, that logic is closed under neither Outer nor Inner Cancellation – a contrast with the smallest normal monomodal logic, 𝐊{𝐊}, which is closed the former rule. (Reformulating the point about Inner Cancellation: in 𝐊t{𝐊}_t, unlike 𝐊{𝐊}, □□ is special, since all □□-formulas, but not all formulas, are provably equivalent to the results of prefixing them with ‘□◇−1□◇^{-1}’.) To bring these considerations to bear on the topic of inverses, we adapt our earlier notation in the following way: 𝐒t(𝐋){𝐒}_t({𝐋}) and St(𝐑)S_t({𝐑}) are the extensions of the normal tense logic 𝐒t{𝐒}_t by the addition of, respectively, a congruential left inverse for □□ and a congruential right inverse for □□. As foreshadowed above, the extensions are unnecessary whenever they are conservative:

Proposition 6.12. The following are equivalent for any normal tense logic 𝐒t{𝐒}_t:

(i)
𝐒t(𝐋){𝐒}_t({𝐋}) is a conservative extension of 𝐒t{𝐒}_t.
(ii)
𝐒t{𝐒}_t provides an indigenous left inverse for □□.
(iii)
𝐒t{𝐒}_t is closed under the Outer Cancellation Rule for □□.

Proof. (i) ⇒\Rightarrow (iii): As in the monomodal case (Section 1); (iii) ⇒\Rightarrow (ii): deriving the biconditional form of (1) from (3), as above, gives ◇−1◇^{-1} as an indigenous left inverse; (ii) ⇒\Rightarrow (i): obvious. ■

A similar proof, using the derivation of (2) from (3) above, establishes:

Proposition 6.13. The following are equivalent for any normal tense logic 𝐒t{𝐒}_t:

(i)
𝐒t(𝐑){𝐒}_t({𝐑}) is a conservative extension of 𝐒t{𝐒}_t.
(ii)
𝐒t{𝐒}_t provides an indigenous right inverse for □□.
(iii)
𝐒t{𝐒}_t is closed under the Inner Cancellation Rule for □□.

Since, although the left and right inverses which the proofs of Props. 6.12, 6.13, deliver are not normal, we can say more than simply that they are congruential: they are the duals of normal modal operators (viz. □−1□^{-1}, in both cases). Rather than directly discussing the case of normal inverse extensions of a tense logic 𝐒t{𝐒}_t, the logics 𝐒t𝐋{𝐒}_t{𝐋} and 𝐒t𝐑{𝐒}_t{𝐑}, we close with an observation concerning something such normality makes possible: that the inverses LL and RR themselves have tense-logical ‘companions’, indicated by a superscripted ‘⇔1\Leftrightarrow1’, in the sense that we have φ→LM−1φ\varphi\to LM^{-1}\varphi; φ→L−1Mφ\varphi\to L^{-1}M\varphi, and similarly with R,SR,S, for the right-hand case. Call these normal 4-modal extensions of 𝐒t{𝐒}_t: 𝐒t𝐋t{𝐒}_t{𝐋}_t and 𝐒t𝐑t{𝐒}_t{𝐑}_t.

Proposition 6.14. For any normal tense logic 𝐒t{𝐒}_t, R−1R^{-1} is a left inverse of □−1□^{-1} in 𝐒t𝐑t{𝐒}_t{𝐑}_t; L−1L^{-1} is a right inverse of □−1□^{-1} in 𝐒t𝐋t{𝐒}_t{𝐋}_t

Proof. We prove the first assertion, the second being similar. ‘⊢\vdash’ indicates provability in 𝐊t𝐑t{𝐊}_t{𝐑}_t, and hence in 𝐒t𝐑t{𝐒}_t{𝐑}_t for any normal 𝐒t{𝐒}_t. We need to show ⊢R−1□−1φ↔φ\vdash R^{-1}□^{-1}\varphi\leftrightarrow\varphi (for arbitrary φ\varphi). Beginning with the fact that ⊢φ→□Rφ\vdash\varphi\to□ R\varphi, we proceed through the following tense-logically standard steps:

(1)
⊢◇−1φ→Rφ\vdash◇^{-1}\varphi\to R\varphi
(2)
⊢S−1◇−1φ→S−1Rφ\vdash S^{-1}◇^{-1}\varphi\to S^{-1}R\varphi
(3)
⊢S−1◇−1φ→φ\vdash S^{-1}◇^{-1}\varphi\to\varphi
(4)
⊢φ→R−1□−1φ\vdash\varphi\to R^{-1}□^{-1}\varphi

Note that the transition from (1) to (2) exploits the normality of R−1R^{-1} (via the definition S−1=¬R−1¬S^{-1}=\neg R^{-1}\neg).

For the converse, we begin with that fact that ⊢□Rφ→φ\vdash□ R\varphi\to\varphi, and putting φ=S−1◇−1ψ\varphi=S^{-1}◇^{-1}\psi, we proceed:

(5)
⊢□RS−1◇−1ψ→S−1◇−1ψ\vdash□ RS^{-1}◇^{-1}\psi\to S^{-1}◇^{-1}\psi
(6)
⊢□◇−1ψ→S−1◇−1ψ\vdash□◇^{-1}\psi\to S^{-1}◇^{-1}\psi
(7)
⊢ψ→S−1◇−1ψ\vdash\psi\to S^{-1}◇^{-1}\psi
(8)
⊢R−1□−1φ→φ\vdash R^{-1}□^{-1}\varphi\to\varphi.

The desired result then follows from (4) and (8). ■

Thus in the case of the normal inverse extensions of 𝐊t{𝐊}_t there is no loss incurred by not separately recording in the labels the existence of inverses for the operator □−1□^{-1}. For the merely congruential extensions, a more detailed investigation may profit from greater explicitness in this regard, with a labelling recording separately the existence of right or left inverses not only □□ for but also for □−1□^{-1}, by contrast with that employed above, in which the ‘(𝐑{𝐑})’ or ‘(𝐋{𝐋})’ in the labels address only the provision of inverses for the former operator. We have contented ourselves in the above remarks only with conveying the flavour of tense-logical inverses and touching on some points of contrast with the earlier monomodal case.
