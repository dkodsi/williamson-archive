# Review of R.B. Marcus, Modalities. Philosophical Essays

Timothy Williamson

Philosophical Books 36 (2) (1995): 120–122.

The first systematic modern studies of quantified modal logic (QML) appeared in 1946: Carnap’s ‘Modalities and Quantification’ and ‘A Functional Calculus of First Order Based on Strict Implication’ by Ruth Barcan (as she then was). The following year, she published the first proof of the necessity of identity. QML was not universally welcomed. Its birth followed Quine’s early attempts to deny its right to life; he argued that quantification into modal contexts cannot be coherently interpreted because they are referentially opaque. Church and Smullyan soon pointed out the fallacy in Quine’s argument; its use of definite descriptions violated the scope distinctions emphasised in Russell’s theory of descriptions. Nevertheless, suspicion continued to hang over modal logic, especially QML (‘modal’ was fashionably pronounced to rhyme with ‘puddle’). The focus of Quine’s attack gradually shifted to the charge that QML commits one to obscurantist essentialism. Marcus replied that the commitment is to the intelligibility of non-trivial essentialist claims, not their truth, that they are intuitively intelligible, and that physical science sometimes gives us evidence for their truth (for causal modalities and properties essential to all members of a kind). Her reply, though forceful, challenged a deep-rooted conception of all necessity as verbal necessity. Only after Kripke and Putnam’s comprehensive attack on that conception and the former’s results in formal semantics did QML gain general acceptance.

Although the present collection omits Marcus’s early papers, it contains much philosophical reflection on their ideas. Deontic and doxastic modalities are also discussed; Marcus is sceptical about alleged inconsistencies in our moral principles or beliefs. There are fifteen papers, originally published between 1961 and 1990, two technical notes and the text of a 1962 debate between Marcus, Quine, Kripke, Føllesdal and McCarthy.

Marcus often anticipates later work. As early as 1962, she treated proper names as mere tags. This is not just to treat them as rigid designators, for such definite descriptions as ‘The square of 5’ and ‘The actual pope’ are rigid designators but not tags. Rather, it anticipates the more specific notion of direct reference.

Only one of the book’s many rewarding strands will be followed here. A theorem of its author’s systems is the Barcan formula (BF) $◇\exists x\,\phi(x) \supset \exists x\,◇\phi(x)$ (for example, ‘If there could have been something divine, then there is something that could have been divine’). The converse (CBF) is also a theorem. On Kripke’s semantics, where each possible world has its own domain of quantification, BF is invalid unless one stipulates that, whenever a world $x$ is accessible from a world $w$, the domain of $w$ includes the domain of $x$. CBF is invalid unless one stipulates the converse inclusion. Together, the two formulas require a constant domain (as each does separately if accessibility is symmetric).

Marcus rejects variation between domains on the grounds that it amounts to the admission of (mere) possibilia; she argues that we cannot refer to possibilia. Kripke’s semantics with varying domains is indeed unsatisfying, for it treats quantifiers in the object-language but not in the meta-language as worldbound. If the domain of some world contains an element $i$ not in the domain of the actual world, then the truth of $\forall x\,\phi(x)$ requires $i$ to satisfy $\phi(x)$ if the quantifier belongs to the meta-language but not if it belongs to the object-language. Why this discriminatory policy? After all, the meta-language can itself be treated as an object-language. However, merely to stipulate a constant domain is equally unsatisfying, for it deprives the formalism of its intuitive interpretation. We incline to believe that, although Queen Victoria and Cavour could have had a child, nothing that actually exists could have been their child. Given these beliefs, it is BF that commits us to possibilia, for instance, non-existent possible children. At least the following responses merit consideration:

(i) One rejects possibilia, BF and CBF, blaming the problem on the attempt to give the semantics of a modal object-language in a non-modal meta-language. In a modal meta-language (as used by Peacocke and others), one can describe putative counter-examples to BF without invoking possibilia. A non-modal meta-language is mathematically convenient but philosophically misleading.

(ii) One accepts possibilia, BF and CBF, holding that we can refer to possibilia. Marcus’s argument to the contrary assumes that we can refer only to what we have empirically encountered (p. 207). One might prefer Kripke’s suggestion that we can use a definite description to fix the reference of a proper name, and invoke descriptions such as ‘what would have resulted from the union of sperm $s$ and egg $e$’. The name may still be directly referential, for the description does not give its meaning.

(iii) One accepts possibilia, BF and CBF, holding that, although we cannot refer to possibilia individually, we can quantify over them.

Both (ii) and (iii) involve a non-substitutional view of quantification: (iii) obviously, (ii) because a substitutional treatment of the quantification implicit in the description requires independent reference to the designated item. In several of the papers Marcus defends a substitutional account of quantification (this may have been an obstacle to acceptance of her work). Most recently, however, she argues that non-substitutional quantification is also required (p. 213), in which case (ii) and (iii) cannot be ruled out on substitutional grounds. We seem to quantify over space-time points without the physical possibility of encountering individual points empirically (at best we encounter very small regions); why should possibilia be different?

Of course, one can quantify substitutionally if one so wishes. As Copeland has pointed out, BF and CBF will then be valid only if the class of substituted terms is constant from world to world. Moreover, quantification just over what we happen to have named is of little interest. Marcus treats the substitutional account as easily extended to quantification over everything that we could name (pp. 119–124), but does not explain how to solve the implicit problems. For example, we should like to evaluate ‘Some star is unnamed’ as true. An actually unnamed star could have been named ‘S’, but to make ‘S is unnamed’ true the semantics must evaluate it under its merely possible meaning at the actual world. How is this to be done without possibilia in a way satisfactory for the nominalist to whom Marcus is recommending substitutional quantification? Again, if there are nondenumerably many objects, we may be able to name any of them, but cannot name all of them. Marcus replies that a first-order theory (interpreted non-substitutionally) with a nondenumerable model has a denumerable model (p. 124). She does not argue (for example, in Putnam’s style) that there is no sense in which the former might be the intended model and the latter unintended. Moreover, the implicitly invoked Löwenheim–Skolem theorem does not apply to second-order theories.

Although the book makes few concessions to the non-technical reader, it is of far more than technical significance. Marcus has participated in a critical turn of twentieth-century philosophy: not the linguistic turn, but that later turn by which a rigorous study of natural and artificial language uncovered a space of non-linguistic questions, answerable by logically disciplined metaphysics. To understand this turn one must understand Marcus’s achievement, and its temporary neglect.
