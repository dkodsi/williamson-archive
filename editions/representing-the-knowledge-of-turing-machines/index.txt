# Representing the knowledge of Turing machines

Hyun Song Shin and Timothy Williamson

Theory and Decision 37 (1994): 125–146.

Abstract

When each state of the world is a maximally specific, consistent description of the world, including the description of the knowledge and ignorance of all individuals, the standard partitional model of knowledge is inconsistent with the assumption that an individual’s powers are limited to that of a Turing machine. We show, however, that the epistemic logic $S4$ is consistent with computational constraints.

Keywords: computability, knowledge.

# 1. Introduction

In this paper, we shall follow the implications of the following pair of principles.

(i)
The state space should be construed as the set of all maximally specific descriptions of the world, including a complete specification of the knowledge (and ignorance) of all individuals.
(ii)
An individual has exactly the reasoning ability of some computing machine.

Each of these principles, taken in isolation, have been enormously influential in decision theory and game theory, but relatively little attention has been devoted to the examination of the conjunction of these statements. Principle (i) has its historical roots in the theory of probability and in the identification of the state space with the sample space associated with a random variable, and in the logician’s idea of a ‘possible world’ as a maximally specific and consistent truth assignment to a set of sentences. Savage (1954) and Jeffrey (1983), among others, have developed this principle in decision theory, while Harsanyi (1967) has popularized this notion for the study of games. Bayesian game theory has now become one of the most important analytical tools in the economics of information, and has generated a huge literature. The work of Aumann (1976, 1987) is worthy of particular mention, and the reader is referred to the recent symposium and survey in the Journal of Economic Perspectives (Brandenburger 1992, Geanakoplos 1992, and Reny 1992).

The assertion that decision makers should be endowed with the abilities of a computing machine (and no more) can be seen as a response to the lacuna in conventional treatments of decision making which neglects the procedural aspects of rationality. This distinction between the substantive and procedural aspects of rationality as propounded, for example, by Simon (1976) takes on particular significance in game theory owing to the additional dimension of the problem posed by iterated knowledge, i.e., knowledge of what others know or don’t know. Binmore (1987) argues that the standard assumption in game theory that all players are “perfectly rational” becomes problematic when players are seen as computing machines. A related line of research, with similar methodological overtones, has been to examine the outcomes in specific games played between computing machines, both finite automata (as in Rubinstein 1986, and Abreu and Rubinstein 1988) and general Turing machines (as in Anderlini 1989, and Canning 1992). The motivation of these papers is to examine the extent to which the results of conventional game theory survive explicit incorporation of procedural rationality. In the case of the literature on finite automata, an additional motive is to explore the effects of bounded rationality on the outcomes of specific games.

In following up the consequences of the conjunction of principles (i) and (ii), we shall employ a framework which is as close as possible to the standard treatments of information and knowledge while being able to accommodate sufficient structure to formalize the notion that individuals are Turing machines. For a description of alternative approaches to modelling knowledge and of the current state of the art, the reader is referred to Lismont and Mongin (1994). In our paper, we start with a state space $W$ each state of which describes the environment in which the individuals find themselves. In the case of Bayesian games, each state $w$ will specify the game being played at that state. Since individuals’ knowledge depend on the environment, the knowledge of any individual will vary from state to state. More precisely, for each individual $i$, we associate with each state $w$ a set of formulas, interpreted as those which $i$ knows – that is, $i$’s theory at $w$.

We require that each individual’s theory include a version of arithmetic. In our case, the version of arithmetic employed is the theory $Q$, described in Boolos and Jeffrey (1980, p. 158). This requirement is permissible because the theorems of $Q$ can be generated by a Turing machine. We do not want the reasoning abilities of individuals to be more limited than those of such a machine. The theory $Q$ is a weaker theory than Peano Arithmetic, but has been shown to be sufficient to carry through the main results of Gödel (1931) and Rosser (1936). In particular, any consistent recursively axiomatized extension of $Q$ is susceptible to the Gödel incompleteness theorems. We shall make essential use of this result ourselves.

In outline, our approach may be sketched as follows. The set of formulas Form in our framework is built up from two disjoint sets of atomic sentences. The first is a set of non-arithmetical sentences $\Pi'$, describing the environment in which the individuals find themselves. The second is a set of sentences $\Pi$ in the language of arithmetic. Thus, a typical formula in Form will be a complex formula built up from arithmetical and nonarithmetical components, which are combined by means of logical connectives and the knowledge operators. The first question we address is whether the knowledge of these individuals can be represented in terms of the standard partitional information structures. In other words, whether we can define an equivalence relation of indistinguishability between states so that it is true that an individual knows $A$ at $w$ if and only if $A$ is true at every state which is indistinguishable from $w$. Our first result is the negative one that, even if the knowledge of non-arithmetical formulas is governed by a partitional structure, the inclusion of arithmetical knowledge precludes a partitional structure over the knowledge of the set of all formulas. This is accomplished by demonstrating that the principle:

(5)
$\neg KA \rightarrow K\neg KA$

cannot hold for all formulas, $A$, even though (5) holds for all purely non-arithmetical formulas. This result rests on the observation that (5) implies (under soundness) that the set of formulas known by an individual at a state is not only recursively enumerable, but also recursive, thereby contradicting Rosser’s (1936) version of Gödel’s first incompleteness theorem. We will have a chance to review these concepts below.

Having established this negative result, we embark on our positive programme of seeing which principles of knowledge can be held consistently. The principle:

(4)
$KA \rightarrow KKA$

is shown to be consistent with computability in our framework. More importantly, our framework also satisfies the key principle:

(T)
$KA \rightarrow A$.

This principle would seem to be an essential feature of knowledge, and yet, if the provability predicate of arithmetic is interpreted as the knowledge operator, (T) cannot hold in general, on pain of inconsistency. Boolos (1979) provides a detailed account of the modal logic of provability, and Binmore and Shin (1992) note some of the consequences of these results for game theory. In the following section, we rehearse some of the main results in this area.

We should state at the outset that our purpose is not define knowledge in terms of any specific logical system, but rather to show how an $S4$ logic of knowledge is consistent with computational constraints (and how $S5$ is not). Thus, the principle (T) is, in the first instance, an assumption which is then subsequently shown to be consistent with computational constraints. We do not believe that there is a unique “correct” system of logic for Turing machines which is waiting to be derived from first principles. There will be many logics which are consistent with machine knowledge, and the choice between them will be made on other grounds.

In short, our claim in this paper is twofold. Firstly, an $S4$ logic of knowledge is consistent with the limitations imposed by the fact that individuals are Turing machines, but secondly, the standard partitional representation of knowledge, i.e., the logic $S5$, is inconsistent with machine knowledge. We do not go much beyond making these two observations. Our motivation is to construct a model of knowledge for Turing machines which is as close as possible to the standard representation in economics and decision theory in terms of partitions over the state space. In this respect, our first (negative) result seems to be one which is insensitive to the details of the particular construction of the system.

Our result that the knowledge of Turing machines can satisfy $S4$ may have some bearing on decision theory, in particular on the idea of coherent belief revision in multiperiod settings. At a basic level, coherent belief change could be described as one which precludes the existence of “Dutch Books” against the decision maker. Although partitional information and Bayes updating from the true prior is sufficient for coherent belief revision, the results of Morris (1992) suggest that coherence can be maintained for more general information structures, provided that beliefs follow specified updating rules. In particular, $S4$ is shown to be adequate, provided that updated beliefs satisfy additional restrictions. The details of such arguments are beyond the scope of this paper, and we do not attempt to link our discussion to that of belief coherence. Indeed, we are keenly aware that the extension of our framework to settings which involve probabilistic beliefs is an important and potentially fruitful exercise.

Before proceeding with our construction, we sketch some of the background to our discussion by reviewing some earlier work in this area, and by stating some methodological preliminaries.

# 2. Background and Preliminaries

In this paper, it will be assumed without argument that a notion of knowledge can be applied to machines. We assume a fixed formal language, and treat the machine as knowing the truth of a formula $A$ in it if and only if the machine eventually outputs “Yes” on input “$A$?”. It may be more accurate to view the output of “Yes” on input “$A$?” as the defining property of the machine’s belief of $A$ rather than its knowledge, but we will not dwell on this distinction. Since our framework preserves the principle $KA \rightarrow A$, no substantial issue will turn on the distinction between knowledge and belief.

What the machine knows will be considered as a formal system, with a recursively enumerable set of theorems. The machine’s knowledge is allowed to vary with the state of the world. Thus, each machine in each state of the world is associated with a formal system, whose theorems are what it knows in that state. The machine has some knowledge of its environment, which is incorporated in the axioms of its formal system. Thus a given machine is associated with different formal systems in different states of the world between which its knowledge of the environment differs. It will be assumed that the machines are numerate; in each state each machine’s formal system includes a basic system for arithmetic, $Q$ (Boolos and Jeffrey 1980, p. 158). Moreover, it will be assumed that all the systems include truth-functional logic: all tautologies and modus ponens.

Treating knowledge as provability in arithmetic must be handled with some care, in view of Gödel’s incompleteness theorem. Gödel (1931) showed how one can compute, for each formula $A$ in the language of arithmetic a numeral $\lceil A\rceil$ and define a predicate $\operatorname{Bew}(\cdot)$ in that language which expresses the provability of $A$. If $\operatorname{Bew}(\lceil\cdot\rceil)$ is interpreted as the knowledge operator $K$, then the properties of $K$ include that of the modal logic $G$. $G$ is a normal system whose theorems include:

(Taut)
All Truth-functional Tautologies

and all instances of:

(K)
$K(A \rightarrow B) \rightarrow (KA \rightarrow KB)$
(Löb)
$K(KA \rightarrow A) \rightarrow KA$

One can also show that, for any two formulas $A$ and $B$,

(MP)
If $A$ and $A \rightarrow B$ are theorems of $G$, then so is $B$.
(RN)
If $A$ in a theorem of $G$, then so is $KA$.

From this, all instances of the following are theorems, too.

(4)
$KA \rightarrow KKA$.

However, $G$ does not include all instances of the following, on pain of inconsistency.

(T)
$KA \rightarrow A$.

For let $\bot$ be any contradiction. If $K\bot \rightarrow \bot$ is a theorem, then so is $K(K\bot \rightarrow \bot)$ by (RN), so by (Löb) and (MP) $K\bot$, and hence $\bot$, are theorems. This point is closely related to Gödel’s incompleteness theorem, for $K\bot \rightarrow \bot$ is equivalent to $\neg K\bot$, a sentence expressing the consistency of the theory. No consistent extension of $Q$ can prove its own consistency.

There is a way in which one can repair this particular difficulty. One might define $KA$ not as “it is provable that $A$”, but as “it is provable and true that $A$”, i.e., formally by $\operatorname{Bew}(\lceil A\rceil) \wedge A$ rather than by $\operatorname{Bew}(\lceil A\rceil)$. This definition automatically secures (T). In this case the theory also includes the following class of (not very intuitive) formulas.

(Grz)
$K(K(A \rightarrow KA) \rightarrow A) \rightarrow A$.

One also retains (K) and (RN). The system is axiomatized by (Taut), (K), (Grz), (MP) and (RN) is known as $S4\mathrm{Grz}$ (see Boolos 1979, pp. 159–167). All instances of (T) and (4) are derivable in it (van Benthem and Blok 1978).

However, this repair does not go to the heart of the problem. First, it makes $KA$ express a condition one half of which (the truth of $A$) is independent of the activity of the machine. Second, in reasoning about its knowledge of the environment, a machine often needs to know that it is ignorant about some matter, e.g., that it does not know whether the coin will come up heads or tails at the next toss. Such ignorance is formalized as $\neg KA \wedge \neg K\neg A$. But if this is a theorem, so that the machine is aware of its ignorance, $K(\neg KA \wedge \neg K\neg A)$ is a theorem by (RN) and so is $K\neg KA \wedge K\neg K\neg A$. However, $S4\mathrm{Grz}$ contains the following class of formulas.

(M)
$K\neg K\neg A \rightarrow \neg K\neg KA$.

If one knows that one doesn’t know that $A$ is false, then one doesn’t know that one doesn’t know that $A$ is true; thus one cannot know that one doesn’t know whether $A$ is true. That is, $K(\neg KA \wedge \neg K\neg A)$ is inconsistent in $S4\mathrm{Grz}$. One can prove this by using the fact that the theorems of $S4\mathrm{Grz}$ are exactly the formulas valid on all finite weak partial orderings, i.e., the accessibility relation is reflexive, transitive, and antisymmetric (Boolos 1979, p. 161). In such orderings, each world can access an end-point, i.e., a world that accesses only itself. Since $\neg KA \wedge \neg K\neg A$ cannot be true at an end point (it is true only at worlds that can access at least two worlds), $K(\neg KA \wedge \neg K\neg A)$ is not true at any world. The problem is even worse in the original system $G$, for there if $\neg KA$, and $KA \rightarrow A$, hence $K(KA \rightarrow A)$ is a theorem, then $KA$ must be a theorem by (Löb), so that the system is inconsistent.

These problems require a fresh approach. It is intolerable to suppose that the numeracy of the machine prevents it from having knowledge of its own ignorance even in simple empirical situations. Our point of departure is to reinterpret the knowledge operator. The reinterpretation is quite subtle, for we retain the assumption that what an individual knows is precisely what is provable in that individual’s theory, where the axioms of that theory may include empirical information gathered from the environment about the state of the world. To abandon this assumption would be to refuse to take the computational constraint seriously. The assumption may appear to leave us no room for manoeuvre. However, interpretations of a knowledge operator can differ simply in the way they specify the knower. Although this difference seems trivial, it becomes crucial once the operator is iterated. To see this, consider the predicament of Oedipus, who is the son of Jocasta without knowing it. One might think that it makes no difference whether one interprets $K$ as ‘Oedipus knows’ or ‘the son of Jocasta knows’. If Oedipus knows that it is hot, and knows that he, Oedipus, knows that it is hot, it follows that the son of Jocasta knows that it is hot, but it does not follow that the son of Jocasta knows that the son of Jocasta knows that it is hot. In fact, the son of Jocasta does not even know that she has a son. Thus, $KKA$ is true under one interpretation of $K$ and false under another. Turing machines are susceptible to a related phenomenon. Each type of machine is associated with a description $\#$, listing outputs for each input and machine state. The Gödelian interpretation of $KA$ as $\operatorname{Bew}(\lceil A\rceil)$ interprets it as something like ‘The machine with description $\#$ knows’. But it cannot be assumed that the machine with description $\#$ knows that it is the machine with description $\#$. If you ask it ‘Are you the machine with description $\#$?’, it may not answer ‘Yes’. Machines of this kind are studied in Benacerraf (1967) and Reinhardt (1986). It follows that the Gödelian interpretation of $K$ is not the one best suited to decision theory. Consider again the formula $K(\neg KA \wedge \neg K\neg A)$. We want it to express the agent’s knowledge that he does not know whether $A$, knowledge on which he might act, e.g. by seeking more information. On the Gödelian interpretation, the formula says that the machine with description $\#$ knows that the machine with description $\#$ does not know whether $A$. But if it does not know that it itself is the machine with description $\#$, it may not know that it itself does not know whether $A$, and may therefore do nothing. To avoid this problem, we must interpret the knowledge operator with a specification of the machine under which the machine can recognize itself, just as Oedipus knows that he is Oedipus. We shall proceed on this understanding. Since our interpretation of $K$ is not Gödelian, there is no reason to think that $K$ must obey the principles of the system $G$. We now proceed to investigate some constraints on $K$.

# 3. The Framework and a Negative Result

Our construction is motivated by our goal of providing an account of the knowledge of a Turing machine which is as close as possible to the familiar partitional model of knowledge. We can think of our Turing machine as one which has access to empirical and factual knowledge, as well as to the theorems of arithmetic. Thus, the set of formulas over which the Turing machine has knowledge is of necessity a hybrid set consisting of arithmetical and non-arithmetical sentences, and complex formulas which combine both types by means of logical connectives and the knowledge operator. For our purposes, the extra structure offered by sentences of arithmetic will play no semantic role. Well formed formulas of arithmetic will be regarded as atomic propositions in our logic. However, the structure of arithmetic will play the key role of forcing the machine’s theory to be recursively enumerable, but not recursive. It is time that we reviewed some of these basic concepts.

First, recall the definitions of recursive and recursively enumerable sets. Informally, a set $X$ of natural numbers is recursive if there is a Turing computable procedure that on input $n$ gives output 1 if $n \in X$ and output 0 if $n \notin X$. The set $x$ is recursively enumerable if there is a Turing computable procedure that on input $n$ gives output 1 if $n \in X$, and either gives output 0 or never terminates when $n \notin X$. Note that $X$ is recursive if and only if it and its complement are recursively enumerable: a one-way positive test and a one-way negative test are jointly equivalent to a two-way test. We can extend these notions from natural numbers to formulas via the standard procedure for coding the latter by the former. A theory is said to be recursively axiomatized if the set of its axioms is recursive, i.e., it is computable whether or not something is an axiom. Now, if a theory is recursively axiomatized, the set of its theorems is recursively enumerable, because any theorem can be recognized as such via inspection of its proof. But the set of theorems need not be recursive, for there may be no algorithm for recognizing every non-theorem as such. Precisely this is the case with arithmetic.

With these preliminaries, we now proceed to describe our framework. For clarity, our construction will proceed in three steps. First, we define the basis for the empirical or factual knowledge of our individuals in terms of standard possible world semantics and partitional information structures. Next, we endow individuals with arithmetical knowledge by assuming that they know (at every state of the world) all the theorems of a version of arithmetic. Finally, we combine the two sources of knowledge, and construct the set of formulas known by an individual at each state of the world.

Step 1. Let $W$ be a finite set of states of the world. Let $I$ be a set of individuals. For each $i \in I$, there is an equivalence relation $R_i$ on $W$. These equivalence relations will define the respective information partition of each individual. Let $\Pi'$ be a set of atomic sentences which make empirical or factual claims about the world. For $w \in W$ let $\pi'(w)$ be the set of formulas in $\Pi'$ true at $w$. For each individual $i \in I$, let $K_i$ be the knowledge operator associated with $i$. We denote by

$\mathit{Form}(\Pi')$

the set of formulas generated by $\Pi'$, the logical connectives $\{\wedge,\neg\}$, and the knowledge operators $\{K_i\}$. The truth of formulas in $\mathit{Form}(\Pi')$ will be governed by standard possible world semantics. We thus define the relation $\ast$ between members of $W$ and formulas, where $w\ast A$ means that $A$ is true at $w$.

$w\ast A \Leftrightarrow A \in \pi'(w)$

$w\ast \neg A \Leftrightarrow \text{not }w\ast A$

$w\ast A \wedge B \Leftrightarrow w\text{*} A\text{ and }w\ast B$

$w\ast K_iA \Leftrightarrow \forall w'\in W,\,(wR_iw' \Rightarrow w'\ast A)$

Finally, we denote by $\Phi_i(w)$ the set of formulas in $\mathit{Form}(\Pi')$ known by $i$ at $w$. That is,

$\Phi_i(w)=\{A\in\mathit{Form}(\Pi')\mid w\ast K_iA\}$.

Step 2. Let $\Pi$ be a theory in the language of arithmetic which is recursively axiomatized. In particular, we will require that $\Pi$ contains the theory $Q$ mentioned above, and also that it be consistent. From Rosser’s extension of Gödel’s theorem, no consistent extension of $Q$ can be recursive. Thus, the pair of conditions above implies:

(A1)
$Q \subseteq \Pi$
(A2)
$\Pi$ is recursively enumerable, but not recursive.

We shall assume that every individual knows every element of $\Pi$ at every state $w \in W$.

Step 3. We now define the general set of Formulas $\mathit{Form}$ as follows. Begin by defining the set of Atomic Formulas $\mathit{Atom}$ as consisting of the union of the two disjoint sets $\Pi$ and $\Pi'$. Then, $\mathit{Form}$ is the set generated by $\mathit{Atom}$, the connectives $\{\wedge,\neg\}$ and the set of knowledge operators $\{K_i\}_{i\in I}$. The other logical connectives are defined in terms of $\{\wedge,\neg\}$ in the usual way. Also, we will use the notation:

$\mathit{At}(w)$

to denote the set of atomic formulas true at state $w$. All instances of the following are assumed to be known at every state.

(T)
$K_iA \rightarrow A$
(K)
$K_i(A \rightarrow B) \rightarrow (K_iA \rightarrow K_iB)$
(4)
$K_iA \rightarrow K_iK_iA$

Moreover, knowledge will be closed under the rules (MP) and (RN) to be defined below. For any subset $\Gamma \subseteq \mathit{Form}$, let:

$\operatorname{MP}(\Gamma)\equiv\{B\mid \exists A\,\in\Gamma\text{ and }A\rightarrow B\in\Gamma\}$

$\operatorname{RN}_i(\Gamma)\equiv\{K_iA\mid A\in\Gamma\}$

$\operatorname{RN}(\Gamma)\equiv\displaystyle\bigcup_{i\in I}\operatorname{RN}_i(\Gamma)$.

We can thus define a subset $\Sigma$ of formulas which are known at every state. $\Sigma$ is defined in terms of the following sequence $\Sigma_0,\Sigma_1,\Sigma_2,\ldots$.

$\Sigma_0=\Pi\cup\operatorname{Taut}\cup(\mathrm{K})\cup(\mathrm{T})\cup(4)$

$\Sigma_{n+1}=\Sigma_n\cup\operatorname{MP}(\Sigma_n)\cup\operatorname{RN}(\Sigma_n)$

$\Sigma=\displaystyle\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\Sigma_n$

Now, by bringing together the set $\Sigma$ and the set $\Phi_i(w)$, we can define a set of formulas $\Delta_i(w)$ which are known by the Turing machine $i$ at state $w$. $\Delta_i(w)$ will be closed under (MP) and $(\operatorname{RN}_i)$, but it need not be closed under $(\operatorname{RN}_j)$, for $j\neq i$. Formally, $\Delta_i(w)$ is defined as:

$\Delta_{i,0}(w)=\Sigma\cup\Phi_i(w)$

$\Delta_{i,n+1}(w)=\Delta_{i,n}(w)\cup\operatorname{MP}(\Delta_{i,n}(w))\cup\operatorname{RN}_i(\Delta_{i,n}(w))$

$\Delta_i(w)=\displaystyle\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\Delta_{i,n}(w)$.

This completes the description of our system. We shall now investigate the properties of the sets $\Delta_i(w)$. We begin by verifying that, under suitable conditions, these sets are recursively enumerable and so capable of being generated by Turing machines.

PROPOSITION 1. If $W$, $I$ and $\Pi'$ are finite then $\Delta_i(w)$ is recursively enumerable, for all $i\in I$ and $w\in W$.

Proof. First note that $\ast$ as restricted to $W\times\mathit{Form}(\Pi')$ is a recursive relation, for it is definable in terms of the function $\pi'(\cdot)$ from the finite set $W$ to subsets of the finite set $\Pi'$, negation, conjunction, quantification over the finite set $W$, and the finite family $\{R_i\}$ of finite sets of order pairs. Thus, for $i\in I$ and $w\in W$, $\Phi_i(w)$ is also recursive, for it is defined by recursive means in terms of $\ast$. Moreover, since $\Pi$ is recursively enumerable, $\Sigma$ is recursively enumerable by construction. Hence, $\Delta_i(w)$ is recursively enumerable by construction. □

We can now state our first substantial result. It is a negative result concerning the limitations on the knowledge of Turing machines in our framework. The truth of a formula $A$ in the state $w$ is written formally as:

$w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; A$,

and defined recursively as follows.

$w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; P \Leftrightarrow P\in\mathit{At}(w)$

$w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;\neg A \Leftrightarrow \text{not }w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; A$

$w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; A\wedge B \Leftrightarrow w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; A\text{ and }w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; B$

$w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; K_i(A) \Leftrightarrow A\in\Delta_i(w)$

Let us say that the state $w$ is $i$-sound if in it everything that machine $i$ can prove is true. That is, $w$ is $i$-sound if $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; A$, for all $A\in\Delta_i(w)$.

The $i$-soundness of the state $w$ is equivalent to the statement that every formula of the form $K_iA\rightarrow A$ is true at $w$. We have yet to verify this statement (it will be the subject of the next section). Subject to $i$-soundness, we can prove that the information of the Turing machines cannot be partitional. Consider the following set of formulas.

(5$i$)
$\neg K_iA\rightarrow K_i\neg K_iA$

According to $(5i)$, if machine $i$ doesn’t know something, it knows that it doesn’t know it. $(5i)$ would be true if the states of the world were partitioned by the relation of indiscriminability by $i$, and $i$ knows just those proportions true in all states indiscriminable from the one it is in. For then, if it did not know $A$, $\neg K_iA$ would be true in every state indiscriminable from the one $i$ is in, so $K_i\neg K_iA$ would be true. However, $(5i)$ puts impossible demands on the computational capacity of a Turing machine. Even if $i$ doesn’t know $A$, it may be unable to discriminate a state in which it does know $A$ from the true state. Say that $(5i)$ is valid in $w$ if every instance of $(5i)$ is true at $w$.

PROPOSITION 2. If $w$ is $i$-sound, $(5i)$ is valid in $w$, and $\Delta_i(w)$ is recursively enumerable, then $\Delta_i(w)$ is recursive.

Proof. We first show that $\Delta_i(w)=\{A\in\mathit{Form}\mid \neg K_iA\notin\Delta_i(w)\}$. For if $A\in\Delta_i(w)$, then $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; K_iA$, so that not $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;\neg K_iA$. By hypothesis, $w$ is $i$-sound, so that $\neg K_iA\notin\Delta_i(w)$. Conversely, suppose that $\neg K_iA\notin\Delta_i(w)$. Since $(5i)$ is valid in $w$, we have $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;\neg K_iA\rightarrow K_i\neg K_iA$. Thus, either $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; K_iA$ or $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; K_i\neg K_iA$. The latter case implies $\neg K_iA\in\Delta_i(w)$, contrary to hypothesis. Thus, $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; K_iA$, so $A\in\Delta_i(w)$. Thus, $\Delta_i(w)$ is the complement of $\{A\mid \neg K_iA\in\Delta_i(w)\}$ in $\mathit{Form}$. But the latter set is recursively enumerable, for one can recursively enumerate it by writing down $A$ in the recursive enumeration of $\{A\mid\neg K_iA\in\Delta_i(w)\}$ if and when $\neg K_iA$ appears in the recursive enumeration of $\Delta_i(w)$. Since $\Delta_i(w)$ and its complement are recursively enumerable, $\Delta_i(w)$ is recursive. □

COROLLARY 1. If $w$ is $i$-sound, $(5i)\subseteq\Delta_i(w)$, and $\Delta_i(w)$ is recursively enumerable, then $\Delta_i(w)$ is recursive.

Proof. If $w$ is $i$-sound, $(5i)\subseteq\Delta_i(w)$, then $(5i)$ is valid in $w$, so Proposition 2 applies.

PROPOSITION 3. If $(5i)$ is valid in $w$, $Q\subseteq\Delta_i(w)$ and $\Delta_i(w)$ is recursively enumerable, then $w$ is not $i$-sound.

Proof. By Rosser’s (1936) strengthening of Gödel’s first incompleteness theorem, any recursive extension of $Q$ is inconsistent. The result then follows by Proposition 2. □

COROLLARY 2. If $(5i)\subseteq\Delta_i(w)$, $Q\subseteq\Delta_i(w)$, and $\Delta_i(w)$ is recursively enumerable, then $w$ is not $i$-sound.

Since the set of theorems generated by Turing machine $i$ is recursively enumerable, Proposition 3 shows that $(5i)$ makes a false claim about such a machine’s knowledge. The obvious corollary is that the machine itself cannot correctly make this claim.

# 4. The Positive Result

One of the key hypotheses in showing that Turing machines cannot have partitional knowledge was that every state $w$ is $i$-sound. In this section, we demonstrate that this is indeed the case, provided that the arithmetical theory used by the machines is correct. In so doing, we will be showing that every instance of

(T)
$K_iA\rightarrow A$

is true at every state. The statement of our result will be that if every element of $\Pi$ is true in every state, then every element of $\Delta_i(w)$ is true in state $w$. Let us say that a state $w$ is sound if it is $i$-sound for all $i\in I$.

PROPOSITION 4. If $\Pi\subseteq\mathit{At}(w)$, then $w$ is sound.

The proof of this result requires more substantial groundwork as compared to our earlier results. The natural idea would be to prove that if $A\in\Delta_j(w)$ then $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; A$ by induction on the construction of $\Delta_j(w)$. However, the presence of the schema (T) blocks the basis of this induction, for in order to show that $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; K_iA\rightarrow A$, one would have to show that if $A\in\Delta_j(w)$ then $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; A$, which is just what we were trying to prove. A more indirect strategy is therefore needed. What we do is to transform each formula $A$ into a formula ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$ in such a way that instances of (T) are transformed into tautologies. We then perform the induction for ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$ rather than $A$, and recover the result for $A$ afterwards. ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$ is defined recursively as follows.

${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{P}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}=P$, (for $P\in\mathit{Atom}$),

${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{\neg A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}=\neg{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$,

${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A\wedge B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}={}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$

${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{K_jA}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}={}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$

The proof will proceed by a series of lemmas, each of which is stated under the hypothesis of Proposition 4. For ease of reference, the proofs of the individual lemmas are gathered in the appendix.

LEMMA 4.1. For every $A\in\mathit{Form}$, ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow A\in\Sigma$.

LEMMA 4.2. For $w\in W$ and $A\in\Sigma$, $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$.

LEMMA 4.3. For $w\in W$ and $A\in\Sigma$, $w\ast{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$.

LEMMA 4.4. For $w\in W$ and $A\in\mathit{Form}$, $w\ast{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow A$.

LEMMA 4.5. For $i\in I$, $w\in W$ and $A\in\Delta_i(w)$, $w\ast A$.

LEMMA 4.6. For $w\in W$ and $A\in\mathit{Form}(\Pi')$, $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; A$ if and only if $w\ast A$.

LEMMA 4.7. For $j\in I$, $w\in W$ and $A\in\Delta_j(w)$, $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$.

LEMMA 4.8. For $w\in W$ and $A\in\mathit{Form}$, $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow A$.

The proof of Proposition 4 can now be completed. For, by Lemmas 4.7 and 4.8, for all $j\in I$, $w\in W$ and $A\in\Delta_j(w)$, we have $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; A$. Thus, we can conclude that $w$ is sound for all $w\in W$. □

It remains for us to note that the rule (RN) implies that instances of the formula $K_iA\rightarrow K_iK_iA$ are true at every state. Together with Proposition 4, this tells us that our epistemic logic for Turing machines is $S4$. The most notable feature is that the principle $K_iA\rightarrow A$ is consistent within our framework, thereby restoring the interpretation of $K_i$ as a knowledge operator. This is in contrast to the modal logic $G$.

We do not claim that our construction is the only one available for Turing machines. Analogues of Proposition 4 could be proved for many different logics. However, the purpose of our exercise has been to present a construction which is designed to get as close to partitional knowledge as a Turing machine can. It is worth mentioning that our negative result does not make essential use of the construction of $\Delta_i(w)$. It is a very general result which relies on the fact that the set of formulas which a numerate Turing machine knows cannot be recursive, on pain of inconsistency.

LEMMA 4.1. For $A\in\mathit{Form}$, ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow A\in\Sigma$.

Proof. By induction on the construction of $A$.

Basis. If $P\in\mathit{Atom}$, ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{P}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}=P$, so ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{P}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow P\in\operatorname{Taut}$, but $\mathit{Taut}\subseteq\Sigma$ by construction of the latter, so ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{P}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow P\in\Sigma$.

Induction step. Suppose that $A\notin\mathit{Atom}$, and that the lemma holds for all formulas less complex than $A$.

Case (a): $A$ is of the form $\neg B$. By induction hypothesis, ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow B\in\Sigma$. Now ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{\neg B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow\neg B=\neg{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow\neg B$, and $({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow B)\rightarrow(\neg{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow\neg B)\in\mathit{Taut}$, so $({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}} B)\rightarrow(\neg{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow\neg B)\in\Sigma$. By construction, $\Sigma$ is closed under MP, so $\neg{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow\neg B\in\Sigma$, i.e. ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow A\in\Sigma$.

Case (b): $A$ is of the form $B\wedge C$. Like case (a).

Case (c): $A$ is of the form $K_jB$. By induction hypothesis, ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow B\in\Sigma$. By reasoning as in case (a), ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\rightarrow B\in\Sigma$ and $B\rightarrow{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\in\Sigma$. By construction, $\Sigma$ is closed under $\operatorname{RN}_j$. Hence $K_j({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\rightarrow B)\in\Sigma$ and $K_j(B\rightarrow{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})\in\Sigma$. Since $(\mathrm{K})\subseteq\Sigma$ and $\Sigma$ is closed under MP, $K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\rightarrow K_jB\in\Sigma$ and $K_jB\rightarrow K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\in\Sigma$. Since $(\mathrm{T})\subseteq\Sigma$, $K_jB\rightarrow B\in\Sigma$. But $(K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\rightarrow K_jB)\rightarrow((K_jB\rightarrow K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})\rightarrow((K_jB\rightarrow B)\rightarrow((B\rightarrow{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})\rightarrow(({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})\leftrightarrow K_jB))))\in\mathit{Taut}$, so $({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})\leftrightarrow K_jB\in\Sigma$, i.e. ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow A\in\Sigma$.

LEMMA 4.2. For $w\in W$ and $A\in\Sigma$, $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$.

Proof. Fix $w\in W$. We show that for $A\in\Sigma_n$ $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$ by induction on $n$.

Basis. If $A\in\Sigma_0$ then $A\in\Pi\cup\operatorname{Taut}\cup(\mathrm{K})\cup(\mathrm{T})\cup(4)$.

Case (a): $A\in\Pi$. Thus $A\in\mathit{Atom}$, so ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}=A$. By hypothesis, $\Pi\subseteq\mathit{At}(w)$. Thus $A\in\mathit{At}(w)$. Hence $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$.

Case (b): $A\in\mathit{Taut}$. Since ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$ commutes with $\neg$ and $\wedge$, ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\in\mathit{Taut}$. Since $\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;$ is defined so that $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; B$ for all $B\in\mathit{Taut}$, $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$.

Case (c): $A\in(\mathrm{K})$. Thus for some $B,C,j$, $A=K_j(B\rightarrow C)\rightarrow$ $(K_jB\rightarrow K_jC)$. Now ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$ commutes with $\rightarrow$, which is defined in terms of $\neg$ and $\wedge$. Hence ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}=(({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\rightarrow{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{C}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})\wedge K_j({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\rightarrow{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{C}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}))\rightarrow(({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})\rightarrow({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{C}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{C}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}))$. Evidently, it suffices to show $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; K_j({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\rightarrow{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{C}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})\rightarrow(K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\rightarrow K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{C}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})$. Suppose $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; K_j({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\rightarrow{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{C}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})$ and $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$. Then ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\rightarrow{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{C}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\in\Delta_j(w)$ and ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\in\Delta_j(w)$. By construction, $\Delta_j(w)$ is closed under MP. Thus ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{C}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\in\Delta_j(w)$. Hence $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{C}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$, as required.

Case (d): $A\in(\mathrm{T})$. Thus for some $B,j$, $A=K_jB\rightarrow B$. Hence ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}=({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})\rightarrow{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$, so ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\in\mathit{Taut}$. Thus $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$.

Case (e): $A\in(4)$. Thus for some $B,j$, $A=K_jB\rightarrow K_jK_jB$. Hence ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}=({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})\rightarrow(({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})\wedge K_j({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}))$. Evidently, it suffices to show $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\rightarrow K_j({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})$. Suppose $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$. Then ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\in\Delta_j(w)$. By construction, $\Delta_j(w)$ is closed under $\operatorname{RN}_j$. Thus $K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\in\Delta_j(w)$. Now ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\rightarrow(K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\rightarrow({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}))\in\mathit{Taut}$, so ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\rightarrow(K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\rightarrow({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}))\in\Delta_j(w)$. Since $\Delta_j(w)$ is closed under MP, ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\in\Delta_j(w)$. Thus $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; K_j({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})$, as required.

Induction step. Suppose that for all $A\in\Sigma_n$, $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$. It suffices to show that if $A\in\operatorname{MP}(\Sigma_n)\cup\operatorname{RN}(\Sigma_n)$ then $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$.

Case (a): $A\in\operatorname{MP}(\Sigma_n)$. Thus for some $B$, $B\in\Sigma_n$ and $B\rightarrow A\in\Sigma_n$. By induction hypothesis $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$ and $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B\rightarrow A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$. Since ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B\rightarrow A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}={}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\rightarrow{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$, $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$.

Case (b): $A\in\operatorname{RN}(\Sigma_n)$. Thus for some $B,j$, $B\in\Sigma_n$ and $A=K_jB$. By induction hypothesis, $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$. By Lemma 4.1, ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow B\in\Sigma$. Since $B\in\Sigma_n\subseteq\Sigma$, ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\in\Sigma\subseteq\Delta_j(w)$, so $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$. Thus $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$, i.e. $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$.

LEMMA 4.3. For $w\in W$ and $A\in\Sigma$, $w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$.

Proof. We show by induction on $n$ that for all $A\in\Sigma_n$, $w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$.

Basis. $n=0$.

Case (a): $A\in\Pi$. $A\in\mathit{Atom}$ and ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}=A$; since $\Pi\subseteq\mathit{At}(w)$, $A\in\mathit{At}(w)$, so $w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$.

Case (b): $A\in\mathit{Taut}$. As in (b) of the basis of Lemma 4.2, ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\in\mathit{Taut}$, so $w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$.

Case (c): $A\in(\mathrm{K})$. As in 4.2(c), ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$ is of the form $(({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\rightarrow{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{C}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})\wedge K_j({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\rightarrow{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{C}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}))\rightarrow(({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})\rightarrow({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{C}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{C}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}))$, which is a theorem of the modal system K and therefore valid in the possible worlds semantics for $\text{*}$.

Case (d): $A\in(\mathrm{T})$. As in 4.2(d), ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\in\mathit{Taut}$, so $w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$.

Case (e): $A\in(4)$. As in 4.2(e), ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$ is of the form $({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})\rightarrow(({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})\wedge K_j({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}))$, which is a theorem of the modal system K4 and therefore valid in the possible worlds semantics for $\text{*}$ given the transitivity of $R_j$.

Induction step. Suppose that for all $A\in\Sigma_n$ and $w\in W$, $w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$. It suffices to show that if $A\in\operatorname{MP}(\Sigma_n)\cup\operatorname{RN}(\Sigma_n)$ and $w\in W$ then $w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$.

Case (a): $A\in\operatorname{MP}(\Sigma_n)$. Similar to (a) of the induction step of 4.2.

Case (b): $A\in\operatorname{RN}(\Sigma_n)$. Thus, for some $B\in\Sigma_n$ and $i\in I$, $A=K_iB$. Pick $w\in W$. By induction hypothesis, $w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$ and, for all $x$ such that $wR_ix$, $x\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$. By definition of $\text{*}$, $w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_i{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$, i.e. $w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$.

LEMMA 4.4. For $w\in W$ and $A\in\mathit{Form}$, $w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow A$.

Proof. By induction on the construction of $A$.

Basis. If $P\in\mathit{Atom}$, ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{P}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}=P$, so ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{P}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow P\in\mathit{Taut}$, so $w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{P}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow P$.

Induction step. Suppose that $A\notin\mathit{Atom}$, and that the lemma holds for all formulas less complex than $A$.

Case (a): $A$ is of the form $\neg B$. By induction hypothesis, $w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow B$, so $w\text{*}\neg{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow\neg B$, i.e. $w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow A$.

Case (b): $A$ is of the form $A\wedge C$. Similar to (a).

Case (c): $A$ is of the form $K_jB$. By induction hypothesis, for all $w\in W$, $w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow B$. By reasoning like that in 4.3, $w\text{*} K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow K_jB$. Since $R_j$ is reflexive, $w\text{*} K_jB\rightarrow B$, so $w\text{*} K_jB\rightarrow{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$. Thus $w\text{*}({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})\leftrightarrow K_jB$, i.e. $w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\rightarrow A$.

LEMMA 4.5. For $i\in I$, $w\in W$ and $A\in\Delta_i(w)$, $w\text{*} A$.

Proof. Fix $i\in I$ and $w\in W$. We show by induction on $n$ that for all $x$ such that $wR_ix$ and $A\in\Delta_{i,n}(w)$, $x\text{*} A$.

Basis. $n=0$. If $A\in\Delta_{i,0}(w)$ then $A\in\Sigma\cup\Phi_i(w)$. Let $wR_ix$.

Case (a): $A\in\Sigma$. $x\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$ by 4.3, so $x\text{*} A$ by 4.4.

Case (b): $A\in\Phi_i(w)$. By definition of $\Phi_i(w)$, $w\text{*} K_iA$, so $x\text{*} A$ since $wR_ix$.

Induction step. Suppose that for all $x$ such that $wR_ix$, if $A\in\Delta_{i,n}(w)$ then $x\text{*} A$. It suffices to show that for all $x$ such that $wR_ix$, if $A\in\operatorname{MP}(\Delta_{i,n}(w))\cup\operatorname{RN}_i(\Delta_{i,n}(w))$ then $x\text{*} A$.

Case (a): $A\in\operatorname{MP}(\Delta_{i,n}(w))$. For some $B$, $B\in\Delta_{i,n}(w)$ and $B\rightarrow A\in\Delta_{i,n}(w)$. By induction hypothesis, for all $x$ such that $wR_ix$, $x\text{*} B$ and $x\text{*} B\rightarrow A$, so $x\text{*} A$.

Case (b): $A\in\operatorname{RN}_i(\Delta_{i,n}(w))$. For some $B$, $B\in\Delta_{i,n}(w)$ and $A=K_iB$. By induction hypothesis, for all $x$ such that $wR_ix$, $x\text{*} B$. Since $R_i$ is transitive, for all $x$ such that $wR_ix$, for all $y$ such that $xR_iy$, $y\text{*} B$. By definition of $\text{*}$, for all $x$ such that $wR_ix$, $x\text{*} K_iB$.

LEMMA 4.6. For $w\in W$ and $A\in\mathit{Form}(\Pi')$, $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; A$ if and only if $w\text{*} A$.

Proof. By induction on the construction of $A$.

Basis. If $P\in\mathit{Atom}$, $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; P$ iff $P\in\mathit{At}(w)$ iff $w\text{*} P$.

Induction step. Suppose that $A\notin\mathit{Atom}$, and that the lemma holds for all formulas less complex than $A$. If $A$ is of the form $\neg B$ or $B\wedge C$, the result is routine. Suppose that $A\in\mathit{Form}(\Pi')$ is of the form $K_jB$. If $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; K_jB$, then $B\in\Delta_j(w)$, so $K_jB\in\Delta_j(w)$ because $\Delta_j(w)$ is closed under $\operatorname{RN}_j$, so $w\text{*} K_jB$ by 4.5. Conversely, if $w\text{*} K_jB$ then $B\in\Phi_j(w)\subseteq\Delta_j(w)$ because $B\in\mathit{Form}(\Pi')$, so $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; K_jB$.

LEMMA 4.7. For $j\in I$, $w\in W$ and $A\in\Delta_j(w)$, $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$.

Proof. Fix $j\in I$ and $w\in W$. We show by induction on $n$ that for $A\in\Delta_{j,n}(w)$, $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$.

Basis. $n=0$. If $A\in\Delta_{i,0}(w)$ then $A\in\Sigma\cup\Phi_i(w)$.

Case (a): $A\in\Sigma$. $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$ by 4.2.

Case (b): $A\in\Phi_j(w)$. $A\in\mathit{Form}(\Pi')$ and $w\text{*} K_jA$, so by 4.4 $w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{K_jA}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$, i.e. $w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$, so $w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$, so $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$ by 4.6.

Induction step. Suppose that for all $A\in\Delta_{j,n}(w)$ and $w\in W$, $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$. It suffices to show that if $A\in\operatorname{MP}(\Delta_{j,n}(w))\cup\operatorname{RN}_j(\Delta_{j,n}(w))$ and $w\in W$ then $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$.

Case (a): $A\in\operatorname{MP}(\Delta_{j,n}(w))$. Like (a) of the induction step of 4.2.

Case (b): $A\in\operatorname{RN}_j(\Delta_{j,n}(w))$. Thus for some $B\in\Delta_{j,n}(w)$, $A=K_jB$. By induction hypothesis, $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$. By Lemma 4.1, ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow B\in\Sigma\subseteq\Delta_j(w)$. Since $B\in\Delta_{j,n}(w)$, ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\in\Delta_j(w)$, so $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$. Thus $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$, i.e. $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$.

LEMMA 4.8. For $w\in W$ and $A\in\mathit{Form}$, $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow A$.

Proof. By induction on the construction of $A$. The only interesting case is the induction step for $A=K_jB$. Suppose that $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow B$. If $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$, i.e. $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$, then $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$, so ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\in\Delta_j(w)$, so $B\in\Delta_j(w)$ by 4.1, so $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; K_jB$. Conversely, if $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; K_jB$ then $B\in\Delta_j(w)$, so ${}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\in\Delta_j(w)$ by 4.1, so $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$; moreover, since $B\in\Delta_j(w)$, $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$ by 4.7; thus $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}$. Hence $w\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\;{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow A$.
