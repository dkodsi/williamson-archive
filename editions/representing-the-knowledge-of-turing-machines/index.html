Representing the knowledge of Turing machines

Hyun Song Shin and Timothy Williamson

Theory and Decision 37 (1994): 125–146.

Abstract

When each state of the world is a maximally specific, consistent description of the world, including the description of the knowledge and ignorance of all individuals, the standard partitional model of knowledge is inconsistent with the assumption that an individual’s powers are limited to that of a Turing machine. We show, however, that the epistemic logic S4S4 is consistent with computational constraints.

Keywords: computability, knowledge.

1. Introduction

In this paper, we shall follow the implications of the following pair of principles.

(i)

The state space should be construed as the set of all maximally specific descriptions of the world, including a complete specification of the knowledge (and ignorance) of all individuals.

(ii)

An individual has exactly the reasoning ability of some computing machine.

Each of these principles, taken in isolation, have been enormously influential in decision theory and game theory, but relatively little attention has been devoted to the examination of the conjunction of these statements. Principle (i) has its historical roots in the theory of probability and in the identification of the state space with the sample space associated with a random variable, and in the logician’s idea of a ‘possible world’ as a maximally specific and consistent truth assignment to a set of sentences. Savage (1954) and Jeffrey (1983), among others, have developed this principle in decision theory, while Harsanyi (1967) has popularized this notion for the study of games. Bayesian game theory has now become one of the most important analytical tools in the economics of information, and has generated a huge literature. The work of Aumann (1976, 1987) is worthy of particular mention, and the reader is referred to the recent symposium and survey in the Journal of Economic Perspectives (Brandenburger 1992, Geanakoplos 1992, and Reny 1992).

The assertion that decision makers should be endowed with the abilities of a computing machine (and no more) can be seen as a response to the lacuna in conventional treatments of decision making which neglects the procedural aspects of rationality. This distinction between the substantive and procedural aspects of rationality as propounded, for example, by Simon (1976) takes on particular significance in game theory owing to the additional dimension of the problem posed by iterated knowledge, i.e., knowledge of what others know or don’t know. Binmore (1987) argues that the standard assumption in game theory that all players are “perfectly rational” becomes problematic when players are seen as computing machines. A related line of research, with similar methodological overtones, has been to examine the outcomes in specific games played between computing machines, both finite automata (as in Rubinstein 1986, and Abreu and Rubinstein 1988) and general Turing machines (as in Anderlini 1989, and Canning 1992). The motivation of these papers is to examine the extent to which the results of conventional game theory survive explicit incorporation of procedural rationality. In the case of the literature on finite automata, an additional motive is to explore the effects of bounded rationality on the outcomes of specific games.

In following up the consequences of the conjunction of principles (i) and (ii), we shall employ a framework which is as close as possible to the standard treatments of information and knowledge while being able to accommodate sufficient structure to formalize the notion that individuals are Turing machines. For a description of alternative approaches to modelling knowledge and of the current state of the art, the reader is referred to Lismont and Mongin (1994). In our paper, we start with a state space WW each state of which describes the environment in which the individuals find themselves. In the case of Bayesian games, each state ww will specify the game being played at that state. Since individuals’ knowledge depend on the environment, the knowledge of any individual will vary from state to state. More precisely, for each individual ii, we associate with each state ww a set of formulas, interpreted as those which ii knows – that is, ii’s theory at ww.

We require that each individual’s theory include a version of arithmetic. In our case, the version of arithmetic employed is the theory QQ, described in Boolos and Jeffrey (1980, p. 158). This requirement is permissible because the theorems of QQ can be generated by a Turing machine. We do not want the reasoning abilities of individuals to be more limited than those of such a machine. The theory QQ is a weaker theory than Peano Arithmetic, but has been shown to be sufficient to carry through the main results of Gödel (1931) and Rosser (1936). In particular, any consistent recursively axiomatized extension of QQ is susceptible to the Gödel incompleteness theorems. We shall make essential use of this result ourselves.

In outline, our approach may be sketched as follows. The set of formulas Form in our framework is built up from two disjoint sets of atomic sentences. The first is a set of non-arithmetical sentences Π′\Pi', describing the environment in which the individuals find themselves. The second is a set of sentences Π\Pi in the language of arithmetic. Thus, a typical formula in Form will be a complex formula built up from arithmetical and nonarithmetical components, which are combined by means of logical connectives and the knowledge operators. The first question we address is whether the knowledge of these individuals can be represented in terms of the standard partitional information structures. In other words, whether we can define an equivalence relation of indistinguishability between states so that it is true that an individual knows AA at ww if and only if AA is true at every state which is indistinguishable from ww. Our first result is the negative one that, even if the knowledge of non-arithmetical formulas is governed by a partitional structure, the inclusion of arithmetical knowledge precludes a partitional structure over the knowledge of the set of all formulas. This is accomplished by demonstrating that the principle:

(5)

¬KA→K¬KA\neg KA \rightarrow K\neg KA

cannot hold for all formulas, AA, even though (5) holds for all purely non-arithmetical formulas. This result rests on the observation that (5) implies (under soundness) that the set of formulas known by an individual at a state is not only recursively enumerable, but also recursive, thereby contradicting Rosser’s (1936) version of Gödel’s first incompleteness theorem. We will have a chance to review these concepts below.

Having established this negative result, we embark on our positive programme of seeing which principles of knowledge can be held consistently. The principle:

(4)

KA→KKAKA \rightarrow KKA

is shown to be consistent with computability in our framework. More importantly, our framework also satisfies the key principle:

(T)

KA→AKA \rightarrow A.

This principle would seem to be an essential feature of knowledge, and yet, if the provability predicate of arithmetic is interpreted as the knowledge operator, (T) cannot hold in general, on pain of inconsistency. Boolos (1979) provides a detailed account of the modal logic of provability, and Binmore and Shin (1992) note some of the consequences of these results for game theory. In the following section, we rehearse some of the main results in this area.

We should state at the outset that our purpose is not define knowledge in terms of any specific logical system, but rather to show how an S4S4 logic of knowledge is consistent with computational constraints (and how S5S5 is not). Thus, the principle (T) is, in the first instance, an assumption which is then subsequently shown to be consistent with computational constraints. We do not believe that there is a unique “correct” system of logic for Turing machines which is waiting to be derived from first principles. There will be many logics which are consistent with machine knowledge, and the choice between them will be made on other grounds.

In short, our claim in this paper is twofold. Firstly, an S4S4 logic of knowledge is consistent with the limitations imposed by the fact that individuals are Turing machines, but secondly, the standard partitional representation of knowledge, i.e., the logic S5S5, is inconsistent with machine knowledge. We do not go much beyond making these two observations. Our motivation is to construct a model of knowledge for Turing machines which is as close as possible to the standard representation in economics and decision theory in terms of partitions over the state space. In this respect, our first (negative) result seems to be one which is insensitive to the details of the particular construction of the system.

Our result that the knowledge of Turing machines can satisfy S4S4 may have some bearing on decision theory, in particular on the idea of coherent belief revision in multiperiod settings. At a basic level, coherent belief change could be described as one which precludes the existence of “Dutch Books” against the decision maker. Although partitional information and Bayes updating from the true prior is sufficient for coherent belief revision, the results of Morris (1992) suggest that coherence can be maintained for more general information structures, provided that beliefs follow specified updating rules. In particular, S4S4 is shown to be adequate, provided that updated beliefs satisfy additional restrictions. The details of such arguments are beyond the scope of this paper, and we do not attempt to link our discussion to that of belief coherence. Indeed, we are keenly aware that the extension of our framework to settings which involve probabilistic beliefs is an important and potentially fruitful exercise.

Before proceeding with our construction, we sketch some of the background to our discussion by reviewing some earlier work in this area, and by stating some methodological preliminaries.

2. Background and Preliminaries

In this paper, it will be assumed without argument that a notion of knowledge can be applied to machines. We assume a fixed formal language, and treat the machine as knowing the truth of a formula AA in it if and only if the machine eventually outputs “Yes” on input “AA?”. It may be more accurate to view the output of “Yes” on input “AA?” as the defining property of the machine’s belief of AA rather than its knowledge, but we will not dwell on this distinction. Since our framework preserves the principle KA→AKA \rightarrow A, no substantial issue will turn on the distinction between knowledge and belief.

What the machine knows will be considered as a formal system, with a recursively enumerable set of theorems. The machine’s knowledge is allowed to vary with the state of the world. Thus, each machine in each state of the world is associated with a formal system, whose theorems are what it knows in that state. The machine has some knowledge of its environment, which is incorporated in the axioms of its formal system. Thus a given machine is associated with different formal systems in different states of the world between which its knowledge of the environment differs. It will be assumed that the machines are numerate; in each state each machine’s formal system includes a basic system for arithmetic, QQ (Boolos and Jeffrey 1980, p. 158). Moreover, it will be assumed that all the systems include truth-functional logic: all tautologies and modus ponens.

Treating knowledge as provability in arithmetic must be handled with some care, in view of Gödel’s incompleteness theorem. Gödel (1931) showed how one can compute, for each formula AA in the language of arithmetic a numeral ⌈A⌉\lceil A\rceil and define a predicate Bew⁡(⋅)\operatorname{Bew}(\cdot) in that language which expresses the provability of AA. If Bew⁡(⌈⋅⌉)\operatorname{Bew}(\lceil\cdot\rceil) is interpreted as the knowledge operator KK, then the properties of KK include that of the modal logic GG. GG is a normal system whose theorems include:

(Taut)

All Truth-functional Tautologies

and all instances of:

(K)

K(A→B)→(KA→KB)K(A \rightarrow B) \rightarrow (KA \rightarrow KB)

(Löb)

K(KA→A)→KAK(KA \rightarrow A) \rightarrow KA

One can also show that, for any two formulas AA and BB,

(MP)

If AA and A→BA \rightarrow B are theorems of GG, then so is BB.

(RN)

If AA in a theorem of GG, then so is KAKA.

From this, all instances of the following are theorems, too.

(4)

KA→KKAKA \rightarrow KKA.

However, GG does not include all instances of the following, on pain of inconsistency.

(T)

KA→AKA \rightarrow A.

For let ⊥\bot be any contradiction. If K⊥→⊥K\bot \rightarrow \bot is a theorem, then so is K(K⊥→⊥)K(K\bot \rightarrow \bot) by (RN), so by (Löb) and (MP) K⊥K\bot, and hence ⊥\bot, are theorems. This point is closely related to Gödel’s incompleteness theorem, for K⊥→⊥K\bot \rightarrow \bot is equivalent to ¬K⊥\neg K\bot, a sentence expressing the consistency of the theory. No consistent extension of QQ can prove its own consistency.

There is a way in which one can repair this particular difficulty. One might define KAKA not as “it is provable that AA”, but as “it is provable and true that AA”, i.e., formally by Bew⁡(⌈A⌉)∧A\operatorname{Bew}(\lceil A\rceil) \wedge A rather than by Bew⁡(⌈A⌉)\operatorname{Bew}(\lceil A\rceil). This definition automatically secures (T). In this case the theory also includes the following class of (not very intuitive) formulas.

(Grz)

K(K(A→KA)→A)→AK(K(A \rightarrow KA) \rightarrow A) \rightarrow A.

One also retains (K) and (RN). The system is axiomatized by (Taut), (K), (Grz), (MP) and (RN) is known as S4GrzS4\mathrm{Grz} (see Boolos 1979, pp. 159–167). All instances of (T) and (4) are derivable in it (van Benthem and Blok 1978).

However, this repair does not go to the heart of the problem. First, it makes KAKA express a condition one half of which (the truth of AA) is independent of the activity of the machine. Second, in reasoning about its knowledge of the environment, a machine often needs to know that it is ignorant about some matter, e.g., that it does not know whether the coin will come up heads or tails at the next toss. Such ignorance is formalized as ¬KA∧¬K¬A\neg KA \wedge \neg K\neg A. But if this is a theorem, so that the machine is aware of its ignorance, K(¬KA∧¬K¬A)K(\neg KA \wedge \neg K\neg A) is a theorem by (RN) and so is K¬KA∧K¬K¬AK\neg KA \wedge K\neg K\neg A. However, S4GrzS4\mathrm{Grz} contains the following class of formulas.

(M)

K¬K¬A→¬K¬KAK\neg K\neg A \rightarrow \neg K\neg KA.

If one knows that one doesn’t know that AA is false, then one doesn’t know that one doesn’t know that AA is true; thus one cannot know that one doesn’t know whether AA is true. That is, K(¬KA∧¬K¬A)K(\neg KA \wedge \neg K\neg A) is inconsistent in S4GrzS4\mathrm{Grz}. One can prove this by using the fact that the theorems of S4GrzS4\mathrm{Grz} are exactly the formulas valid on all finite weak partial orderings, i.e., the accessibility relation is reflexive, transitive, and antisymmetric (Boolos 1979, p. 161). In such orderings, each world can access an end-point, i.e., a world that accesses only itself. Since ¬KA∧¬K¬A\neg KA \wedge \neg K\neg A cannot be true at an end point (it is true only at worlds that can access at least two worlds), K(¬KA∧¬K¬A)K(\neg KA \wedge \neg K\neg A) is not true at any world. The problem is even worse in the original system GG, for there if ¬KA\neg KA, and KA→AKA \rightarrow A, hence K(KA→A)K(KA \rightarrow A) is a theorem, then KAKA must be a theorem by (Löb), so that the system is inconsistent.

These problems require a fresh approach. It is intolerable to suppose that the numeracy of the machine prevents it from having knowledge of its own ignorance even in simple empirical situations. Our point of departure is to reinterpret the knowledge operator. The reinterpretation is quite subtle, for we retain the assumption that what an individual knows is precisely what is provable in that individual’s theory, where the axioms of that theory may include empirical information gathered from the environment about the state of the world. To abandon this assumption would be to refuse to take the computational constraint seriously. The assumption may appear to leave us no room for manoeuvre. However, interpretations of a knowledge operator can differ simply in the way they specify the knower. Although this difference seems trivial, it becomes crucial once the operator is iterated. To see this, consider the predicament of Oedipus, who is the son of Jocasta without knowing it. One might think that it makes no difference whether one interprets KK as ‘Oedipus knows’ or ‘the son of Jocasta knows’. If Oedipus knows that it is hot, and knows that he, Oedipus, knows that it is hot, it follows that the son of Jocasta knows that it is hot, but it does not follow that the son of Jocasta knows that the son of Jocasta knows that it is hot. In fact, the son of Jocasta does not even know that she has a son. Thus, KKAKKA is true under one interpretation of KK and false under another. Turing machines are susceptible to a related phenomenon. Each type of machine is associated with a description #\#, listing outputs for each input and machine state. The Gödelian interpretation of KAKA as Bew⁡(⌈A⌉)\operatorname{Bew}(\lceil A\rceil) interprets it as something like ‘The machine with description #\# knows’. But it cannot be assumed that the machine with description #\# knows that it is the machine with description #\#. If you ask it ‘Are you the machine with description #\#?’, it may not answer ‘Yes’. Machines of this kind are studied in Benacerraf (1967) and Reinhardt (1986). It follows that the Gödelian interpretation of KK is not the one best suited to decision theory. Consider again the formula K(¬KA∧¬K¬A)K(\neg KA \wedge \neg K\neg A). We want it to express the agent’s knowledge that he does not know whether AA, knowledge on which he might act, e.g. by seeking more information. On the Gödelian interpretation, the formula says that the machine with description #\# knows that the machine with description #\# does not know whether AA. But if it does not know that it itself is the machine with description #\#, it may not know that it itself does not know whether AA, and may therefore do nothing. To avoid this problem, we must interpret the knowledge operator with a specification of the machine under which the machine can recognize itself, just as Oedipus knows that he is Oedipus. We shall proceed on this understanding. Since our interpretation of KK is not Gödelian, there is no reason to think that KK must obey the principles of the system GG. We now proceed to investigate some constraints on KK.

3. The Framework and a Negative Result

Our construction is motivated by our goal of providing an account of the knowledge of a Turing machine which is as close as possible to the familiar partitional model of knowledge. We can think of our Turing machine as one which has access to empirical and factual knowledge, as well as to the theorems of arithmetic. Thus, the set of formulas over which the Turing machine has knowledge is of necessity a hybrid set consisting of arithmetical and non-arithmetical sentences, and complex formulas which combine both types by means of logical connectives and the knowledge operator. For our purposes, the extra structure offered by sentences of arithmetic will play no semantic role. Well formed formulas of arithmetic will be regarded as atomic propositions in our logic. However, the structure of arithmetic will play the key role of forcing the machine’s theory to be recursively enumerable, but not recursive. It is time that we reviewed some of these basic concepts.

First, recall the definitions of recursive and recursively enumerable sets. Informally, a set XX of natural numbers is recursive if there is a Turing computable procedure that on input nn gives output 1 if n∈Xn \in X and output 0 if n∉Xn \notin X. The set xx is recursively enumerable if there is a Turing computable procedure that on input nn gives output 1 if n∈Xn \in X, and either gives output 0 or never terminates when n∉Xn \notin X. Note that XX is recursive if and only if it and its complement are recursively enumerable: a one-way positive test and a one-way negative test are jointly equivalent to a two-way test. We can extend these notions from natural numbers to formulas via the standard procedure for coding the latter by the former. A theory is said to be recursively axiomatized if the set of its axioms is recursive, i.e., it is computable whether or not something is an axiom. Now, if a theory is recursively axiomatized, the set of its theorems is recursively enumerable, because any theorem can be recognized as such via inspection of its proof. But the set of theorems need not be recursive, for there may be no algorithm for recognizing every non-theorem as such. Precisely this is the case with arithmetic.

With these preliminaries, we now proceed to describe our framework. For clarity, our construction will proceed in three steps. First, we define the basis for the empirical or factual knowledge of our individuals in terms of standard possible world semantics and partitional information structures. Next, we endow individuals with arithmetical knowledge by assuming that they know (at every state of the world) all the theorems of a version of arithmetic. Finally, we combine the two sources of knowledge, and construct the set of formulas known by an individual at each state of the world.

The ii-soundness of the state ww is equivalent to the statement that every formula of the form KiA→AK_iA\rightarrow A is true at ww. We have yet to verify this statement (it will be the subject of the next section). Subject to ii-soundness, we can prove that the information of the Turing machines cannot be partitional. Consider the following set of formulas.

(5ii)

According to (5i)(5i), if machine ii doesn’t know something, it knows that it doesn’t know it. (5i)(5i) would be true if the states of the world were partitioned by the relation of indiscriminability by ii, and ii knows just those proportions true in all states indiscriminable from the one it is in. For then, if it did not know AA, ¬KiA\neg K_iA would be true in every state indiscriminable from the one ii is in, so Ki¬KiAK_i\neg K_iA would be true. 4. The Positive Result

One of the key hypotheses in showing that Turing machines cannot have partitional knowledge was that every state ww is ii-sound. In this section, we demonstrate that this is indeed the case, provided that the arithmetical theory used by the machines is correct. In so doing, we will be showing that every instance of

Proof. We show by induction on nn that for all A∈ΣnA\in\Sigma_n, w*^A^w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}.

Basis. n=0n=0.

Case (b): A∈𝑇𝑎𝑢𝑡A\in\mathit{Taut}. As in (b) of the basis of Lemma 4.2, ^A^∈𝑇𝑎𝑢𝑡{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\in\mathit{Taut}, so w*^A^w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}.

Case (c): A∈(K)A\in(\mathrm{K}). As in 4.2(c), ^A^{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}} is of the form ((^B^→^C^)∧Kj(^B^→^C^))→((^B^∧Kj^B^)→(^C^∧Kj^C^))(({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\rightarrow{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{C}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})\wedge K_j({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\rightarrow{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{C}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}))\rightarrow(({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})\rightarrow({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{C}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{C}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})), which is a theorem of the modal system K and therefore valid in the possible worlds semantics for *\text{*}.

Case (d): A∈(T)A\in(\mathrm{T}). As in 4.2(d), ^A^∈𝑇𝑎𝑢𝑡{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\in\mathit{Taut}, so w*^A^w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}.

Case (e): A∈(4)A\in(4). As in 4.2(e), ^A^{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}} is of the form (^B^∧Kj^B^)→((^B^∧Kj^B^)∧Kj(^B^∧Kj^B^))({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})\rightarrow(({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})\wedge K_j({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})), which is a theorem of the modal system K4 and therefore valid in the possible worlds semantics for *\text{*} given the transitivity of RjR_j.

Induction step. Suppose that for all A∈ΣnA\in\Sigma_n and w∈Ww\in W, w*^A^w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}. It suffices to show that if A∈MP⁡(Σn)∪RN⁡(Σn)A\in\operatorname{MP}(\Sigma_n)\cup\operatorname{RN}(\Sigma_n) and w∈Ww\in W then w*^A^w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}.

Case (a): A∈MP⁡(Σn)A\in\operatorname{MP}(\Sigma_n). Similar to (a) of the induction step of 4.2.

By induction hypothesis, w*^B^w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}} and, for all xx such that wRixwR_ix, x*^B^x\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}. By definition of *\text{*}, w*^B^∧Ki^B^w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_i{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}, i.e. w*^A^w\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}.

LEMMA 4.4. For w∈Ww\in W and A∈𝐹𝑜𝑟𝑚A\in\mathit{Form}, w*^A^↔Aw\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow A.

Proof. By induction on the construction of AA.

Basis. If P∈𝐴𝑡𝑜𝑚P\in\mathit{Atom}, ^P^=P{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{P}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}=P, so ^P^↔P∈𝑇𝑎𝑢𝑡{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{P}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow P\in\mathit{Taut}, so w*^P^↔Pw\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{P}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow P.

Induction step. Suppose that A∉𝐴𝑡𝑜𝑚A\notin\mathit{Atom}, and that the lemma holds for all formulas less complex than AA.

Case (a): AA is of the form ¬B\neg B. By induction hypothesis, w*^B^↔Bw\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow B, so w*¬^B^↔¬Bw\text{*}\neg{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow\neg B, i.e. w*^A^↔Aw\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow A.

Case (b): AA is of the form A∧CA\wedge C. Similar to (a).

Case (c): AA is of the form KjBK_jB. By induction hypothesis, for all w∈Ww\in W, w*^B^↔Bw\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow B. By reasoning like that in 4.3, w*Kj^B^↔KjBw\text{*} K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\leftrightarrow K_jB. Since RjR_j is reflexive, w*KjB→Bw\text{*} K_jB\rightarrow B, so w*KjB→^B^w\text{*} K_jB\rightarrow{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}. Thus w*(^B^∧Kj^B^)↔KjBw\text{*}({}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\wedge K_j{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{B}^{\text{\textasciicircum}})\leftrightarrow K_jB, i.e. w*^A^→Aw\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}\rightarrow A.

LEMMA 4.5. For i∈Ii\in I, w∈Ww\in W and A∈Δi(w)A\in\Delta_i(w), w*Aw\text{*} A.

Proof. Fix i∈Ii\in I and w∈Ww\in W. We show by induction on nn that for all xx such that wRixwR_ix and A∈Δi,n(w)A\in\Delta_{i,n}(w), x*Ax\text{*} A.

Basis. n=0n=0. If A∈Δi,0(w)A\in\Delta_{i,0}(w) then A∈Σ∪Φi(w)A\in\Sigma\cup\Phi_i(w). Let wRixwR_ix.

Case (a): A∈ΣA\in\Sigma. x*^A^x\text{*}{}^{\text{\textasciicircum}}{A}^{\text{\textasciicircum}} by 4.3, so x*Ax\text{*} A by 4.4.

Case (b): A∈Φi(w)A\in\Phi_i(w). By definition of Φi(w)\Phi_i(w), w*KiAw\text{*} K_iA, so x*Ax\text{*} A since wRixwR_ix.

Induction step. Suppose that for all xx such that wRixwR_ix, if A∈Δi,n(w)A\in\Delta_{i,n}(w) then x*Ax\text{*} A. It suffices to show that for all xx such that wRixwR_ix, if A∈MP⁡(Δi,n(w))∪RN⁡i(Δi,n(w))A\in\operatorname{MP}(\Delta_{i,n}(w))\cup\operatorname{RN}_i(\Delta_{i,n}(w)) then x*Ax\text{*} A.

Case (a): A∈MP⁡(Δi,n(w))A\in\operatorname{MP}(\Delta_{i,n}(w)). For some BB, B∈Δi,n(w)B\in\Delta_{i,n}(w) and B→A∈Δi,n(w)B\rightarrow A\in\Delta_{i,n}(w). By induction hypothesis, for all xx such that wRixwR_ix, x*Bx\text{*} B and x*B→Ax\text{*} B\rightarrow A, so x*Ax\text{*} A.

Case (b): A∈RN⁡i(Δi,n(w))A\in\operatorname{RN}_i(\Delta_{i,n}(w)). For some BB, B∈Δi,n(w)B\in\Delta_{i,n}(w) and A=KiBA=K_iB. By induction hypothesis, for all xx such that wRixwR_ix, x*Bx\text{*} B. Since RiR_i is transitive, for all xx such that wRixwR_ix, for all yy such that xRiyxR_iy, y*By\text{*} B. By definition of *\text{*}, for all xx such that wRixwR_ix, x*KiBx\text{*} K_iB.

LEMMA 4.6. For w∈Ww\in W and A∈𝐹𝑜𝑟𝑚(Π′)A\in\mathit{Form}(\Pi'), w|=Aw\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; A if and only if w*Aw\text{*} A.

Basis. If P∈𝐴𝑡𝑜𝑚P\in\mathit{Atom}, w|=Pw\;\mathord{|}\mathord{=}\; P iff P∈𝐴𝑡(w)P\in\mathit{At}(w) iff w*Pw\text{*} P.

Suppose that A∈𝐹𝑜𝑟𝑚(Π′)A\in\mathit{Form}(\Pi') is of the form KjBK_jB.
