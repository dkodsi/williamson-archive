Ethics, supervenience and Ramsey sentences

Timothy Williamson

Philosophy and Phenomenological Research 62 (3) (2001): 625–630.

In From Metaphysics to Ethics, Frank Jackson presents an impressively systematic conception of modal reality and its relation to our thought. His account of ethics is a test case for this conception. I will argue that Jackson’s reasoning for his conclusions about ethics is unsound.

Jackson begins his discussion of ethics by assuming cognitivism, defined as the doctrine that ethical sentences are semantically capable of being truth-valued (113). I happily accept that assumption. Next, Jackson assumes the global supervenience of the ethical on the descriptive (119):

(S)
For all [worlds] ww and w*w\text{*}, if ww and w*w\text{*} are exactly alike descriptively then they are exactly alike ethically.

Jackson does not attempt to define ‘descriptive’ rigorously, but makes it clear that he will not defend (S) by applying the term in a tendentiously promiscuous fashion (120). For example, he will not appeal to the familiar sense in which we describe people in ethical terms (it is useful to know whether someone you are about to meet is good or bad). Perhaps one could stipulate that a term is to count as descriptive if and only if it is purely non-evaluative, although that is not exactly what Jackson says. At any rate, I am willing to accept (S) on his understanding of ‘descriptive’, and even, for the sake of argument, his further claim that (S) is “both a priori true and necessary” (119).

Granted cognitivism, Jackson argues from (S) to the conclusion that ethical properties are descriptive properties. He first presents the familiar Kim-style argument that, given (S), for any ethical sentence there is a necessarily equivalent descriptive sentence, a possibly infinite disjunction of possibly infinite conjunctions. He then claims:

The same line of argument can be applied mutatis mutandis to ethical and descriptive predicates and open sentences: for any ethical predicate there is a purely descriptive one that is necessarily co-extensive with it. (123)

He concludes:

It follows that ethical properties are descriptive properties. For it is a consequence of the way the ethical supervenes on the descriptive that any claim about how things are made in ethical vocabulary makes no distinctions among the possibilities that cannot in principle be made in purely descriptive vocabulary. (ibid.)

Although this extension of the argument from closed sentences to open sentences and predicates may look plausible, it is fallacious. (S) does not imply that ethical predicates are co-extensive with descriptive predicates, let alone necessarily co-extensive. However coarsely properties are individuated, (S) does not imply that ethical properties are descriptive properties.

The point is a formal one. The global supervenience claim that worlds exactly alike in A\mathrm{A}-respects are exactly alike in B\mathrm{B}-respects does not imply that B\mathrm{B}-predicates are co-extensive with A\mathrm{A}-predicates, even when A\mathrm{A}-predicates are closed under infinite logical operations. Here is an example. Let the A\mathrm{A}-predicates be all truth-functions of ‘drunk’ and ‘sober’ as predicates true of people. Worlds are exactly alike in A\mathrm{A}-respects if and only if each A\mathrm{A}-predicate is satisfied by exactly the same things in them at exactly the same times. Let the B\mathrm{B}-predicates be all truth-functions of ‘odd’ and ‘even’ as predicates true of natural numbers. Worlds are exactly alike in B\mathrm{B}-respects if and only if each B\mathrm{B}-predicate is satisfied by exactly the same things in them. Since mathematics is not contingent and B\mathrm{B}-predicates are mathematical predicates, it is not contingent whether a given thing satisfies a given B\mathrm{B}-predicate. Thus all worlds are exactly alike in B\mathrm{B}-respects. The global supervenience thesis therefore holds trivially: for all worlds ww and w*w\text{*}, if ww and w*w\text{*} are exactly alike in A\mathrm{A}-respects then they are exactly alike in B\mathrm{B}-respects. But it does not follow that every B\mathrm{B}-predicate is co-extensive with an A\mathrm{A}-predicate. Since ‘drunk’ and ‘sober’ are true of no natural numbers, any truth-function of them is true of all natural numbers or of none. Thus no A\mathrm{A}-predicate is co-extensive with the B\mathrm{B}-predicate ‘even’.

Someone might deny that all worlds are alike in B\mathrm{B}-respects on the grounds that the extension of a negative B\mathrm{B}-predicate such as ‘not even’ includes all non-mathematical objects and it is contingent what non-mathematical objects there are. Even if that view were accepted, it would make no difference to the point at issue: global supervenience would still hold. For if two worlds are exactly alike in A\mathrm{A}-respects, then ‘not drunk’ has the same extension in them, as does ‘drunk’, so there are the same non-mathematical objects in those worlds, so they are exactly alike in B\mathrm{B}-respects too.

For safety’s sake, we can consider another example. Define a predicate FF to be uniform if and only if necessarily everything satisfies it if anything does (□∀x∀y(Fx≡Fy)□\forall x\,\forall y\,(Fx \equiv Fy)). For example, ‘such that Napoleon died in 1821’ is a uniform predicate. All truth-functions of uniform predicates are uniform predicates. Let the A\mathrm{A}-predicates be the uniform predicates (whether or not they are currently expressed in a natural language). Then ww and w*w\text{*} are exactly alike in A\mathrm{A}-respects only if they are identical, for we can define a uniform predicate FwF_w to be true in ww of everything and in every other world of nothing; since something satisfies the A\mathrm{A}-predicate FwF_w in ww (on the assumption that every world contains at least one object, if only a mathematical one) and w*w\text{*} is exactly like ww in A\mathrm{A}-respects, something satisfies FwF_w in w*w\text{*}, so w*w\text{*} is ww. Consequently, the global supervenience thesis holds whatever the B\mathrm{B}-respects are, for if ww and w*w\text{*} are the same world then they are exactly alike in all respects. For example, if the B\mathrm{B}-predicates are the physical predicates, then worlds exactly alike in A\mathrm{A}-respects are exactly alike in B\mathrm{B}-respects too. But it does not follow that every B\mathrm{B}-predicate is co-extensive with an A\mathrm{A}-predicate. For if anything satisfies a predicate GG co-extensive with a uniform predicate then everything satisfies GG; but some things satisfy a physical predicate such as ‘six inches long’ while others do not.

A third way to see the same point is to note that the (crazy) supposition that there is only one possible world trivializes (S) but does not trivialize the claim that every ethical predicate is co-extensive with a descriptive predicate. Yet Jackson’s argument from (S) to co-extensiveness does not assume that there is more than one possible world.

Thus Jackson’s argument for the claim that ethical properties are descriptive properties is fallacious. Although (S) can be interpreted as saying that the total pattern of descriptive properties fixes the total pattern of ethical properties, it does not follow that an individual’s place in the pattern of descriptive properties fixes its place in the pattern of ethical properties. In moving from comparisons of individuals to comparisons of worlds one loses information; any argument from global supervenience alone to the co-extensiveness of ethical properties of individuals with descriptive properties attempts in vain to recover that information.

What Jackson needs as a premise in order to argue that ethical properties are descriptive properties in the fashion he envisages is not (S) but something like this:

(S1)
For all worlds ww and w*w\text{*} and individuals ii and i*i\text{*}, if ii in ww and i*i\text{*} in w*w\text{*} are exactly alike descriptively then they are exactly alike ethically.

For the reasons just explained, (S1) does not follow from (S) alone. Of course, (S1) follows trivially from the claim that ethical properties are necessarily co-extensive with descriptive properties. Conversely, one can argue from (S1) to that claim by substituting pairs of an individual and a world for worlds in the Kim-style argument for the necessary equivalence of ethical propositions with descriptive propositions. For nn-place relations one would need a principle standing to (S1) as nn-tuples of individuals stand to individuals.

Jackson might reply that, although (S1) is stronger than (S), the reasons for finding (S) plausible are also reasons for finding (S1) plausible. Moreover, an alternative argument might be offered on Jackson’s behalf. Suppose that for every possible bearer ii of ethical properties the property =i=i of being ii counts as descriptive. Let E\mathrm{E} be an ethical property. Given (S), the proposition that ii has E\mathrm{E} is necessarily equivalent to a descriptive proposition Di,E\mathrm{D}_{i,\mathrm{E}}. Now ii and E\mathrm{E} need not be in any sense constituents of Di,E\mathrm{D}_{i,\mathrm{E}}, so one cannot abstract a property Dx,E\mathrm{D}_{x,\mathrm{E}} by ‘dropping’ ii from Di,E\mathrm{D}_{i,\mathrm{E}}. However, the conjunctive property =i&Di,E=i \mathbin{\&} \mathrm{D}_{i,\mathrm{E}} of being ii and such that Di,E\mathrm{D}_{i,\mathrm{E}} holds is itself descriptive; ii has it in those worlds in which ii has E\mathrm{E}, and nothing else has it in any world. But then E\mathrm{E} is equivalent to the disjunction of the =i&Di,E=i \mathbin{\&} \mathrm{D}_{i,\mathrm{E}} properties taken over all possible bearers ii of ethical properties; that disjunction is a descriptive property.

The matter is more delicate than it may look. If the descriptive is required to be purely qualitative, then, contrary to the premise of that alternative argument, the property of being a particular individual ii may not count as descriptive. Even if the descriptive is not purely qualitative, designators of some bearers of ethical properties may still not count as descriptive. After all, we sometimes attribute ethical properties to ethical entities, for example in saying that wickedness is worse than naughtiness. Presumably we need to exclude ethical properties themselves as such bearers. More significantly, too purist a conception of the descriptive might exclude the paradigmatic bearers of ethical properties, such as persons. Suppose, for example, that all descriptive predicates are actually co-extensive with truth-functions of basic predicates of fundamental physics. Suppose also that all basic predicates of fundamental physics are true only of mereological sums of particles and/or fields. Consequently, if FF is a descriptive predicate and ii and jj are not mereological sums of particles and/or fields, then ii satisfies FF if and only if jj satisfies FF. Thus if persons are not mereological sums of particles and/or fields (perhaps because they lack the requisite essential properties), then all persons actually satisfy the same descriptive predicates, so (S1) would make all persons exactly alike ethically in the actual world, which they are not. Of course, Jackson is at liberty to reject one or more of the premises on which this reductio ad absurdum of (S1) depends. My point is that, in order to do so, he would have to engage with the nature of descriptive and ethical reality in a much deeper way than he does in defence of (S). In particular, a more specific account is needed of both descriptive properties and the bearers of ethical properties, in order to check that the former are sufficiently discriminating between the latter to satisfy (S1). That is not to say that (S1) is false, merely that Jackson has not yet provided adequate materials to show that it is true. Vague invocations of physicalism are not enough.

There is also an opposite danger, of trivializing the claim that ethical properties are descriptive properties. For example, disquotational identities of this kind are arguably necessary:

(R1)
Rightness = the property to which ‘right’ actually refers in this context.

On an unwary conception of the descriptive, the right-hand side of (R1) is a descriptive designation of the ethical property of rightness. Jackson presumably does not intend anything as trivial as that. The range of the descriptive must be tightened enough to exclude (R1), but not so much as to falsify (S1).

Jackson offers to identify the ethical properties in descriptive terms, using the method of Ramsey sentences as refined by Carnap and David Lewis. Let the ethical properties be P1,…,Pn\mathrm{P}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{P}_n, and let M(P1,…,Pn)\mathrm{M}(\mathrm{P}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{P}_n) be mature folk morality’s account of them. Since all the ethical content is packed into those property names, the predicate M(Y1,…,Yn)\mathrm{M}(\mathrm{Y}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{Y}_n) is descriptive. Jackson endorses a form of moral functionalism on which “the ethical concepts are fixed by their place in the network of mature folk morality” (140). According to moral functionalism, M(P1,…,Pn)\mathrm{M}(\mathrm{P}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{P}_n) is equivalent to this modified Ramsey sentence:

(MRS)
∃X1…∃Xn∀Y1…∀Yn(M(Y1,…,Yn)≡(X1=Y1&…&Xn=Yn))\exists \mathrm{X}_1 \ldots \exists \mathrm{X}_n \forall \mathrm{Y}_1 \ldots \forall \mathrm{Y}_n (\mathrm{M}(\mathrm{Y}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{Y}_n) \equiv (\mathrm{X}_1 = \mathrm{Y}_1 \mathbin{\&} \ldots \mathbin{\&} \mathrm{X}_n = \mathrm{Y}_n))

Let rightness be Pi\mathrm{P}_i. Then, given its equivalence to (MRS), M(P1,…,Pn)\mathrm{M}(\mathrm{P}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{P}_n) implies this for any action AA:

(R2)
AA is right iff ∃X1…∃Xn(A has Xi&∀Y1…∀Yn(M(Y1,…,Yn)≡(X1=Y1&…&Xn=Yn))\exists \mathrm{X}_1 \ldots \exists \mathrm{X}_n (A\text{ has }\mathrm{X}_i \mathbin{\&} \forall \mathrm{Y}_1 \ldots \forall \mathrm{Y}_n (\mathrm{M}(\mathrm{Y}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{Y}_n) \equiv (\mathrm{X}_1 = \mathrm{Y}_1 \mathbin{\&} \ldots \mathbin{\&} \mathrm{X}_n = \mathrm{Y}_n))

Given the necessity of (R2), rightness can thus be specified in descriptive terms as the property implicitly attributed to AA by the right-hand side of (R2). Unfortunately, space does not permit me to examine this interesting proposal as carefully as it deserves. I will make two brief comments.

First, the justification of (R2) depends on the necessary truth of mature folk morality. What Jackson says in support of that assumption is unconvincing. He points out that if we are not to change the subject we should mean what the folk mean by their moral terms. He immediately draws this conclusion:

We had better, that is, identify our subject via the folk theory of rightness, wrongness, goodness, badness, and so on. We need to identify rightness as the property that satisfies, or near enough satisfies, the folk theory of rightness—and likewise for the other moral properties. (118)

Non sequitur. We can mean the same by a word and disagree radically about its application. For example, if everyone in a community insists on applying the term ‘just’ to slavery, it does not follow that an outsider who refuses to do so is using the word ‘just’ in a different sense. A whole community can be wrong in its application of a term. Although Jackson distinguishes current crude folk morality from mature folk morality, he does not describe the path from the former to the latter in a way which offers any obvious guarantee that systematic errors about what is right and wrong will not persist. Perhaps advances in the theory of reference or in ethical theory will vindicate Jackson’s specific claim about the reference of ethical terms, but the present support for it is weak.

Second, (R2) depends on the derivation of (MRS) from mature folk morality, which in turn depends on the assumption that mature folk morality implies that it has a unique realization. More precisely, the assumption is that M(P1,…,Pn)\mathrm{M}(\mathrm{P}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{P}_n) implies this:

(!)
∀X1…∀Xn∀Y1…∀Yn((M(X1,…,Xn)&M(Y1,…,Yn))⊃(X1=Y1&…&Xn=Yn))\forall \mathrm{X}_1 \ldots \forall \mathrm{X}_n \forall \mathrm{Y}_1 \ldots \forall \mathrm{Y}_n ((\mathrm{M}(\mathrm{X}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{X}_n) \mathbin{\&} \mathrm{M}(\mathrm{Y}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{Y}_n)) \supset (\mathrm{X}_1 = \mathrm{Y}_1 \mathbin{\&} \ldots \mathbin{\&} \mathrm{X}_n = \mathrm{Y}_n))

As Jackson acknowledges in a footnote, “[t]his assumption deserves further discussion” (140). It is not a peripheral technical matter; it is a central article of faith in Jackson’s programme.

Mature folk morality is supposed to be the point at which folk morality reaches reflective equilibrium. Jackson cannot exclude the possibility that on some significant issues folk morality lacks the resources ever to reach reflective equilibrium. Some moral disputes may be irresolvable—which of course does not imply that there is no right answer, just that we cannot agree on what it is. Rather than conclude that there is no such thing as mature folk morality, we can treat it as neutral on such questions. Let such a contested moral proposition be N(P1,…,Pn)\mathrm{N}(\mathrm{P}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{P}_n). Since M(P1,…,Pn)\mathrm{M}(\mathrm{P}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{P}_n) leaves N(P1,…,Pn)\mathrm{N}(\mathrm{P}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{P}_n) undecided, it is plausible that there are properties X1,…,Xn\mathrm{X}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{X}_n for which M(X1,…,Xn)&N(X1,…,Xn)\mathrm{M}(\mathrm{X}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{X}_n) \mathbin{\&} \mathrm{N}(\mathrm{X}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{X}_n) holds and properties Y1,…,Yn\mathrm{Y}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{Y}_n for which M(Y1,…,Yn)&∼N(Y1,…,Yn)\mathrm{M}(\mathrm{Y}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{Y}_n) \mathbin{\&} \mathord{\sim}\mathrm{N}(\mathrm{Y}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{Y}_n) holds. This is not moral relativism, because we need not (in fact, should not) regard the identifications of P1,…,Pn\mathrm{P}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{P}_n with X1,…,Xn\mathrm{X}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{X}_n and with Y1,…,Yn\mathrm{Y}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{Y}_n as ‘equally true’. By Leibniz’s Law, the conjunction X1=Y1&…&Xn=Yn\mathrm{X}_1 = \mathrm{Y}_1 \mathbin{\&} \ldots \mathbin{\&} \mathrm{X}_n = \mathrm{Y}_n is false. But then there are properties X1,…,Xn\mathrm{X}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{X}_n and (in at least one instance) distinct properties Y1,…,Yn\mathrm{Y}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{Y}_n for which M(X1,…,Xn)&M(Y1,…,Yn)\mathrm{M}(\mathrm{X}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{X}_n) \mathbin{\&} \mathrm{M}(\mathrm{Y}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{Y}_n) holds, contrary to (!). Thus either mature folk morality is false or it does not imply that it has a unique realization. Either way, moral functionalism is false and the argument for (R2) unsound.

The argument is by no means decisive, for the existence of such properties as X1,…,Xn\mathrm{X}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{X}_n and Y1,…,Yn\mathrm{Y}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{Y}_n has not been properly established. Nevertheless, it shows how strong Jackson’s assumption is. In the absence of more support for unique realization than Jackson provides, moral functionalism looks quite implausible.
