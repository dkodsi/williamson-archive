# The broadness of the mental: some logical considerations

Timothy Williamson

Philosophical Perspectives 12, Language, Mind, and Ontology (1998): 389–410.

# 0.

When I attribute a mental state to you in ordinary language, the implications of my statement can easily outrun your boundaries. I say that you see paper; as every sceptic knows, you could be in the same internal state as someone who sees paper without seeing paper yourself; my statement is true only if paper is there before your eyes, outside you. In some sense, my statement is not purely about you. For theoretical purposes, wouldn’t it be more perspicuous to resolve the mixture into its underlying elements, by separating a statement purely about you from another purely about the environment external to you? After all, causation is local; no action at a distance; doesn’t the causal explanation of your actions require the isolation of what is local to you from background conditions on the environment? This resolution might amount to an analysis, giving a necessary and sufficient condition for the truth of my original statement. Alternatively, it might replace that statement without being equivalent to it by doing its causal-explanatory work better. Either way, internal and external factors are separated.

I will argue on structural grounds that no such separation is possible. The attribution of an ordinary mental state is not equivalent to the conjunction of something purely internal with something purely external, and this inequivalence is essential to the causal-explanatory work the attribution can do.

# 1.

The issue must be defined in more rigorous terms before it can usefully be addressed. What exactly is the distinction between the internal and the external? The boundaries of the agent that our attributions of mental states outrun are spatiotemporal boundaries. The spatial boundary is naturally identified with that of the agent’s body, although for present purposes it could just as well be identified with that of the brain (or the head). But only what goes on within the agent’s body at the time of action counts as internal, for past bodily goings on are not local in the sense in which causation is supposed to be local. The internal will be identified with the total internal physical state of the agent at the relevant time, the external with the total physical state of the external environment.

To a first approximation, the internal and the external can be treated as independent variables. This is not completely unproblematic; if determinism holds and the external includes the past (as it must for the treatment of issues about reference), then the external nomically determines the internal.1 However, this kind of interdependence of the internal and the external is really a problem for the attempts to separate them which this paper criticizes. It is therefor fair to ignore it, on the assumption that it can be handled by minor modifications of the framework—for example, by restricting what aspects of the past count as environmental. After all, to a first approximation, colour and shape can be treated as independent properties of an object, even if its colour ultimately depends on its microscopic geometry.

Everything I say will be consistent with the mildly physicalist assumption that the internal and the external are jointly exhaustive as well as mutually exclusive, in the sense that the total internal physical state of the subject and the total physical state of the external environment jointly determine the total state of the world (no difference in the latter without a difference in at least one of the former).

Some terminology will help. A case is a possible total state of a system, the system consisting of an agent at a time paired with an external environment (which may contain other subjects). A case is like a possible world, but with a distinguished subject and time (a ‘centred world’). Different cases can distinguish different subjects and times. A condition obtains or fails to obtain in each case. Conditions are specified by ‘that’ clauses. The pronoun ‘one’ and the present tense in such clauses refer to the distinguished agent and time respectively. Thus the condition that one is happy obtains in a case $\alpha$ iff in $\alpha$ the agent of $\alpha$ is happy at the time of $\alpha$.

A condition $\mathrm{C}$ entails a condition $\mathrm{D}$ iff for every case $\alpha$, if $\mathrm{C}$ obtains in $\alpha$ then $\mathrm{D}$ obtains in $\alpha$. The conditions $\mathrm{C}$ and $\mathrm{D}$ are identical iff for every case $\alpha$, $\mathrm{C}$ obtains in $\alpha$ iff $\mathrm{D}$ obtains in $\alpha$. Truth-functions of conditions are defined in the obvious way; for example, the conjunction of $\mathrm{C}$ and $\mathrm{D}$ obtains in $\alpha$ iff both $\mathrm{C}$ and $\mathrm{D}$ obtain in $\alpha$ (the criterion of identity for conditions ensures that such truth-functions have unique values).

A case $\alpha$ is internally like a case $\beta$ iff the total internal physical state of the agent in $\alpha$ is exactly the same as the total internal physical state of the agent in $\beta$. A condition $\mathrm{C}$ is narrow iff for all cases $\alpha$ and $\beta$, if $\alpha$ is internally like $\beta$ then $\mathrm{C}$ obtains in $\alpha$ iff $\mathrm{C}$ obtains in $\beta$.2 In other terminology, narrow conditions supervene on or are determined by internal physical state; no difference in whether they obtain without a difference in that state. $\mathrm{C}$ is broad iff it is not narrow.

When we attribute mental states to each other in ordinary language, the conditions of which we speak are often broad. That one sees Naples, that one remembers Naples, that one loves Naples, that one keeps referring to Naples: all are broad conditions, because none obtains in cases in which one lacks even indirect causal connection with Naples, whereas one’s internal physical state has no such necessary dependence on a city. The content of one’s propositional attitudes is a notorious source of broad conditions, manifest in the discussion initiated by Putnam, Burge, McDowell, Evans and others. I believe that tigers growl; someone could be in exactly the same total internal physical state without believing that tigers growl, because what (if anything) he believes growl are not tigers but creatures of another kind with the same appearance prevalent in his environment. The condition that one believes that tigers growl is broad; likewise for other propositional attitudes and other contents. I believe that this screen flickers; someone could be in exactly the same total internal physical state without believing that this screen flickers, because what (if anything) he believes flickers is not this screen but another with the same appearance in front of him. Beyond the content, the attitude to it is a further source of broad conditions. One knows that it is raining only if it is raining; one could be in the same total internal physical state as someone who knows that it is raining without oneself knowing that it is raining, because one is the victim of an elaborate hoax. For almost any proposition $p$, the condition that one knows $p$ is broad; likewise for other factive propositional attitudes, such as perceiving and remembering. I have defended elsewhere the claim of such factive states to be as genuinely mental as any states are.3

A broad mental condition nevertheless entails various narrow conditions. Indeed, there is a strongest narrow condition that it entails, i.e. a narrow condition that it entails and that entails every such narrow condition. To see this, let virtual-C be the condition that obtains in a case $\alpha$ iff $\mathrm{C}$ obtains in some case internally like $\alpha$. Virtual-$\mathrm{C}$ is narrow because internal likeness is transitive and symmetric. $\mathrm{C}$ entails virtual-$\mathrm{C}$ because internal likeness is reflexive. Moreover, virtual-$\mathrm{C}$ entails every narrow condition that $\mathrm{C}$ entails; for if $\mathrm{C}$ entails a narrow condition $\mathrm{D}$, and virtual-$\mathrm{C}$ obtains in a case $\alpha$, then $\mathrm{C}$ obtains in some case $\beta$ internally like $\alpha$, so $\mathrm{D}$ obtains in $\beta$ (since $\mathrm{C}$ entails $\mathrm{D}$), so $\mathrm{D}$ obtains in $\alpha$ (since $\mathrm{D}$ is narrow); hence virtual-$\mathrm{C}$ entails $\mathrm{D}$. Thus virtual-$\mathrm{C}$ is the strongest narrow condition that $\mathrm{C}$ entails.4

# 2.

Some think like this: the causing of my present action is here and now; only narrow conditions supervene on the here and now; so narrow conditions must play a privileged role in the causal explanation of action; if a causal explanation of action cites a broad mental condition, an underlying narrow condition must do the real work; we can isolate that narrow condition by subtracting from the broad mental condition the environmental accretions that make it broad; we can recover the original broad mental condition from the narrow condition by adding back those accretions.5 They conceive the original broad mental condition as the conjunction of the narrow condition and a condition as purely external as the former is purely internal—for instance, the condition that one believes truly that it is raining as the conjunction of the narrow condition that one believes that it is raining and the environmental condition that it is raining. More formally, define external likeness on the model of internal likeness: a case $\alpha$ is externally like a case $\beta$ iff the total physical state of the external environment in $\alpha$ is exactly the same as the total physical state of the external environment in $\beta$. A condition $\mathrm{C}$ is environmental iff for all cases $\alpha$ and $\beta$, if $\alpha$ is externally like $\beta$ then $\mathrm{C}$ obtains in $\alpha$ iff $\mathrm{C}$ obtains in $\beta$. In other terminology, environmental conditions supervene on or are determined by the physical state of the external environment. A condition $\mathrm{C}$ is composite iff it is the conjunction of some narrow condition $\mathrm{D}$ with some environmental condition $\mathrm{E}$. As a special case, a narrow mental condition is trivially composite, for it is the conjunction of itself with the environmental condition that holds in all cases whatsoever. $\mathrm{C}$ is prime iff it is not composite. The line of thought that began with the here-and-nowness of causation led to the conclusion that mental conditions are composite.

That line of thought is inconclusive, not least because it uses ill-defined notions of addition and subtraction. I will argue that its conclusion is false; many of the mental conditions that we attribute to each other in ordinary language are prime.

First, a preliminary exploration of the properties of primeness and compositeness. We can define precisely the narrow and environmental conditions of which a given condition, if composite, is the conjunction. For let outward-C be the condition that stands to the external as virtual-$\mathrm{C}$ stands to the internal: outward-$\mathrm{C}$ obtains in $\alpha$ iff $\mathrm{C}$ obtains in some case externally like $\alpha$. Outward-$\mathrm{C}$ is the strongest environmental condition that $\mathrm{C}$ entails. $\mathrm{C}$, if composite, is the conjunction of virtual-$\mathrm{C}$ and outward-$\mathrm{C}$. For let $\mathrm{C}$ be the conjunction $\mathrm{D}\wedge \mathrm{E}$ of a narrow condition $\mathrm{D}$ and an environmental condition $\mathrm{E}$. Since $\mathrm{C}$ entails $\mathrm{D}$, virtual-$\mathrm{C}$ entails $\mathrm{D}$ by §1; similarly, outward-$\mathrm{C}$ entails $\mathrm{E}$. Thus the conjunction of virtual-$\mathrm{C}$ and outward-$\mathrm{C}$ entails $\mathrm{D}\wedge \mathrm{E}$, i.e. $\mathrm{C}$. Conversely, $\mathrm{C}$ entails the conjunction of virtual-$\mathrm{C}$ and outward-$\mathrm{C}$, whether or not $\mathrm{C}$ is composite. Consequently, $\mathrm{C}$, if composite, is the conjunction of virtual-$\mathrm{C}$ and outward-$\mathrm{C}$. To argue that $\mathrm{C}$ is prime is in effect to argue that $\mathrm{C}$ can fail to obtain when both virtual-$\mathrm{C}$ and outward-$\mathrm{C}$ obtain.

How can we show that a condition $\mathrm{C}$ is prime? Suppose that $\mathrm{C}$ obtains in two cases $\alpha$ and $\beta$. Consider a case $\gamma$ internally like $\alpha$ but externally like $\beta$; we can assume that such a case is possible because otherwise interdependence of the internal and the external would itself undermine the idea that they can be separated. Now suppose that $\mathrm{C}$ is the conjunction of a narrow condition $\mathrm{D}$ with an environmental condition $\mathrm{E}$. Then $\mathrm{C}$ obtains in $\gamma$. For since $\mathrm{C}$ entails $\mathrm{D}$, $\mathrm{D}$ obtains in $\alpha$; since $\mathrm{D}$ is narrow, $\mathrm{D}$ also obtains in $\gamma$, which is internally like $\alpha$. Similarly, since $\mathrm{C}$ entails $\mathrm{E}$, $\mathrm{E}$ obtains in $\beta$; since $\mathrm{E}$ is environmental, $\mathrm{E}$ also obtains in $\gamma$, which is externally like $\beta$. Since $\mathrm{C}$ obtains whenever both $\mathrm{D}$ and $\mathrm{E}$ obtain, and they both obtain in $\gamma$, $\mathrm{C}$ obtains in $\gamma$, as required. Thus we can show that $\mathrm{C}$ is prime simply by exhibiting three cases $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$ such that $\gamma$ is internally like $\alpha$ and externally like $\beta$ and $\mathrm{C}$ obtains in $\alpha$ and $\beta$ but not in $\gamma$.6 We can do that for most ordinary mental conditions.

A picture may help. The horizontal axis represents total internal physical states; the vertical axis represents physical states of the external environment. A structural analogy may also clarify what is going on. Suppose that a property $\mathrm{P}$ is the conjunction of a colour property $\mathrm{Co}$ and a shape property $\mathrm{Sh}$, and that both a black sphere and a white cube have $\mathrm{P}$. Then a black cube also has $\mathrm{P}$: it has $\mathrm{Co}$ because it is the same colour as the black sphere, which has $\mathrm{Co}$, and it has $\mathrm{Sh}$ because it is the same shape as the white cube, which has $\mathrm{Sh}$. By contraposition, if a black sphere and a white cube have a property that a black cube lacks, that property is not the conjunction of a colour property and a shape property.

In this way, as a first example, we can argue that the condition that one sees water is prime. Let $\alpha$ be a case in which one sees water normally with one’s right eye; one’s left eye receives light rays that by chance are like those it would receive from water, but they are all emitted by a waterless device just in front of that eye; however, a brain injury prevents further processing of input from one’s left eye. Let $\beta$ be a case which differs from $\alpha$ by reversing the roles of the two eyes; in $\beta$, one sees water normally with one’s left eye; one’s right eye receives light rays that by chance are like those it would receive from water, but they are all emitted by a waterless device just in front of that eye; however, a brain injury prevents further processing of input from one’s right eye. Now consider a case $\gamma$ internally like $\alpha$ and externally like $\beta$. In $\gamma$, a brain injury prevents further processing of input from one’s left eye, because it does so in $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ is internally like $\alpha$. Equally, in $\gamma$, one’s right eye does not receive light rays from water, because it does not do so in $\beta$ and $\gamma$ is externally like $\beta$. Thus, in $\gamma$, neither eye both receives light rays from water and has its input to the brain subject to further processing. Consequently, in $\gamma$, one does not see water. Yet, in $\alpha$ and $\beta$, one does see water. By the earlier argument, the condition that one sees water is prime. Obviously, for almost any $x$, the example can be modified to show that the condition that one sees $x$ is prime; it is not the conjunction of a narrow condition and an environmental condition. Extensions to other senses are not hard to envisage.

We can demonstrate the primeness of the condition that one believes that this screen flickers by substituting this screen for water in the foregoing example. For we can assume that in $\alpha$ and $\beta$ one’s belief that this screen flickers concerns this screen only in virtue of one’s visual link to this screen; in $\gamma$, since one fails to see this screen, one lacks the belief. The point generalizes to other object-dependent contents and other propositional attitudes.

Take next the condition that one believes that tigers growl. To make the example as perspicuous as possible, assume that one believes propositions by storing sentences that mean them in a belief box; it should be clear that nothing in the underlying structure of the example requires a language of thought or a belief box. Let $\alpha$ be a case in which the language of thought has a word $\mathrm{T1}$ that means tigers (and therefore has the appropriate causal connections with tigers) and a word $\mathrm{G}$ that means growl; one believes that tigers growl because one stores the sentence $\mathrm{T1G}$ in one’s belief box; one’s language of thought also has a word $\mathrm{T2}$ that does not mean tigers, because it lacks the appropriate causal connections with tigers; one does not store the sentence $\mathrm{T2G}$ in one’s belief box. Let $\beta$ be a case which differs from $\alpha$ by reversing the roles of $\mathrm{T1}$ and $\mathrm{T2}$; in $\beta$, $\mathrm{T2}$ means tigers (and therefore has the appropriate causal connections with tigers) and $\mathrm{G}$ still means growl; one believes that tigers growl because one stores $\mathrm{T2G}$ in one’s belief box; one’s language of thought still has the word $\mathrm{T1}$, but in $\beta$ it does not mean tigers, because it lacks the appropriate causal connections with tigers; one does not store $\mathrm{T1G}$ in one’s belief box. Now consider a case $\gamma$ internally like $\alpha$ and externally like $\beta$. In $\gamma$, one does not store $\mathrm{T2G}$ in one’s belief box, because one does not do so in $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ is internally like $\alpha$. Equally, in $\gamma$, $\mathrm{T1G}$ does not mean that tigers growl, because $\mathrm{T1}$ does not mean tigers, since $\mathrm{T1}$ lacks the appropriate causal connections with tigers in $\beta$ and $\gamma$ is externally like $\beta$. Thus, in $\gamma$, neither $\mathrm{T1G}$ nor $\mathrm{T2G}$ both means that tigers growl and is stored in the belief box. We can legitimately assume that in none of the three cases does any sentence in the language of thought other than $\mathrm{T1G}$ and $\mathrm{T2G}$ mean that tigers growl. Consequently, in $\gamma$, one stores no sentence that means that tigers growl in one’s belief box; one therefore fails to believe that tigers growl. Yet, in $\alpha$ and $\beta$, one does believe that tigers growl. Thus the condition that one believes that tigers growl is prime. Again, the point generalizes to other externally individuated contents and other propositional attitudes.

We can also argue that epistemic conditions are also prime. The previous example might even suffice, for in some cases $\alpha$ and $\beta$ of the specified kind one knows that tigers growl; in $\gamma$, one fails to know that tigers growl because one fails to believe that tigers growl. However, it is more illuminating to pick an example in which the belief is held constant and what varies is its epistemic status. Let $\alpha$ be a case in which one knows by testimony that the election was rigged; Smith tells one, he is trustworthy and one trusts him; Brown also tells one that the election was rigged, but he is not trustworthy and one does not trust him. Let $\beta$ be a case which differs from $\alpha$ by reversing the roles of Smith and Brown; in $\beta$, one knows by testimony that the election was rigged; Brown tells one, he is trustworthy and one trusts him; Smith also tells one that the election was rigged, but he is not trustworthy and one does not trust him. Now consider a case $\gamma$ internally like $\alpha$ and externally like $\beta$. In $\gamma$, one does not trust Brown, because one does not trust him in $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ is internally like $\alpha$. Equally, in $\gamma$, Smith is not trustworthy, because he is not trustworthy in $\beta$ and $\gamma$ is externally like $\beta$. Thus, in $\gamma$, neither Smith nor Brown is both trustworthy and trusted. We can legitimately assume that in none of the three cases does one have any other way of knowing that the election was rigged. Consequently, in $\gamma$, one does not know that the election was rigged. Yet, in $\alpha$ and $\beta$, one does know that the election was rigged. Thus the condition that one knows that the election was rigged is prime. Since the example does not turn on the specific content of the knowledge, it can be modified to show for almost any proposition $p$ that the condition that one knows $p$ is prime.

The common pattern in the examples should now be clear. A mental state can be realized in different ways, each of which has both internal and external necessary conditions; in $\alpha$, one way has both its internal and its external condition met while the other has neither condition met; $\beta$ reverses the two ways in status; in $\gamma$, the recombination of internal features of $\alpha$ with external features of $\beta$ means that neither way has both its conditions met. Endless examples can be constructed to this pattern. I will henceforth assume that mental conditions are characteristically prime; they are not conjunctions of narrow conditions and environmental conditions.

# 3.

Do concepts of prime conditions serve any theoretical purpose? In the examples that demonstrate primeness, what is the point of classifying case $\gamma$ separately from cases $\alpha$ and $\beta$? I will argue that we need concepts of prime conditions for the same reasons for which we need concepts of broad conditions generally.

Consider seeing. What is the point of classifying a case in which one sees water separately from a case in which one is in exactly the same internal physical state but sees only a mirage? The difference may not matter for one’s action at the next instant, if the action is itself individuated by its internal physical nature (if it were individuated broadly, we should still be wondering about the point of broad individuation). But our interest is not confined to action at the next instant (if there is one; if time is dense, there is not). One is thirsty; how likely is one to be drinking soon? Likely enough, if one sees water. Much less likely, if what one sees is a mirage. Even if drinking is individuated narrowly, its explanation in terms of the earlier state of the system involves the presence of water in the environment, not just the earlier internal physical state of the agent. Concepts of broad mental conditions give us a better understanding of connections between present states and actions in the non-immediate future, because the connections involve interaction with the environment.

The need to think about connections between earlier mental states and later actions is largely a need to think about connections between mental states and actions separated by seconds, days or years. The causal explanation of action is frequently concerned with the structure of the agent’s deliberation. But deliberation frequently occurs some time before the moment of action. In deliberating, one assesses alternative courses of action in the light of one’s beliefs and desires, decides which is best, and forms the intention to pursue it; one puts the intention into effect only when the time for action comes. How and whether one puts the intention into effect depend on one’s interaction with the environment in the intervening period. At the moment of action, one may not even remember one’s deliberations in any detail. To confine the explanation of action to the instant before action is to omit much of what makes action rational. Historical explanation is certainly not confined to the instant before action; ‘Why did Napoleon invade Russia?’ is not a question about his state an instant before the invasion began, after months of planning.7 Moreover, most actions take time; one cannot instantaneously eat an apple, write a letter or go for a walk. Extended actions involve complex interaction with the environment.

Could we analyse each action into basic physical actions, and then explain each basic action in terms of the agent’s internal state at the preceding instant? Conjoining those proximal explanations of the basic actions would not yield a good explanation of the original non-basic action. Suppose, for example, that we wish to explain why someone went for a walk. Perhaps we can analyse the walk into a sequence of steps. But for each step, the proximal explanation of his taking it will not mention what explains why he went for a walk: he desired exercise.

For the reasons for which we need concepts of broad conditions, we need concepts of prime conditions. The relevance of seeing water now to drinking soon is not exhausted by the agent’s internal state and the presence of water. Before one can drink the water, one must get oneself to it. Typically, one will steer one’s way by keeping the water in sight and making a complex series of adjustments to one’s position in a feedback loop. The present coincidence of one’s internal physical state with the state one would be in if one saw the water from that perspective is not enough; the coincidence must continue until one reaches the water. The kind of causal relation in which one stands to something when one sees it often enables one to keep it in sight. By contrast, if the matching of internal state and external environment is mere coincidence, there is no reason why it should continue. We can find just this contrast in the cases that demonstrated the primeness of the condition that one sees water. Other things being equal, one can keep the water in sight with one’s right eye in case $\alpha$ and with one’s left eye in case $\beta$; in case $\gamma$, one has no means of maintaining the match between internal state and external environment. The match might continue, but that would be good luck.8 Thus the need to understand the connection between present states and action in the non-immediate future gives us reason to classify case $\gamma$ separately from cases $\alpha$ and $\beta$. To classify in that way is to use a concept of a certain prime condition.

We should not expect such considerations to yield a definition of seeing. Attempts to provide non-circular necessary and sufficient conditions for ordinary concepts have a miserable record of failure. The concept of seeing is the resultant of very many forces. The forces considered here make the concept of seeing a concept of a prime condition; they need not determine that condition uniquely.

The argument generalizes to other prime conditions. Sometimes, the content of a propositional attitude depends on a perceptual link to an object, as when one believes that this screen flickers; the foregoing considerations apply immediately. At other times, our thought of an individual or kind depends on a causal link independent of present perception. Nevertheless, such a link is typically renewable: it enables us to have further causal interactions with the individual or kind, or at least further causal dependencies on it if it no longer exists, for example by finding out more about it and acting on that information (none of which implies that reference could be defined in terms of renewable causal links). Of the cases that demonstrated the primeness of the condition that one believes that tigers growl, such renewability is likely to be available in $\alpha$ and $\beta$ but not in $\gamma$. Thus the need to understand the connection between present states and action in the non-immediate future gives us reason to use concepts of conditions that are prime because the content constitutively depends on a causal link with an individual or kind.

We can extend the argument to knowledge in more detail, taking a hint from Plato. In the Meno (97A–98A), Socrates raises a question about the value of knowledge. Knowing that this road goes to Larissa is useful to you if you want to go to Larissa. But merely believing truly that this road goes to Larissa seems to be equally useful to you, for you will get there just the same. Why should we value knowledge more than mere true belief? Socrates responds by a comparison with the statues of Daedalus, which run away unless they are tethered. True beliefs are liable to be lost, unless they are so anchored that they constitute knowledge.9

What does Plato mean? Surely he recognized that mere true beliefs can be held with dogmatic confidence, and knowledge lost through forgetting. But belief can also be sensitive to evidence. One can lose a mere true belief by discovering the falsity of further beliefs on which it had been essentially based; quite often, the truth will out. One cannot lose knowledge that way, because a true belief essentially based on false beliefs does not constitute knowledge. For example, I might derive the true belief that this road goes to Larissa from the two false (but perhaps justified) beliefs that Larissa is due north and that this road goes due north; when dawn breaks in an unexpected quarter and I realize that this road goes south, without having been given any reason to doubt that Larissa is due north, I abandon the belief that this road goes to Larissa. Since that true belief was essentially based on false beliefs, it did not constitute knowledge. The case is an obvious variation on Gettier’s counterexamples to the analysis of knowledge as justified true belief.10

In other cases, a true belief not essentially based on false beliefs still fails to constitute knowledge, because misleading evidence against that true belief is rife in one’s environment, although one happens to be unaware of it oneself. For example, I might correctly classify a dog by sight as friendly; in ordinary circumstances I might thereby come to know that it is friendly. However, this one behaves in ways that, if observed, would justify the false suspicion that it is hostile. So far it happens to have behaved like that only when my back was turned, and I have not yet formed any such suspicion. But my true belief that it is friendly does not constitute knowledge, and could be lost at any moment. To know that it is friendly, I must not be surrounded by such misleading counterevidence, and my true belief must not be too vulnerable to this kind of overturning. The case is a variation on Harman’s examples of the undermining of knowledge by evidence one does not possess.11

Present knowledge is less vulnerable than mere present true belief to rational undermining by future evidence, which is not to say that it is completely invulnerable to such undermining. If your cognitive faculties are in good order, the probability of your believing $p$ tomorrow is greater conditional on your knowing $p$ today than on your merely believing $p$ truly today (i.e. believing $p$ truly without knowing $p$).12 Consequently, the probability of your believing $p$ tomorrow is greater conditional on your knowing $p$ today than on your believing $p$ truly today.13 Of course, profoundly dogmatic beliefs that are impervious to future evidence and do not constitute knowledge may be even more likely to persist than beliefs that are rationally sensitive to future evidence and do constitute knowledge, but then the subject’s cognitive faculties are not in good order. Since the difference between your present knowledge and your present true beliefs matters for predicting your future beliefs, it matters for predicting your future actions, because they will depend on your future beliefs.

The evidence that may undermine mere present belief needs time to emerge. Although the difference between knowledge and belief can make a present psychological difference—for instance, knowledge excludes various kinds of irrationality that belief does not—the present argument concerns only delayed impact, not action at the ‘next’ instant.14 We do not value knowledge more than true belief for instant gratification.

We should not expect to define knowledge in terms of persistent true belief, still less in terms of subsequent action. What the argument does suggest is that when a condition stated in non-circular terms (belief, truth, justification, causation, …) fails to be necessary and sufficient for knowledge, that divergence will yield a divergence in implications for future action; the task of stating noncircularly a condition equivalent to knowledge with respect to implications for future action is no easier than the task of stating non-circularly a condition necessary and sufficient for knowledge. Probably, both tasks are impossible.15

These considerations apply to the cases that demonstrated the primeness of epistemic conditions. In $\alpha$ and $\beta$, one knows $p$ by testimony, and one’s belief in $p$ is correspondingly stable; one has also been told $p$ by someone untrustworthy whom one distrusts, but that does not make one’s belief less stable, for it does not rest on that testimony. In $\gamma$, one believes $p$ because one trusts the untrustworthy informant who tells one $p$; one distrusts the trustworthy informant who tells one $p$. One’s belief is less stable, for in the long run one may recognize the untrustworthiness of the informant on whom one relies. That one may also recognize the trustworthiness of one’s other informant is only partial compensation. The description of $\gamma$ involves a specific threat to one’s belief. The descriptions of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ involve no such specific threat; although one might come to distrust the trustworthy informant, no reason has emerged why one should. Given normal background conditions, one’s belief in $p$ is more stable in $\alpha$ and $\beta$ than in $\gamma$. The need to understand the connection between present states and action in the non-immediate future gives us reason to use concepts of prime epistemic conditions.

# 4.

Some may nevertheless claim that prime conditions are theoretically redundant. For let $\alpha$ be any case in which a prime condition $\mathrm{C}$ obtains, and $\mathrm{D}$ and $\mathrm{E}$ respectively the strongest narrow condition and the strongest environmental condition that obtain in $\alpha$. Then it is plausible that the conjunction $\mathrm{D}\wedge \mathrm{E}$ entails $\mathrm{C}$. For $\mathrm{D}$ completely specifies the internal physical state of the subject, and $\mathrm{E}$ completely specifies the physical state of the rest of the world, so, unless some physical relations fail to qualify as either internal or external (e.g. for holistic reasons), $\mathrm{D}\wedge \mathrm{E}$ completely determines the physical state of the world in $\alpha$, and if the total state of the world supervenes on its total physical state, then $\mathrm{D}\wedge \mathrm{E}$ entails $\mathrm{C}$. Although the assumptions just made are not uncontroversial, let us allow them for the sake of argument. Thus, in any particular case, all the consequences we want of a prime condition follow from a compound condition obtaining in that case. Why should our best theory bother with the prime condition?

Let $\mathrm{F}$ be the condition that one subsequently performs a certain action. Suppose that $\mathrm{F}$ obtains in $\alpha$; we want to know why. If the prime condition $\mathrm{C}$ makes $\mathrm{F}$ highly probable, given background conditions, we are tempted to cite $\mathrm{C}$ in explaining why $\mathrm{F}$ obtains. But $\mathrm{D}\wedge \mathrm{E}$ presumably makes $\mathrm{F}$ certain, so why not cite $\mathrm{D}\wedge \mathrm{E}$ rather than $\mathrm{C}$? Doesn’t it give the real causal explanation?

Our best theory is intended to capture significant generalizations. The action would have been performed in many cases other than $\alpha$, in which $\mathrm{D}\wedge \mathrm{E}$ does not obtain; $\mathrm{D}\wedge \mathrm{E}$ is sufficient but nothing like necessary for $\mathrm{F}$. A theory that relies on conditions like $\mathrm{D}\wedge \mathrm{E}$ may leave uncaptured a significant generalization relating $\mathrm{F}$ to $\mathrm{C}$. What has not been shown is that significant generalizations about prime conditions can be replaced by significant generalizations about compound conditions.

Good explanations have an appropriate generality. If one cites a sufficient condition for the condition to be explained, or one near enough so for the purpose in hand, the purported explanation can nevertheless fail because the condition to be explained would still have obtained in the same way even if the cited condition had not obtained. For example, one can explain why someone died by saying that he was run over by a bus; the explanation becomes worse, not better, if one specifies that the bus was red, for its colour had nothing to do with his death. If all metals have a certain property, one will be unhappy with attempts to explain why gold has it that cite properties of gold not shared by other metals. Again, if a condition obtains necessarily, then to explain why it obtains by deriving it from conditions that obtain only contingently is to miss its modal generality (as conventionalist explanations characteristically do).16

Many features of the maximally specific condition $\mathrm{D}\wedge \mathrm{E}$ will be quite irrelevant to the obtaining of $\mathrm{F}$. They will concern physical events that form no part of the causal chain between the agent’s initial mental state and the final performance of the action. The agent would have performed the action anyway, even if those features had been different. Their inclusion is a defect in the explanation. A highly specific account may constitute a good explanation of how something happened without constituting a good explanation of why it happened.17

Reductionist strategies of explanation risk providing bad explanations by citing highly specific conditions and thereby missing the generality of the conditions to be explained. Successful reductions involve no loss of generality. Something common to all genuine instances of the given phenomenon is identified in lower-level terms. The present argument does not undermine the explanatory value of those reductions. But that value is not shared by explanations that use no such generalization about the phenomenon, and merely provide or rather gesture towards a maximally specific description in lower-level terms of the particular case at hand.

Defences of narrow content often treat the total state of the external environment (‘circumstances’, ‘context’) as one component of the favoured explanations of action, the other comprising attitudes to narrow contents. On the face of it, this is to give up generality across different states of the environment. Yet such accounts do not allow the internal component of the explanation to be similarly unarticulated; the condition that one has a certain attitude to a certain ‘narrow content’ is supposed to be a general one, obtaining in a range of different cases. Once generality is acknowledged as an explanatory virtue, the question arises whether it can best be achieved by explanations that factorize in the envisaged way.

We need concepts of prime conditions to achieve appropriate generality in explaining action. Consider again the cases that demonstrated primeness. $\alpha$ and $\beta$ were mutually symmetric; the best explanation of the agent’s subsequent actions might well generalize across $\alpha$ and $\beta$, citing a condition that obtains in both. But if it also obtained in $\gamma$, the explanation would be weakened, for then the cited condition would not rule out a range of cases in which the agent’s subsequent actions in $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are much less likely (see §3). Thus the cited condition should obtain in $\alpha$ and $\beta$ but not in $\gamma$, and therefore be prime.

Of course, generality is only one of many explanatory virtues. Some purported explanations achieve spurious generality by using disjunctive concepts. For example, if someone was crying because she was bereaved, it does not improve the explanation to say that she was crying because she was bereaved or chopping onions. But ordinary mental concepts of prime conditions (such as the concept of seeing) are not disjunctive. To argue that such concepts do not express genuine common properties on grounds of explanatory uselessness would be viciously circular, for such concepts give generality to our explanations; unless they are already assumed not to express common properties, nothing has been done to undermine their apparent explanatory usefulness.

When we explain why a condition $\mathrm{C}$ obtains by citing a prior condition $\mathrm{D}$, the generality of our explanation varies inversely with $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{D}]$, the probability of $\mathrm{C}$ conditional on $\neg \mathrm{D}$; in that sense, we lack generality to the degree to which $\mathrm{D}$ fails to be necessary for $\mathrm{C}$.18 The converse explanatory virtue is sufficiency, which varies with $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]$, the probability of $\mathrm{C}$ conditional on $\mathrm{D}$. We can combine these into a single explanatory virtue, the degree to which $\mathrm{C}$ is correlated with $\mathrm{D}$. The higher the correlation, the better the answer to the question ‘Does $\mathrm{D}$ obtain?’ as a guide to the answer to the question ‘Will $\mathrm{C}$ obtain?’; correlation is also a predictive virtue.

Correlation is itself only one of many explanatory virtues, but it is the one of present interest. I will expound a more rigorous framework for discussing it, and then apply the framework to the use of prime conditions in the causal explanation of action.

# 5.

In probability theory, the standard measure of correlation is the correlation coefficient, which takes values between $+1$ (perfect positive correlation) and $-1$ (perfect negative correlation; the Appendix gives technical details). Formally, this coefficient measures the correlation between random variables, which themselves take numerical values. For present purposes, what matter are correlations between conditions. We can adapt the standard concept in a natural way to speak of the correlation coefficient of two conditions by associating each condition with its indicator random variable, which takes the value 1 when the condition obtains and 0 otherwise, and defining the correlation coefficient of two conditions as the correlation coefficient of their associated indicator random variables. The coefficient $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]$ of correlation between the conditions $\mathrm{C}$ and $\mathrm{D}$ can then be calculated in terms of their probabilities and that of their conjunction. The result is a slightly unperspicuous formula:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\wedge \mathrm{D}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{D}]}{√(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}](1-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}])\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{D}](1-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{D}]))}
$$

The probabilities here are objective properties (chances) of the conditions, for the degree to which two conditions are correlated is an objective matter. But they are not single-case probabilities, for conditions are general, like properties; they can obtain in many actual cases. The relevant probability space comprises both actual and merely possible cases; they will be circumscribed by a set of background conditions, which vary with the explanatory context.

One can easily check that $\mathrm{C}$ and $\mathrm{D}$ are positively correlated ($\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]>0$) iff the probability $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]$ of $\mathrm{C}$ conditional on $\mathrm{D}$ exceeds the unconditional probability $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]$ of $\mathrm{C}$ (Appendix, Proposition 2): one condition raises the probability of the other. The conditions are perfectly positively correlated ($\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]=1$) iff $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]=1$ and $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{D}]=0$ (Appendix, Proposition 5): $\mathrm{C}$ is certain to obtain if $\mathrm{D}$ obtains and certain not to obtain if $\mathrm{D}$ does not obtain, so whether $\mathrm{C}$ obtains can be predicted with certainty on the basis of whether $\mathrm{D}$ obtains.

In explaining action, our concern is with imperfect positive correlations ($0<\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]<1$). For example, the condition that one will perform a certain action in the future may be imperfectly positively correlated with both the condition that one presently knows some proposition and the condition that one believes that proposition truly (we can treat the relevant desire as a background condition). The question was: with which of the latter two conditions is the former condition better correlated? More generally, which of two conditions $\mathrm{D}$ and $\mathrm{E}$, both positively correlated with $\mathrm{C}$, is more highly correlated with $\mathrm{C}$? It turns out that $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]\leq\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{E}]$ iff

$$
(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}])(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{D}])
\leq
(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{E}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}])(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{E}])
$$

(Proposition 3).

Thus both the degree to which $\mathrm{D}$ helps us to predict $\mathrm{C}$ ($\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]$, the degree to which $\mathrm{D}$ raises the probability of $\mathrm{C}$) and the degree to which $\neg \mathrm{D}$ helps us to predict $\neg \mathrm{C}$ ($\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{D}]$, the degree to which $\neg \mathrm{D}$ lowers the probability of $\mathrm{C}$) are relevant. In particular, if $\mathrm{E}$ raises the probability of $\mathrm{C}$ more than $\mathrm{D}$ does and $\neg \mathrm{E}$ lowers the probability of $\mathrm{C}$ more than $\neg \mathrm{D}$ does, then $\mathrm{C}$ is better correlated with $\mathrm{E}$ than with $\mathrm{D}$ (Proposition 4).

A completely schematic example may clarify the picture. I will focus on knowledge, but similar points can be made about other prime conditions. Let $\mathrm{C}$ be the condition that one will perform a certain action, $\mathrm{D}$ the condition that one believes truly a proposition $p$, and $\mathrm{E}$ the condition that one knows $p$. The background conditions can include the agent’s desires and the truth of $p$. Suppose that just three equiprobable possibilities have nonzero probability: one believes truly and knows $p$ and will perform the action; one believes $p$ truly without knowing $p$ and will not perform the action; one neither believes truly nor knows $p$ and will not perform the action. Hence $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\wedge \mathrm{D}\wedge \mathrm{E}]=\mathrm{P}[\neg \mathrm{C}\wedge \mathrm{D}\wedge\neg \mathrm{E}]=\mathrm{P}[\neg \mathrm{C}\wedge\neg \mathrm{D}\wedge\neg \mathrm{E}]=1/3$. Thus it is certain that one will perform the action iff one knows $p$; $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{E}]=1$ and $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{E}]=0$. The two conditions are perfectly positively correlated; $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{E}]=1$. It is also certain that one will perform the action only if one believes $p$ truly, so $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{D}]=0$. However, if one believes $p$ truly, one may or may not perform the action, depending on whether one knows $p$; $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]=1/2$. These two conditions are imperfectly positively correlated; calculation shows that $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]=1/2$. Realistic examples have none of this simplicity. But since the correlation coefficient is a continuous function of the relevant probabilities, small enough changes in the latter make small changes in the former. Thus we can introduce some of the messy complexity of real life into the example and still have performing the action better correlated with knowing than with believing truly ($\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]<\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{E}]$). In particular, this comparative ranking is consistent with nonzero probabilities for the possibilities that one performs the action without knowing $p$, with or without believing $p$ truly ($\mathrm{C}\wedge \mathrm{D}\wedge\neg \mathrm{E}$ and $\mathrm{C}\wedge\neg \mathrm{D}\wedge\neg \mathrm{E}$) and that one fails to perform it while knowing $p$ ($\neg \mathrm{C}\wedge \mathrm{D}\wedge \mathrm{E}$). Although in most realistic examples we cannot expect to calculate exact probabilities or correlation coefficients, such comparative rankings can still be plausible. In very crude terms, if the probability of performing the action conditional on knowing $p$ exceeds the probability of performing it conditional on believing $p$ truly without knowing $p$ by much more than the latter exceeds the probability of performing it conditional on failing to believe $p$ truly, then performing the action is more highly correlated with knowing $p$ than with believing $p$ truly.19

Action is often more highly correlated with belief or with true belief than with knowledge. But not always. You see someone coming to your door; he is about to knock loudly. You are tempted not to reply. How would he react? You ask yourself ‘Does he know that I am in?’, not ‘Does he believe that I am in?’. If before knocking he does know that you are in, he is unlikely to abandon his belief if you fail to reply; he will probably take offence. If before knocking he believes (truly) without knowing that you are in, he is much more likely to abandon his belief if you fail to reply; he will probably not take offence. If before knocking he fails even to believe that you are in, he is even less likely to take offence. Whether he would take offence is better predicted by whether he knows than by whether he believes. His taking offence is more highly correlated with knowing that you are in than with believing (truly) that you are in.

The point is not that knowing exceeds believing in implied degree of confidence; it need not. I know many things without being prepared to bet my house on them. The example works even if the degree of confidence required for belief is stipulated to be the same as the degree of confidence required for knowledge. The visitor who merely believes (truly) when he knocks that you are in may be exceedingly confident that you are in, but abandon the belief when to his astonishment you do not reply, for he is even more confident that if you do not reply you are not in.20 But someone who knows that you are in has grounds that will not be undermined just by your failure to reply. Clearly, all this is a matter of probability; if your visitor merely believes that you are in, he may retain the belief when you do not reply, and take offence; equally, if he knows that you are in, he may abandon the belief in a fit of self-doubt when you do not reply. Nevertheless, the probabilities are often as indicated above.

Consider another example. How long would we expect a fox to be willing to search for a rabbit in the wood before giving up, assuming that initially (a) the fox knows that there is a rabbit in the wood, or (b) the fox believes truly that there is a rabbit in the wood? In (b) but not (a), the fox’s initial true belief may fail to constitute knowledge because the true belief is essentially based on a false one, for instance a false belief that there is a rabbit in a certain hole in the wood. When the fox discovers the falsity of that belief, the reason for the search disappears. That will not happen in (a), because a true belief essentially based on a false one does not constitute knowledge. Thus, given plausible background conditions, more persistence is to be expected in (a) than in (b). In many such cases, lengthy persistence is better explained by initial knowledge than by initial true belief.21

Sometimes the predictive difference between knowledge and true belief is mediated by the cultural significance of knowledge. A notorious criminal may try to eliminate all those who know that he killed the policeman, because they are potential witnesses against him in court. He will not bother to eliminate those who merely believe truly that he did it, because their confidence that he did it, however great, is no threat to him, given the rules of forensic evidence. If we want to predict whether someone will soon be fleeing for her life, ‘Does she know that he shot the policeman?’ is a better question than ‘Does she believe that he shot the policeman?’. It is better even than the question ‘Does she believe that she knows that he shot the policeman?’ when flight is contingent on the criminal’s behaviour and he believes that the testimony of anyone who mistakenly believes themselves to know that he did it will not stand up in court. The danger is knowing too much, not believing too much.

We can also use a schematic example to reinforce the conclusion of §4, by showing in detail how a very specific condition strictly sufficient for the condition to be explained can nevertheless fail to be highly correlated with it. Let $\mathrm{C}$ be the condition that one will perform a certain action, $\mathrm{D}$ the very specific condition obtaining in the case at hand (e.g. completely determining the agent’s internal physical state and the physical state of the environment), and $\mathrm{E}$ the condition that one knows $p$. The background conditions include the agent’s desires. Assume that $\mathrm{D}$ is sufficient for both $\mathrm{C}$ and $\mathrm{E}$, which leaves just five possibilities; suppose that they have these probabilities:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\wedge \mathrm{D}\wedge \mathrm{E}]=1/10\\
&\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\wedge\neg \mathrm{D}\wedge \mathrm{E}]=3/10\\
&\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\wedge\neg \mathrm{D}\wedge\neg \mathrm{E}]=1/10\\
&\mathrm{P}[\neg \mathrm{C}\wedge\neg \mathrm{D}\wedge \mathrm{E}]=1/10\\
&\mathrm{P}[\neg \mathrm{C}\wedge\neg \mathrm{D}\wedge\neg \mathrm{E}]=4/10
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus it is certain that one will perform the action if the specific condition obtains ($\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]=1$) and not certain that one will perform it if one knows $p$ ($\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{E}]=4/5$). However, one is much more likely to perform it if the specific condition does not obtain than if one does not know $p$ ($\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{D}]=4/9>1/5=\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{E}]$). The latter disparity in favour of $\mathrm{E}$ more than compensates for the former disparity in favour of $\mathrm{D}$; calculation shows that $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]=1/3<3/5=\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{E}]$. Performing the action is better correlated with knowing $p$ than with the strictly sufficient specific condition.

# 6.

A high correlation does not guarantee a direct causal connection. If the condition that one knows $p$ is highly correlated with the condition that one will perform a certain action, the reason might be that both the knowledge and the action are effects of a common cause, without the knowledge causing the action. What would it be for the knowledge not to cause the action? Presumably, the condition that one has the knowledge would not be causally relevant in the right sense to the condition that one will perform the action. But then we should not focus on the former in explaining why the latter obtains. Does this seriously threaten the role of prime conditions in the explanation of action?

High correlations are an indispensable though fallible guide to causal structure. Where a high correlation misleads us into falsely postulating a causal connection, more detailed information about further correlations should correct our mistake. The high correlations between prime mental conditions and conditions on subsequent action constitute defeasible evidence for the causal effectiveness of the prime conditions. Higher correlations constituting a genuinely rival explanation would be needed to defeat that evidence.

Given deterministic laws, we might define a present condition $\mathrm{D}$ perfectly correlated with the condition $\mathrm{C}$ that one will perform the action, by stipulating that $\mathrm{D}$ obtains in a case $\alpha$ iff the total present state of the system (agent and environment) in $\alpha$ and the deterministic laws entail that one will perform the action. $\mathrm{C}$ and $\mathrm{D}$ obtain in exactly the same nomically possible cases. Thus, if the laws have probability 1, $\mathrm{C}$ and $\mathrm{D}$ are perfectly correlated (if $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]>0$; otherwise $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]$ is ill-defined). $\mathrm{D}$ is not defined disjunctively. However, the definition of $\mathrm{D}$ unifies the cases in which $\mathrm{D}$ obtains by what happens later (the performance of the action), not by the present state of the system. In many contexts, such a correlation will not give us the kind of understanding we seek (it certainly does not give us what we need for purposes of prediction and control, but that is not quite the same thing). We seek a correlation between a condition given by a concept that unifies the cases in which it obtains in terms of the present state of the system and a condition given by a concept that unifies the cases in which it obtains in terms of the future state of the system; we are willing to sacrifice some degree of correlation in order to achieve such unification.

Even if we can replace the conditions conceived by folk psychology by conditions more highly correlated than they are with the condition that one subsequently performs the action, those new conditions will themselves be prime (as $\mathrm{D}$ is above), for reasons already indicated. Moreover, such explanations may well constitute refinements rather than refutations of the folk psychological explanations.

Discussions of broadness have tended to concentrate on intentional content. Since intentional content is a mental phenomenon, the causal efficacy of broad conditions, and specifically of prime conditions, can appear to require special pleading on behalf of the mental. It does not. The considerations of this paper are not confined to the mental. For example, one can demonstrate in the style of §2 the primeness of the condition that a ship is anchored to the seabed; it is not the conjunction of a condition on the internal physical state of the ship and a condition on the physical state of its external environment. Clearly, the condition that a ship is anchored to the seabed can be causally effective with respect to the ship’s subsequent motion or rest. Primeness is no bar to causal efficacy. It derives its significance from our interest in causal-explanatory connections between states of objects and their subsequent behaviour (in the widest sense) after an interval long enough to permit intervening interaction with their environment. That is the normal case, not the exception, in causal explanation.

# Appendix: Correlation Coefficients

Given real-valued random variables $\mathrm{X}$ and $\mathrm{Y}$ whose arguments are conditions (‘events’) in a suitable probability space, their correlation coefficient $\rho[\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}]$ is defined as $\operatorname{Cov}[\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}]/(\sigma[\mathrm{X}]\sigma[\mathrm{Y}])$; their covariance $\operatorname{Cov}[\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}]$ is in turn defined as $\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{XY}]-\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{X}]\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{Y}]$, where $\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{X}]$ is the expectation of $\mathrm{X}$; the standard deviation $\sigma[\mathrm{X}]$ is defined as $√ \mathrm{E}[(\mathrm{X}-\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{X}])^2]$ (e.g. Parzen 1960: 362). Covariance is not itself an adequate measure of correlation; for example, any random variable is perfectly correlated with itself, but $\operatorname{Cov}[\mathrm{X},\mathrm{X}]=\sigma[\mathrm{X}]^2$, which varies. One must calibrate $\operatorname{Cov}[\mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}]$ by dividing by $\sigma[\mathrm{X}]\sigma[\mathrm{Y}]$.

All these notions can be relativized to a set of background conditions by conditionalizing the underlying probabilities on those conditions.

We must now define correlation coefficients for the conditions on which the probabilities are defined. Denote the probability of a condition $\mathrm{C}$ as $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]$. The indicator random variable $\mathrm{X}(\mathrm{C})$ is 1 if $\mathrm{C}$ obtains and 0 otherwise; thus $\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{X}(\mathrm{C})]=\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]$. For any conditions $\mathrm{C}$ and $\mathrm{D}$ we define their correlation coefficient $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]$ (with harmless ambiguity in $\rho$) as $\rho[\mathrm{X}(\mathrm{C}),\mathrm{X}(\mathrm{D})]$. We will calculate an expression for $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]$ in terms of probabilities, and then derive some elementary facts about it. The conditional probability $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]$ is $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\wedge \mathrm{D}]/\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{D}]$ ($0<\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{D}]$).

We assume that $0<\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]<1$, and similarly for $\mathrm{D}$ and $\mathrm{E}$, otherwise the correlation coefficients for these states are not well-defined.

Proposition 1.

$$\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]=(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\wedge \mathrm{D}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{D}])/√(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}](1-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}])\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{D}](1-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{D}])).$$

Proof.

$$\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Cov}[\mathrm{X}(\mathrm{C}),\mathrm{X}(\mathrm{D})]
&=\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{X}(\mathrm{C})\mathrm{X}(\mathrm{D})]-\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{X}(\mathrm{C})]\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{X}(\mathrm{D})]\\
&=\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{X}(\mathrm{C}\wedge \mathrm{D})]-\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{X}(\mathrm{C})]\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{X}(\mathrm{D})]\\
&=\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\wedge \mathrm{D}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{D}].
\end{aligned}$$

Moreover,

$$\begin{aligned}
\sigma[\mathrm{X}(\mathrm{C})]
&=√ \mathrm{E}[(\mathrm{X}(\mathrm{C})-\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{X}(\mathrm{C})])^2]\\
&=√(\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{X}(\mathrm{C})^2]-(\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{X}(\mathrm{C})])^2) &&\text{by a standard calculation}\\
&=√(\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{X}(\mathrm{C})]-(\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{X}(\mathrm{C})])^2) &&\text{because }\mathrm{X}(\mathrm{C})\in\{0,1\}\\
&=√(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}](1-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}])).
\end{aligned}$$

Proposition 2.

(a)
If $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]>\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]$ then $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]>0$.
(b)
If $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]=\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]$ then $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]=0$.
(c)
If $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]<\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]$ then $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]<0$.

Proof. By Proposition 1, $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]$ has the same sign as $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\wedge \mathrm{D}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{D}]=(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}])\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{D}]$.

Proposition 3.

(a)
If $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]\geq 0$ and $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{E}]\geq 0$, then $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]\leq\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{E}]$ iff
$(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}])(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{D}])\leq(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{E}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}])(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{E}])$.
(b)
If $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]\leq 0$ and $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{E}]\leq 0$, then $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]\leq\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{E}]$ iff
$(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}])(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{D}])\geq(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{E}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}])(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{E}])$.

Proof. (a)

$$\begin{aligned}
(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\wedge \mathrm{D}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{D}])^2
&=(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{D}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{D}])\\
&\quad(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\wedge\neg \mathrm{D}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{D}])\\
&=(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}])\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{D}]\\
&\quad(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}](1-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{D}])-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{D}]\mathrm{P}[\neg \mathrm{D}])\\
&=(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}])(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{D}])\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{D}](1-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{D}]).
\end{aligned}$$

Hence by Proposition 1

$$
\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]^2\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}](1-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}])
=(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}])(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{D}]).
$$

But if $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]\geq0$ and $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{E}]\geq0$, then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]\leq\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{E}]
&\Leftrightarrow \rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]^2\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}](1-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}])\leq\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{E}]^2\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}](1-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}])\\
&\Leftrightarrow (\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}])(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{D}])\\
&\quad \leq (\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{E}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}])(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{E}]).
\end{aligned}
$$

(b) is similar.

Proposition 4. If $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]\leq \mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{E}]$ and $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{D}]\geq \mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{E}]$ then $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]\leq\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{E}]$.

Proof. Suppose that $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]\leq \mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{E}]$ and $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{D}]\geq \mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{E}]$.

Case (i): $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]\leq \mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]$. Then $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]\leq \mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{E}]$, so by Proposition 2 $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]\geq0$ and $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{E}]\geq0$. Hence by Proposition 3(a) $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]\leq\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{E}]$ iff

(*)
$(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}])(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{D}])\leq(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{E}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}])(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{E}]).$

But $0\leq \mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]\leq \mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{E}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]$. Moreover since $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]=\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{D}]\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]+(1-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{D}])\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{D}]$, $0\leq \mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{D}]\leq \mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{E}]$. Hence (*) holds, so $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]\leq\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{E}]$.

Case (ii): $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]<\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]<\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{E}]$. Then $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]<0<\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{E}]$ by Proposition 2.

Case (iii): $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{E}]\leq \mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]$. Similar to case (i), using Proposition 3(b).

Proposition 5.

(a)
$\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{C}]=1$
(b)
$\rho[\mathrm{C},\neg \mathrm{C}]=-1$
(c)
$-1\leq\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]\leq1$
(d)
$\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]=1$ iff $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]=1$ and $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{D}]=0$.
(e)
$\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]=-1$ iff $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]=0$ and $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{D}]=1$.

Proof. (a) and (b) are simple consequences of Proposition 1.

(c) $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]\leq \mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{C}]$ and $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{D}]\geq \mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{C}]$, so $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]\leq\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{C}]=1$ by (a) and Proposition 4. Similarly, $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]\geq\rho[\mathrm{C},\neg \mathrm{C}]=-1$.

(d) Suppose that $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]=1$. Then $\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{C}]\leq\rho[\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}]$ by (a) and (c), and $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]>\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]$ by Proposition 2. Hence by Proposition 3

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}])(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{D}])\\
&\quad\geq (\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{C}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}])(\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{C}])\\
&\quad=(1-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}])\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}].
\end{aligned}
$$

But $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]\leq1-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]$ and $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{D}]\leq \mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]$, so $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]=1-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]$ and $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]-\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{D}]=\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]$, so $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]=1$ and $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{D}]=0$. Conversely, if $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid \mathrm{D}]=1$ and $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\mid\neg \mathrm{D}]=0$ then $\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{D}]=\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}\wedge \mathrm{D}]=\mathrm{P}[\mathrm{C}]$ and the result follows by Proposition 1.

(e) is similar to (d).
