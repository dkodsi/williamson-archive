Unreflective realism

Timothy Williamson

Philosophy and Phenomenological Research 56 (4) (1996): 905–909.

Truth and Objectivity is full of ideas. Only a few of them will be discussed here. First, I consider the book’s use of the notion of the a priori, and raise some problems for it. The trouble is then diagnosed as resulting from a preconception about semantics, one also connected with Wright’s claim that, in disputes between realists and their opponents, the burden of proof always lies on the former. To put it crudely, the preconception is that it is the responsibility of participants in a discourse to fix its status; it attains objectivity only if they do something special to bring that about. An alternative will be suggested, on which realist content can arise by default.

1. A Priori Conditions

Wright uses the notion of the a priori in making precise two central ideas in the book: the Euthyphro Contrast and Cognitive Command. I will take them in turn. The problem is not general to the use in philosophy of an unanalysed notion of the a priori; it is specific to Wright’s use.

(a) The Euthyphro Contrast. Some metaphysical disputes can be schematized by the opposing slogans ‘Because PP, best opinion has it that PP’ (realism) and ‘Because best opinion has it that PP, PP’ (anti-realism). For example: does something look red to normal observers in normal conditions because it is red, or is it red because it looks red to normal observers in normal conditions? The issue is elusive because ‘because’ is. Wright explicates the anti-realism in question as something like the view that it is a priori that in suitable conditions PP if and only if best opinion has it that PP (I omit many qualifications irrelevant to present purposes). Thus someone who is anti-realist about redness (as Wright is, 131) but realist about squareness will say that it is a priori that in suitable conditions xx is red if and only if best opinion has it that xx is red, but not a priori that in suitable conditions xx is square if and only if best opinion has it that xx is square. Wright envisages the source of the relevant a priori knowledge as reflection on concepts (117). He also suggests that it is a priori that colour supervenes on other physical properties (127); presumably this is not true of shape.

On Wright’s view, one could work out the difference in status between shape and colour by conceptual reflection alone, without empirical investigation. There was no need to wait for the scientific discoveries of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. But how could a stone age man, by conceptual reflection, have refuted the speculation that colour was as fundamental a property of things as shape, causally responsible for best opinion about it and not supervenient on other physical properties? It would not help to reply that our present colour concepts embody scientific discoveries. If stone age best opinion could detect independently constituted facts about colour, then so could present best opinion. Anti-realism about present colour terms is only as plausible as anti-realism about stone age colour terms.1

Just as Wright assumes that it is written into the semantics of a colour term that it refers to a secondary quality, so he assumes that it is written into the semantics of a natural-kind term that if it refers at all, it refers to a natural kind. It would then be a priori that such a term does not refer to a physically disunified kind (131). It follows, for any natural-kind term, that it is guaranteed a priori not to function in the way in which ‘jade’ actually functions, referring to the union of the physically disparate natural kinds (e.g. jadeite and nephrite) to which its paradigm samples belonged. There is no such guarantee. For all speakers of English know in advance of empirical investigation, ‘gold’ might function like ‘jade’.

In reference-fixing, the balance between natural divisions and best opinion is not an a priori matter. It depends on what natural divisions there happen to be in the vicinity of paradigm samples of the term.2 Wright’s account of the Euthyphro Contrast condemns the anti-realist to defeat in almost every case.

(b) Cognitive Command. Wright stipulates that a discourse exerts Cognitive Command if and only if it is a priori that differences of opinion formulated within the discourse, unless excusable as a result of vagueness in a disputed statement, or in the standards of acceptability, or variation in personal evidence thresholds, will involve something which may properly be regarded as a cognitive shortcoming (144). The intention is to rule out discourses in which there may be no fact of the matter as to who is right when opinions clash. What follows ‘it is a priori that’, in particular the phrase ‘cognitive shortcoming’, is not perfectly clear, but we may suppose that it has been explicated by a condition C.3 Thus a discourse exerts Cognitive Command if and only if it is a priori that the discourse meets C. Suppose also that C genuinely distinguishes between discourses; some meet C and others do not. The problem is that if the picture sketched under (a) is not hopelessly inaccurate, then one cannot expect to settle the status of a discourse with respect to C a priori; it will depend on what facts of the matter there happen to be in the vicinity. Hardly any discourse will exert Cognitive Command. Unfortunately, I do not have a good enough grasp of C to argue the point in detail.

2. Burdens of Proof

According to Wright, ‘the general rule should be that realism must be earned’; anti-realism is the parsimonious default stance (149). It is always pleasant to stipulate that the burden of proof lies on one’s opponent. However, the present case is not as simple as that. Wright assumes that realism must be earned by philosophers in speaking about a discourse because it must be earned by participants within the discourse. Unless they do something special to introduce realist content, their discourse will have only anti-realist content.

Wright is surely correct to this extent: if realism must be earned within the discourse, then the default stance about the discourse must be anti-realism. The presumption is laziness. But why is it realism rather than anti-realism that must be earned within the discourse? That depends on what is meant by the terms ‘realism’ and ‘anti-realism’, a question to which the book surveys a wide variety of answers. A schematic answer will do for present purposes: one is a realist about a discourse to the extent to which one thinks that truth in it extends beyond the potential for robustly warranted assertibility (superassertibility). Wright’s picture is that a discourse involves truth and falsity just in case it has standards of warranted assertibility and an appropriate syntactic structure; what takes special effort on the part of participants in the discourse is the construction of a standard of truth that outruns the conditions for the question of truth to arise in the first place. As in his use of a priori conditions, Wright relies on the participants to settle the status of their practice. I will argue, to the contrary, that standards of warranted assertibility carry with them a standard of truth that extends beyond the potential for robustly warranted assertibility, without the need of any special effort on the part of participants.

The first point to note is that the assertion that PP is warranted only if the speaker knows that PP.4 It is not enough for it to be highly probable that PP given the speaker’s evidence. For example, if I have bought one of nn tickets in a lottery, the fact that its chance of winning is only 1/n1/n does not entitle me to assert that it will not win, no matter how large nn is. Even if, to no one’s surprise, my ticket does not win, you can justly criticize my assertion by saying ‘But you didn’t know that it would win’. If one lacks knowledge that PP, while being in a state that one cannot discriminate from having such knowledge, then one lacks warrant to assert that PP, while being in a state that one cannot discriminate from having such warrant. The point extends to discourses for which anti-realism is found plausible. To take Wright’s favourite example, discourse about the funny: if I do not know that Frederick Barbarossa made some funny remarks, then I am not warranted in asserting that Frederick Barbarossa made some funny remarks. If a discourse permits warranted assertions, then it permits the expression of knowledge.

To say that a discourse permits the expression of knowledge is not immediately to vindicate realism about that discourse. One might expect a minimal anti-realist notion of truth to be associated with a minimal anti-realist notion of knowledge. However, a less direct line of thought connects knowledge and realism. To know that PP, one must be reliably right that PP.5 In taking it that PP, one is reliably right only if, in all cases that could easily arise and that one could easily fail to discriminate from the given case, it is true that PP. Reliability demands a margin for error. If ‘PP’ is known in case xx, then it is true in every case relevantly similar to xx. There is no need for ‘PP’ to be known in the latter cases, for even if they are known to be relevantly similar, ‘PP’ may not be known to be known in case xx. Indeed, ‘PP’ must sometimes be known in a case, yet unknown in relevantly similar cases, otherwise its truth-conditions would spread over every case.6 Since knowledge is the condition for warranted assertion, the cases in which there is a warrant for asserting that PP are those in which knowledge that PP is available, and these form a narrower class than the cases in which it is true that PP. The condition for warrant to assert ‘PP’ applies to a range of cases only if the truth-condition for ‘PP’ applies to a wider range of cases.

The argument can be extended from warranted assertibility to superassertibility, since the latter is the potential for robustly warranted assertibility, and therefore implies the potential for knowledge. ‘PP’ is superassertible and therefore expresses potential knowledge in a given case only if it is true in relevantly similar cases.7 Thus the superassertibility-condition for ‘PP’ applies to a range of cases only if the truth-condition for ‘PP’ applies to a wider range of cases. That is the mark of the realist conception of truth.

The gap between superassertibility and truth is not something that participants in the discourse need work at. It is not their job to specify the connections between warranted assertibility, reliability and the gap between superassertibility and truth. Their job is merely to be reliable about something sometimes. It is the theorist who points out that to make a warranted assertion is to be reliably right, and that the reliability of the assertion implies the possibility in principle of other cases in which what one actually asserted would have been true in the absence of any warrant for asserting it.

Whether superassertibility and truth diverge extensionally in a given discourse is not usually an a priori matter. Perhaps there are discourses in which participants’ powers of discrimination are so fine in the long run that the margin for error required for superassertibility is zero. However, local infallibility does nothing to vindicate the anti-realist idea that superassertibility might constitute truth. The general point remains that, in virtue of its connection with reliability, warranted assertibility carries with it a potential for truth to outrun superassertibility. Given Wright’s assumption that a discourse involves truth and falsity only if it has standards for warranted assertion, it follows that truth always carries with it the potential to outrun superassertibility.

Nothing in the argument requires participants in the discourse to earn realism; they have done enough if they earn the right to make warranted assertions. That is easy enough according to Wright. The preceding argument suggests that it involves more than one might suppose. Certainly, no a priori guarantee has been provided that there are genuine standards for warranted assertion wherever there appear to be. For all that has been said, some discourses may suffer massive reference failure, and not even get to the point of expressing contents that could be asserted, with or without warrant. Nevertheless, if a discourse appears to have genuine standards for warranted assertion, then the default presumption must be that it really has them. If it does really have them, then it automatically has the potential for truth to outrun what participants can be warranted in asserting. There is no need for the potential to be written into the semantics of the discourse.
