Review of Gillian Russell, Barriers to Entailment: Hume’s Law and Other Limits on Logical Consequence

Timothy Williamson

The Journal of Philosophy 121 (10) (2024): 592–596.

In slogan form, Hume’s Law says, “You can’t get an ought from an is.” In more abstract terms, it postulates a barrier to any entailment from descriptive premises to a normative conclusion. It seems to embody some insight. Yet it is notoriously vulnerable to counterexamples. Trivially, inconsistent premises entail anything, so in particular any inconsistent descriptive premises entail any normative conclusion. Similarly, anything entails a logical truth, so in particular any descriptive premises entail any normative logical truth. More interestingly, Arthur Prior proposed a dilemma for any descriptive sentence ‘D’ and normative sentence ‘N’. The disjunction ‘D or N’ is either descriptive or normative. If it is descriptive, then the descriptive premises ‘D or N’ and ‘Not D’ entail the normative conclusion ‘N’. On the other hand, if the disjunction is normative, then the descriptive premise ‘D’ entails the normative conclusion ‘D or N’. Either way, the supposed barrier is breached.1 Despite such barriers to barriers to entailment, in her new book Gillian Russell aims to extract a correct insight from Hume’s discussion, formulate it precisely, and show it to resist the counterexamples—indeed, prove it formally.

The counterexamples triumph if we simply demarcate the descriptive and normative sentences by the absence or presence in them of designated normative words such as ‘ought’. After all, they may occur inessentially or implicitly. A deeper semantic approach is needed.

Russell’s project is not limited to the barrier between ‘is’ and ‘ought’. She considers in equal detail barriers to entailment from particular premises to universal conclusions, from premises about the past to conclusions about the future, from premises about actuality to conclusions about necessity, and from non-indexical (context-insensitive) premises to indexical (context-sensitive) conclusions. Most of the barriers have a Humean flavor. Russell’s aim is to give them a systematic general treatment; she uses the various cases to cast light on the problem from different angles.

Russell has been pursuing the project for some time, at one stage jointly with Greg Restall; her proposed account has evolved over the years. This book is the culmination. It achieves a notable degree of success. Even those who quarrel with aspects of her approach will find formal foundations to build on, and an extensive database of formal and informal examples. The book is as reader friendly as it reasonably could be, given the trickiness of the relevant issues. Russell explains the technicalities accessibly, taking very little for granted. Anyone who wants to get up to speed on these problems could not do better than read it.

For the most part, Russell works with a standard form of logical consequence for suitably chosen formal languages: premises logically entail a conclusion just if the conclusion is true in every model where every premise is true. Wisely, she confronts the problems in their sharpest form by keeping the logic classical, not protecting the barriers by weakening the logic. As is standard, premises and conclusions are taken to be sentences, not the propositions they express.

To explain Russell’s main technical idea, some background is needed. For each proposed barrier, she defines a corresponding relation R\mathrm{R} on models, which a model M\mathrm{M} has to a model N\mathrm{N} just when M\mathrm{M} is modifiable into N\mathrm{N} in a specified way that does not affect the truth of premises of the target kind but may affect the truth of conclusions of the target kind. For the is/ought barrier, those modifications at most alter the component of the model used for the semantics of the deontic operators. For the particular/universal barrier, the modifications at most extend the domain of individuals and the extensions of atomic predicates for new individuals. For the past/future barrier, the modifications at most alter future history in the model (which has a ‘now’ moment). For the actuality/necessity barrier, the modifications at most extend the domain of worlds and add extensions of atomic predicates for the new worlds. For the indexical barrier, the modifications alter at most the agent and place of the model’s designated context. A formula φ\varphi is defined as RR-breakable just if, for some models M\mathrm{M} and N\mathrm{N}, M\mathrm{M} has R\mathrm{R} to N\mathrm{N}, and φ\varphi is true in M\mathrm{M} but false in N\mathrm{N}: that R\mathrm{R}-modification breaks the truth of φ\varphi. Naturally, φ\varphi is RR-unbreakable just if it is not R\mathrm{R}-breakable, so for any models M\mathrm{M} and N\mathrm{N}, if M\mathrm{M} has R\mathrm{R} to N\mathrm{N}, and φ\varphi is true in M\mathrm{M}, then φ\varphi is true in N\mathrm{N} too. Russell uses this dichotomy of formulas to set up her barrier principles. Thus, for the is/ought barrier and the relevant R\mathrm{R} relation, she equates descriptive formulas with R\mathrm{R}-unbreakable ones (an R\mathrm{R}-modification of the model cannot break their truth) and normative formulas with R\mathrm{R}-breakable ones (an R\mathrm{R}-modification can break their truth). The dichotomy is extended from individual formulas to sets of formulas by treating a set of formulas like the conjunction of its members.

Russell proves what she calls the ‘Limited General Barrier Theorem’, to this effect, for any relation R\mathrm{R} on models, set of formulas Γ\Gamma, and formula φ\varphi:

If Γ\Gamma is R\mathrm{R}-unbreakable and φ\varphi is R\mathrm{R}-breakable, then Γ\Gamma does not logically entail φ\varphi unless for every model M\mathrm{M} in which Γ\Gamma is true, φ\varphi is true in every model to which M\mathrm{M} has R\mathrm{R}. (190)

The ‘unless’ clause signals an exception to the barrier to entailment from R\mathrm{R}-unbreakable premises to R\mathrm{R}-breakable conclusions. The clause is needed. For an informal example, take the entailment from the descriptive ‘Joe is helping’ to ‘Joe is helping or Joe ought to be helping’. The disjunction is R\mathrm{R}-breakable (normative), since it is true in a model M\mathrm{M} where John is not helping but deontic standards are high enough to make ‘Joe ought to be helping’ true, but false in a model to which M\mathrm{M} has R\mathrm{R} where Joe is still not helping but deontic standards are low enough to make ‘Joe ought to be helping’ false. After detailed consideration of numerous variant approaches, Russell argues that friends of Hume’s Law should resign themselves to putting up with such a fiddly exception-clause.

That way of thinking about the issues may over-complicate. To develop an alternative, one can start from what Russell calls the ‘Argument Set Formulation’ of the theorem, in effect:

Let φ\varphi be an R\mathrm{R}-breakable sentence, and Γ\Gamma an R\mathrm{R}-unbreakable set of sentences. Then Γ\Gamma does not logically entail φ\varphi unless Γ∪{φ}\Gamma \cup \{\varphi\} is R\mathrm{R}-unbreakable. (207)

Russell’s condition here of φ\varphi’s R\mathrm{R}-breakability is redundant. For suppose that φ\varphi is R\mathrm{R}-unbreakable; then since Γ\Gamma is R\mathrm{R}-unbreakable, so too is Γ∪{φ}\Gamma \cup \{\varphi\}; thus, the conclusion follows anyway. We can therefore delete the condition from the Argument Set Formulation, obtaining in effect this Unqualified Argument Set Formulation:

If Γ\Gamma is R\mathrm{R}-unbreakable and Γ∪{φ}\Gamma \cup \{\varphi\} R\mathrm{R}-breakable, then Γ\Gamma does not logically entail φ\varphi.

For the is/ought barrier, this amounts to saying: if Γ\Gamma is descriptive and Γ∪{φ}\Gamma \cup \{\varphi\} normative, Γ\Gamma does not logically entail φ\varphi. That is an unqualified barrier to entailment, just with the assumption of R\mathrm{R}-breakability for the expanded set Γ∪{φ}\Gamma \cup \{\varphi\} rather than φ\varphi by itself. For the is/ought barrier: if adding φ\varphi turns an initial descriptive account into a normative account, then the initial account did not logically entail φ\varphi. That seems closer in spirit to the intended is/ought barrier than the Limited General Barrier Theorem. The case of lazy Joe is no exception, for adding the normative φ\varphi ‘Joe is helping or Joe ought to be helping’ to the descriptive Γ\Gamma {‘Joe is helping’} leaves us with a set logically equivalent to ‘Joe is helping’, which is descriptive. From this perspective, the key comparison is between a set of assumptions and an expansion of it, not between the old set and the new member.

The Unqualified Argument Set Formulation is easy to prove from first principles. As Russell notes, R\mathrm{R}-breakability is invariant across logical equivalence, because R\mathrm{R}-breakability is defined in terms of truth in a model, and logically equivalent sentences are true in the same models. This yields a principle about sets of sentences Γ\Gamma and Δ\Delta:

If Γ\Gamma is logically equivalent to Δ\Delta, then Γ\Gamma is R\mathrm{R}-breakable just if Δ\Delta is R\mathrm{R}-breakable.

Now let Δ=Γ∪{φ}\Delta = \Gamma \cup \{\varphi\}. If Γ\Gamma is R\mathrm{R}-unbreakable and Γ∪{φ}\Gamma \cup \{\varphi\} R\mathrm{R}-breakable, then, contraposing the invariance principle, Γ\Gamma is not logically equivalent to Γ∪{φ}\Gamma \cup \{\varphi\}, which implies that Γ\Gamma does not logically entail φ\varphi. The technical point is near-trivial, but the Unqualified Argument Set Formulation may still be useful when we have better cognitive access to the presence or absence of R\mathrm{R}-breakability than to the presence or absence of logical entailment, if such cases arise.

How well do the distinctions between the R\mathrm{R}-unbreakable and the R\mathrm{R}-breakable for the relevant relations R\mathrm{R} fit the corresponding informal distinctions, for instance between the descriptive and the normative? Russell makes mostly natural choices in selecting the R\mathrm{R} relations. However, for the indexicality barrier, there are complications. She permits variation in the agent and place of the context, but not in its time or world. Consequently, ‘I’ and ‘here’ are treated as indexical, but ‘now’ and ‘actually’ as non-indexical. That looks unnatural. Indexicality is usually understood as change in semantic value as the context of utterance varies while the circumstance of evaluation is held fixed (to use Kaplan’s helpful distinction, as Russell does); that criterion applies to the time and world of the context just as much as to its agent and place. But each of Russell’s models contains only one context, and truth in the model is defined as truth at the present time in the actual world of the model, its default circumstance of evaluation, which are in turn equated with the time and world of the model’s context. Thus, varying the context’s time and world automatically varies the default circumstance of evaluation too, and so conflates change and contingency with indexicality in respect of time and world. By contrast, circumstances of evaluation lack parameters for agent and place. As Russell explains, this motivates her asymmetric treatment of different contextual parameters. Still, the result is unsatisfyingly ad hoc. Here, the constraining effect of Russell’s model-theoretic framework on her account is Procrustean. Perhaps the intended barrier for indexicality is not fully analogous to those for the other phenomena to which she applies her theory.

The final seventy pages of the book are meant to extend the account from formal to natural languages. I found them less helpful than the rest, for several reasons. First, since the formal model-theoretic semantic theories Russell uses earlier were successfully developed to model various relevant semantic features of natural languages, she underestimates how strong a case the book has already made for the application of her account to natural languages. Vague appeals by her opponents to the wondrous subtlety of ordinary language have little dialectical force. Second, much work is spent explaining why candidate counterexamples to the Limited General Barrier Theorem fail. Since Russell has already provided a straightforward proof of the theorem, we know in advance that there will be no counterexamples. Third, Russell proposes that the semantics of natural languages requires model-like entities she calls ‘Combinations’ which vary with both how the language is interpreted and how the world is. The theory of Combinations is under-motivated and under-developed, and not much is done with it. For example, variation in the world is supposed to be needed to justify varying the Combination’s domain because “there might have been more or fewer objects” (230); but it could just as well be justified by variation in how contextual restrictions on quantifiers are interpreted.

A better challenge targets the relation between Russell’s formal results and the psychological appeal of barriers to entailment. Pre-theoretically, how do we know which sets of sentences are R\mathrm{R}-breakable and which are not? Hume’s original account of the is/ought case centers on his baffled experience of an author’s transition from ‘is’ and ‘is not’ to ‘ought’ and ‘ought not’, words on a page. That suggests that, psychologically, he may have been using a heuristic triggered by occurrences of specific words rather than methods insensitive to differences between logical equivalents. Nevertheless, he was obviously not indifferent to the meanings of those words, and Russell’s account may help explain how psychological dispositions can manage to track underlying logical relations, more or less. At any rate, she has made an impressive contribution to our understanding of this tricky area.
