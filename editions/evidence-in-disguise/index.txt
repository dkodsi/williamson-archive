# Evidence in disguise

Timothy Williamson

In Martin Grajner and Eva Schmidt (eds.), Epistemic Dilemmas and Epistemic Normativity (2026), 134–151. New York: Routledge.

# 7.1 Introduction

Beliefs, claims, conjectures, and theories are assessed against bodies of evidence. Which does our evidence prove or refute? Even if it is less decisive, which does it confirm or disconfirm?

For brevity, I will use “hypothesis” as a blanket term for beliefs, claims, conjectures, theories, and the like. Thus, we can ask how probable hypotheses are on a given body of evidence. When we update on new evidence, we ask a more dynamic question: does the new evidence make the hypothesis more or less probable than it was before?—or does it make no difference? If the ostensibly new evidence turns out to be just old evidence dressed up to look new, the probability should stay the same; updating was not needed.

The distinction between old and new evidence requires a standard for individuating evidence as the same or different. If we treat evidence as encoded by representations of some sort, we can ask: when do two representations encode the same evidence? If the representations are declarative sentences in a context, and the evidence they encode just comprises the propositions they express in that context, the question becomes: when do two declarative sentences in a context express the same proposition? But, presumably, declarative sentences are not the only representations capable of encoding evidence. We gain evidence from sense perception too, encoded in our own perceptual states. Perhaps such evidence could not be properly encoded in a declarative sentence, even a very long one.

Not all declarative sentences express evidence. It is standardly assumed that any hypothesis inconsistent with the evidence is false, which presumes that all evidence is true. Thus, a false declarative sentence does not encode evidence. Nor is truth enough. A truth is part of your evidence only if you have it, in other words, you stand in an appropriate epistemic relation to that truth. Elsewhere, I have argued that the required epistemic relation is knowing (Williamson 2000, 184-208).

This chapter focuses not on whether truth or knowledge is required, but instead on the nature of the evidence itself, specifically, on how it is individuated, and even more specifically, on whether the identity or distinctness of evidence must be transparent to those who have the evidence. Suppose you receive some evidence, but it is opaque to you whether it is genuinely new evidence or just old evidence in a new guise. Then it may also be opaque to you how you should update on this evidence: whether it enhances the inductive support for some hypothesis, so your confidence in that hypothesis should increase, or whether it is just some of your old inductive support for the hypothesis in disguise, so your confidence in the hypothesis should stay the same. Thus, individuating evidence in ways opaque to us may render us unable to work out how to comply with an epistemic norm, such as one of proportioning one’s confidence in a hypothesis to the degree mandated by one’s evidence. What our evidence appears to support may conflict with what it really does support, in ways we are in no epistemic position to adjudicate, since we do not know which is reality and which appearance. This confusion may even put us in some sort of epistemic dilemma.

Someone might respond: if so, our evidence had better be individuated in a way that is transparent to us. But perfect transparency may be impossible, for general epistemological reasons (Williamson 2000, 93–113). The most systematic framework for thinking rigorously about evidence may imply quite gross failures of transparency, not just slight failures. This chapter works through these issues.

# 7.2 Fine-Grained and Coarse-Grained Evidence

The central choice-point for individuating evidence is how fine-grained or coarse-grained the individuation is to be: the finer the grain, the more it takes for two representations to encode the same evidence; the coarser the grain, the less it takes for them to encode the same evidence.

To get more precise, we need a more informative conception of evidence. We will assume that evidence is propositional, in the sense of being true or false in possible circumstances. That it is propositional does not mean that it is linguistic or quasi-linguistic in form; a sensory perception may be veridical or non-veridical, and have a content true or false in various possible circumstances, without anything linguistic or quasi-linguistic in form. Evidence must be propositional, since hypotheses are consistent or inconsistent with it; consistency and inconsistency are relations between propositional items. In what follows, each piece or body of evidence is treated as a proposition. I have argued for the propositionality of evidence in detail elsewhere (Williamson 2000, 194-200). Thus, the individuation of evidence boils down to the individuation of propositions.

The most salient coarse-grained approach to individuating propositions is intensionalism, on which propositions are identical if and only if they are necessarily equivalent, in other words, they cannot differ in truth-value. In each possible world, they are both true or both false. For present purposes, we take this impossibility to be metaphysical, rather than logical or epistemic; not much here will turn on the exact understanding of “metaphysical.”

That identical propositions are necessarily equivalent is trivial, for each proposition p is necessarily equivalent to itself, so by Leibniz’s Law if p = q, p is necessarily equivalent to q. What is controversial in intensionalism is the converse claim, that necessarily equivalent propositions are identical, which more fine-grained approaches deny. They are hyperintensional, holding that some pairs of propositions are distinct but necessarily equivalent.

Of course, different philosophers may use the theoretical term “proposition” with different senses, perhaps differing in the associated fineness of grain. For present purposes, the question would then be: which of those senses is best suited to understanding the function of evidence? At any rate, a key issue for individuating evidence is whether there are distinct but necessarily equivalent pieces of evidence. The next section considers this issue in the setting of probability theory.

# 7.3 Probability Spaces

A central role for evidence is to provide a basis on which to assess the probability of hypotheses—beliefs, claims, conjectures, theories, …. We need to ask how probable a hypothesis is on our evidence (on such evidential probabilities see Williamson 2000, 209-237). An account of evidence that cannot make sense of probabilities on our evidence is to that extent dysfunctional. That is not to say that all evidential relations are probabilistic, just that some of the most important and best understood of them are. A theory of evidence that does not engage properly with probability theory is something of a non-starter.

The cognitive power of probabilistic thinking derives from its mathematical structure. That structure unfolds within a mathematical framework made formally precise by the definition of a probability space. That definition reveals how evidence is individuated in standard probability theory.

A probability space is based on an underlying set $\Omega$, whose members are called “outcomes”; they can be informally conceived as the possible outcomes of an experiment, mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive; each outcome is maximally specific in all relevant respects. Subsets of $\Omega$ are called “events.” The probability space also includes a probability measure $\operatorname{Pr}$, mapping events to real numbers in the closed interval $[0,1]$, conceived as their probabilities. The impossible empty event has probability 0; the necessary event $\Omega$ has probability 1. The probability of the union of any finite or countably infinite collection of mutually incompatible events is required to be the sum of the probabilities of those events. As a consequence, the probabilities of any event and its complement event sum to 1. To handle combinatorial difficulties that arise if $\Omega$ is infinite, the standard definition of a probability space avoids requiring probabilities to be assigned to all subsets of $\Omega$ (with the device of an “$\mathrm{\sigma}$-field”; see Grimmett and Stirzaker 2001, Chapter 1). For simplicity, I ignore this restriction; it makes no difference to the issues discussed in this chapter.

Structurally, a probability space is like a simple Kripke model for modal logic. Instead of “worlds,” one speaks of “outcomes”; instead of “propositions,” conceived as sets of worlds, one speaks of “events,” conceived as sets of outcomes; instead of “W,” the set of all worlds, one speaks of “$\Omega$,” the set of all outcomes. To complete the analogy, one can treat the universal relation, which any outcome has to any outcome, as an accessibility relation $\mathrm{R}$ for the probability space, so any world is possible from the perspective of any world.

We also want to discuss the probabilities of events conditional on an event E, where we in effect exclude all outcomes outside E and recalibrate: the conditional probability of an event H on E is the proportion (weighted by Pr) of the E-region of the probability space that overlaps the H-region. When $\operatorname{Pr}(\mathrm{E})>0$, we can therefore define the conditional probability $\operatorname{Pr}(\mathrm{H}\mid \mathrm{E})$ of H on E as the ratio $\operatorname{Pr}(\mathrm{H}\cap \mathrm{E})/\operatorname{Pr}(\mathrm{E})$. When $\operatorname{Pr}(\mathrm{E})=0$ but E is non-empty, we can still make sense of the conditional probability $\operatorname{Pr}(\mathrm{H}\mid \mathrm{E})$ by treating conditional probability as basic and undefined, though it must still satisfy the ratio equation for $\operatorname{Pr}(\mathrm{E})>0$. We can conceive E here as information on which to update. Then $\operatorname{Pr}(\mathrm{H}\mid \mathrm{E})$ is the probability of the hypothesis H on the evidence. For present purposes, we make the simplifying assumption that whenever E is evidence, $\operatorname{Pr}(\mathrm{E})>0$ (it is not crucial to the arguments of this chapter). The probability space gives us just what we need for evidential probabilities.

To reach that satisfactory conclusion, we had to treat both the hypothesis H and the evidence E as events, sets of outcomes; otherwise, their unconditional and conditional probabilities would not be defined. In effect, events in a probability space are individuated intensionally: $\mathrm{E}=\mathrm{F}$ whenever, for each possible outcome $o$, E is true in $o$ ($o\in \mathrm{E}$) if and only if F is true in $o$ ($o\in \mathrm{F}$). “Possible” here corresponds to whatever modal standard is built into the probability space, which need not be metaphysical possibility, but many structural effects are the same whatever flavor of modality is involved. In particular, for any such modality, every equation that follows from the axioms for Boolean algebra corresponds to an identity of events. For example, $\mathrm{E}=\mathrm{E}\cup(\mathrm{F}\cap \mathrm{E})$, even if what $\mathrm{E}\cap(\mathrm{F}\cup \mathrm{E})$ is “about” seems to differ from what E is “about.” Consequently, the conditional probabilities $\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{E}\mid \mathrm{E})$ and $\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{E}\mid(\mathrm{E}\cap \mathrm{F})\cup \mathrm{E})$ are both 1, even though the right-to-left entailment is easier to see in the first case than in the second. The same applies when the Boolean equation is vastly harder to verify: the computational differences between the two expressions disappear at the level of events. Thus, the mathematical framework of probability theory automatically enforces much of the flattening effect of intensionalism (Builes 2020 discusses other apparently hyperintensional probabilistic phenomena).

Some philosophers will conclude that standard probability theory needs revising. Such methodological advice is potentially disastrous. No small tweak would suffice to respect computational differences, nor is it clear what larger ones would be needed. A major revision of mathematical probability theory could easily make it computationally intractable, or pitifully weak; either way, it would undermine the scientific value of the theory.

A subtler move is to keep the mathematics unchanged, but modify the interpretation of natural language over the probability space. A sentence “S” of natural language such as “The coin came up heads” is mapped to an event [“S”], a subset of $\Omega$. Normally, it is taken for granted that the linguistic operations of conjunction, disjunction, and negation are to be interpreted by the set-theoretic operations of intersection, union, and complementation in $\Omega$, respectively. For instance, $[\text{“S and T”}]=[\text{“S”}]\cap[\text{“T”}]$. This is no mere convention, since those linguistic operations are used to define the corresponding set-theoretic operations. For instance, the set $\mathrm{E}\cap \mathrm{F}$ is defined as the set to which an outcome belongs if and only if it belongs to E and belongs to F. But other approaches are conceivable. In particular, we could define [“S”] as the set of outcomes $o$ in which the sentence “S” is true as interpreted in o (this is like the use of diagonal propositions to interpret some beliefs in Stalnaker 1999). Thus, if “and” in $o$ is interpreted as or, $o$ belongs to [“S and T”] if and only if $o$ belongs to [“S”] or $o$ belongs to [“T”]. Thus, if $o$ belongs to [“S”] but not to [“T”], then $o$ belongs to [“S and T”] but not to [“S”]∩[“T”], so $[\text{“S and T”}]\ne[\text{“S”}]\cap[\text{“T”}]$. Consequently, sentences of natural language equivalent to each other in propositional logic may be mapped to different events in the probability space. The flattening effect of intensionalism is thereby avoided.

But the reinterpretation strategy only shifts the wrinkle in the carpet. Although the pure mathematical theory of probability remains intact, all the complications return as soon as we try to apply it to a problem stated in natural language. We lose our understanding of the structural relations amongst the events under discussion, and so cannot make proper use of the mathematics. The reinterpretation was in any case ill-motivated, because it models a situation where the logical connectives of natural language are not clearly understood, but such semantic confusions are not the main source of our computational limitations (see Williamson 2024, 244-8). Mathematical probability theory provides a forceful case for the coarse-grained intensional individuation of evidence.

# 7.4 The Format of Evidence

Intensionalism allows us to model the content of evidence as a set of possible worlds, but it does not imply that we must encode that content by a list of labels of possible worlds or in any other specific way. It is largely neutral on the form in which the content is expressed.

Since evidence is propositional, it is easily taken to be expressed in the form of declarative sentences. But that form is inappropriate for much perceptual evidence. When you look at a tree, you may gain evidence for or against the hypothesis “That tree is dying.” The hypothesis itself is expressed by a declarative sentence (as uttered in context), but the evidence is not. That does not mean that the perceptual evidence is non-propositional. In veridical perception, things are the way they appear. In non-veridical perception, things are not the way they appear. That things are the way they appear to be is a proposition, true or false. Indeed, we can even express that proposition in the declarative sentence “Things are this way.” The trouble is that most of the cognitive work is done by the perception at which we are gesturing in the context; without the perceptual connection, the bare sentence is of little use. If you later report your perceptual evidence to people merely by saying “That tree was this way,” they are in no position to judge whether the evidence tells for or against the hypothesis “That tree is dying.”

In short, some evidence is verbalized, and some is not. Evidence is not restricted to any one natural language, or to any one sense modality. To think in probabilistic terms about the underlying evidential relations among all the hypotheses and pieces of evidence in play, we must notionally map all these representations in diverse formats onto propositions (events, subsets of Ω) in a single probability space, to render them mutually commensurable. The same applies when we have to assess the logical or epistemically modal relations among those hypotheses and pieces of evidence, though the set of which the propositions are subsets may be called “W” rather than “Ω.” Thus, propositions are a “common currency” for cognitive interaction. They are not themselves representations. They are nothing like sentences or sensory images. For propositions are conceived as sets of worlds, worlds too are non-representational; they are better understood as something like ways for things to be.

Obviously, this coarse-grained approach to evidence and probability faces some serious challenges, which the next section starts to explore.

# 7.5 Frege Puzzles

One predictable challenge to the coarse-grained framework for evidence and probability comes from Frege puzzles, like that of Hesperus and Phosphorus.

Consider Ignatius, a rational decision-theorist ignorant of English literature. He has encountered the names “Eric Blair” and “George Orwell” but has very little knowledge associated with them; in particular, he does not know that they refer to the same person, nor does he associate either name with the description “anti-totalitarian.” Ignatius knows vaguely what the novel 1984 is about, but cannot name its author. Telling Ignatius “George Orwell wrote 1984” seems to give him much better evidence for the claim “George Orwell was anti-totalitarian” than for the claim “Eric Blair was anti-totalitarian.” One might expect this difference to be reflected in Ignatius’s credences (subjective probabilities), both in his prior conditional credences in the claims on the new evidence before updating and in his posterior unconditional credences in the claims after updating on the new evidence. Yet, by the necessity of identity, since Eric Blair is George Orwell, it is a necessary truth that Eric Blair is George Orwell, so it is a necessary truth that Eric Blair wrote 1984 if and only if George Orwell wrote 1984, and a necessary truth that Eric Blair was anti-totalitarian if and only if George Orwell was anti-totalitarian. Thus, by the coarse-grained intensionalist standard, the proposition that Eric Blair wrote 1984 is the proposition that George Orwell wrote 1984, and the proposition that Eric Blair was anti-totalitarian is the proposition that George Orwell was anti-totalitarian. Thus, swapping the names makes no difference to the content of the evidence and no difference to the content of the hypothesis. Consequently, it makes no difference to the probabilities.

Frege puzzles are no mere artifacts of natural language semantics, but genuine cognitive phenomena clearly relevant to our uses of evidence. Theories of evidence cannot in good conscience just brush them aside.

An initial response might go along these lines: nothing in the mathematical framework of probability theory requires the “worlds” or “outcomes” in Ω to be metaphysically rather than merely epistemically possible. Even if the necessity of identity holds for metaphysical necessity, that does not mean that it must hold for epistemic necessity: even if it is metaphysically necessary that Eric Blair is George Orwell, it does not follow that it is also epistemically necessary that Eric Blair is George Orwell; surely, for Ignatius, it is epistemically possible—indeed, probable—that Eric Blair is not George Orwell. On such a view, the identity “Eric Blair = George Orwell” is true at some points in probability space and false at others, so at least one of the names is not a rigid designator across all epistemically possible worlds, even if it is a rigid designator across all metaphysically possible worlds.

Such a response does not sit easily with standard views in contemporary semantics, on which proper names are not just rigid designators but directly referential. In other words, all they contribute to the compositional semantics is their referent, so co-referential proper names make exactly the same contribution to the compositional semantics. For example, the proposition literally expressed by the sentences “Eric Blair wrote 1984” and “Eric Blair is George Orwell” just are the propositions literally expressed by the sentences “George Orwell wrote 1984” and “Eric Blair is Eric Blair” respectively. Consequently, the response in effect envisages a mapping of sentences to propositions in the probability space other than the propositions they literally express.

A non-standard semantics for proper names would not meet the generality of the problem, since Frege puzzles can arise for any kind of singular term, such as perceptual demonstratives: “this bird” (with the reference fixed by sight) and “that bird” (with the reference fixed by hearing) may non-obviously refer to the same bird. Thus, any kind of directly referential singular term raises the same problem.

At this point, some philosophers will naturally suggest a Fregean solution to Frege puzzles, appealing to a distinction between sense and reference. Thus, the names “Eric Blair” and “George Orwell” have the same reference but different senses. Since Fregean senses are individuated cognitively, that approach looks built to play well with a treatment of the worlds or outcomes in a probability space as epistemically rather than metaphysically possible (though some may be both).

The trouble is that Frege puzzles can arise from mere differences of words, even when the words are paradigms of synonymy. Saul Kripke (1979) pointed that out, and I will use one of his examples. The words “furze” and “gorse” are natural kind terms for the very same kind of shrub. They are not associated in English with different descriptions, except trivially with metalinguistic ones, such as “shrub whose name begins with an ‘f’” or “shrub whose name begins with a ‘g’.” Although an individual speaker may idiosyncratically associate them with different non-metalinguistic descriptions, that is not a feature of their linguistic meaning. But even a native speaker who understands both terms by normal standards and is reasonably confident that they co-refer may still be slightly less confident of “Furze is gorse” than of “Furze is furze,” and so rationally assign a slightly lower probability to the former than to the latter. For such a speaker, learning “Mary saw no furze on that hill” may be slightly better evidence for “There is no furze on that hill” than learning “Mary saw no gorse on that hill,” with corresponding differences in their credences. These differences in cognitive significance arise simply from the difference of word.

Some differences in cognitive significance cut finer than the words themselves, for example when the words are context-sensitive. The word “this,” used as a perceptual demonstrative, differs in cognitive significance depending on the speaker’s state of perceptual attention. Thus, differences in cognitive significance cut at least as finely as ordered pairs of a linguistic expression and a context.

The Fregean explanation of a Frege puzzle depends on a difference in sense without a difference in reference; that is what motivates the distinction between sense and reference. Thus, since a mere difference of word gives rise to a Frege puzzle, the Fregean explanation implies that the mere difference of word gives rise to a difference in sense. But, if the senses of words are individuated as finely as the words themselves (paired with contexts), the motivation for postulating a separate category of senses is undermined: one might as well make do with the words themselves (paired with contexts), and cut out senses as a redundant layer of middle management between language and the world. On this view, a representation does not encode evidence; it is the evidence.

However, that view of evidence as representations fits poorly with the role of evidence as propositions in a probability space, forming a common currency for transactions between evidence and hypotheses, finessing incommensurable differences in representational format. Although one might try to solve the problem by watering down the formal constraints on probability spaces, such attempts drastically undermine the explanatory power of probability theory, which depends on exactly those constraints. The compensatory benefits in handling Frege puzzles look marginal by comparison. Methodologically, the watering-down strategy is a bad bargain, and arguably a case of overfitting (Williamson 2024).

To reinforce the morals of this section, we will consider some cases of transmitting evidence across contexts, where often what matters is preserving reference, not preserving anything like sense.

# 7.6 Tracking Reference

In moving across contexts, we update to keep track of things; otherwise, we may lose evidence. This process illustrates how identity of reference, not identity of sense, let alone identity of representation, is often what matters in our handling of evidence.

Here is an example. A shipwrecked sailor is trying to keep track of what has been happening to him. He remembers: “Today I saw a footprint on the sand.” To preserve what matters in that piece of evidence the next day, he needs a different sentence: he must remember “Yesterday I saw a footprint on the sand.” The word “yesterday” as the sailor used it one day has nothing like the same sense as the word “today” as he used it the previous day; it simply has the same reference.

That example involved the same agent at different times. Here is a similar example that involves different agents at the same time. A mother tells her small son: “Today is your birthday.” To capture what matters in that piece of evidence, he needs a different sentence: he must recognize “Today is my birthday.” The word “my” as used by the son has nothing like the same sense as the word “your” as used by his mother; it simply has the same reference.

In both examples, when things go well, the intended recipient comes to know the proposition expressed by the new sentence; otherwise, it could hardly form part of the recipient’s evidence, on the speaker’s knowledgeable testimony (for the equation of one’s total evidence with one’s total knowledge see Williamson 2000, 200-208). To acquire that knowledge, the sailor needs something like implicit knowledge of the coreference between “today” uttered one day and “yesterday” uttered the next day, and the young son needs something like implicit knowledge of the coreference between “your” uttered by his mother and “my” uttered by him. But all of that is quite consistent with the coarse-grained individuation of the evidence itself, as it is transmitted from one context to another.

Similar phenomena occur for perceptual and memory demonstratives. Here is an example. A robber buries her loot in a forest, intending to return and dig it up again once the coast is clear. She knows “I buried it by this tree,” using “this tree” as a perceptual demonstrative. With a memory image of the tree, she may preserve the knowledge “I buried it by that tree,” using “that tree” as a memory demonstrative, once the tree is no longer in sight. But her vivid memory image of the tree will be of little use if she cannot remember where in the forest the tree is, or how to reach it. Of course, preserving the right reference is not enough. The user must in effect know that reference has been preserved. Moreover, the reference must be preserved in some relevantly usable form. But that does not mean that the usable form must be built into the content.

The transmission of evidence across epistemic contexts or perspectives is crucial to many of its uses: from one time to another in memory, from one agent to another in testimony. Evidence is not typically private; it does not consist in the mental states of a particular individual at a particular time. Obvious examples come from evidence in scientific, legal, political, and other social contexts. Thus, individuating evidence in terms of a single epistemic context or perspective is inappropriate for central functions of evidence.

Another example comes from Anna Mahtani’s work on the interpretation of probability in welfare economics (Mahtani 2017, 2021, 2023). She shows convincingly that although the probabilities used to define an agent’s expected welfare under a policy are officially interpreted as subjective credences, the definition has trouble with Frege cases, where the same agent is presented under several non-obviously equivalent guises, so the agent’s expected welfare should be guise-dependent, but the theory treats it as guise-independent. Mahtani makes an impressively careful attempt to adapt theories of welfare economics to a Fregean interpretation of the probabilities, but the result is a heavy increase in theoretical complexity and, as she admits, no fully satisfactory solution. A better approach might be to understand the probabilities more objectively, as guise-independent, making Frege cases irrelevant, and thereby retain the (slightly reinterpreted) original definitions and theories (Williamson 2024, 212-21; for more on Frege cases for credences see Braun 2016).

This perspective-independent approach to evidence is consonant with now-standard frameworks for the semantics of natural languages, as already described, on which singular terms are directly referential. Semantic theories with nothing like Fregean senses individuated by cognitive significance may be better adapted than those with Fregean senses to cognitive purposes, once the diachronic and social aspects of cognition are taken into account.

# 7.7 Is Evidence Transparent?

Being told “George Orwell wrote 1984” does not put one in exactly the same epistemic position as being told “Eric Blair wrote 1984,” even granted the semantics of direct reference. For, in the first case, when Ignatius learns that George Orwell wrote 1984, and ipso facto learns that Eric Blair wrote 1984, he also learns that the sentence “George Orwell wrote 1984” is true, but does not learn that the sentence “Eric Blair wrote 1984” is true, whereas, in the second case, when Ignatius learns that Eric Blair wrote 1984, and ipso facto learns that George Orwell wrote 1984, he also learns that the sentence “Eric Blair wrote 1984” is true, but does not learn that the sentence “George Orwell wrote 1984” is true. For the direct reference semantics does not challenge the distinctness of the two sentences, and Ignatius can easily tell which of them has been uttered. Such observations may encourage the hope that, by appeal to metalinguistic differences, the coarse-grained view can vindicate the epistemic appearances.

Alas, it is harder than that. Imagine quizzing Ignatius on his credences, and in particular on his conditional credences. He understands the mathematics of probability as an expert, and he is very careful not to confuse use and mention. You make it quite clear to him that you are interested in his conditional probabilities about non-metalinguistic matters. First, you ask him “What is your credence that George Orwell wrote 1984, given that George Orwell wrote 1984?” He gives the trivially mathematically correct answer “1.” Then you ask him “What is your credence that George Orwell wrote 1984, given that Eric Blair wrote 1984?” His first instinct may be to offer some number close to 0, since distinct names tend to have distinct bearers. On reflection, however, it may occur to him that “George Orwell” and “Eric Blair” could be names of the same person. He may even accept a semantic theory on which proper names are directly referential, and appreciate its potential relevance to the case at hand. But still, he is in no position to answer “1” to the second question, since, for all he knows, the two names do not co-refer. If he does accept the direct reference theory, he may have to answer “I don’t know”: from his perspective, his conditional probability may be 1 (if the two names co-refer) and it may be 0 (if the two names do not co-refer). On the other hand, if he accepts something more like a Fregean semantic theory, he may give an answer close to 0, though that answer will be incorrect if the direct reference theory is in fact right. If one tries to measure Ignatius’s credences by his betting behavior instead, the results will be similar, because the bets are still offered to Ignatius in linguistic form.

In short, given the direct reference theory, Ignatius is at best ignorant and at worst mistaken about his own conditional credences on non-metalinguistic matters. Such credences are not in general transparent to the agent.

Switching to credences on metalinguistic matters would not restore transparency. Just as names of people can non-obviously co-refer, so can names of sentences. To use another of Kripke’s examples, someone may not realize that “Paderewski was Polish” uttered in a conversation about pianists is the same sentence as “Paderewski was Polish” uttered in a conversation about politicians (Kripke 1979). Moving the objects of credence into the meta-language only postpones the problem.

Opponents of direct reference semantics may regard all this as constituting a strong argument for a more Fregean approach. We have already seen some of the grave difficulties for Fregean semantics. In particular, one should not imagine that Fregeanism can restore the transparency of evidential relations to the agent. The attempt to make them transparent was what collapsed difference of sense into difference of word. Despite its initial promise, the use of differences in cognitive significance as a guide to differences in meaning has turned out badly for philosophers of language, and epistemologists should not repeat their mistake.

Coarse-grained semantics is far from the only obstacle to the wishfully imagined transparency of evidence. I have argued elsewhere on general epistemological grounds that no non-trivial condition is such that, whenever it obtains, one is in a position to know that it obtains (for this antiluminosity argument see Williamson 2000, 93-113). The argument applies in particular to the condition of having a given proposition as part of one’s evidence, and to the condition of not having that proposition as part of one’s evidence. Coarse-grained semantics adds a further class of (especially vivid) examples of non-transparency.

Should we “re-engineer” the “concept” of evidence, that is, change the meaning of the word “evidence” and of words with similar meanings in English and other languages, in order to make its application more transparent? That would involve making it stand for something more fine-grained. Given the arguments of previous sections, that would in turn require making it more sensitive to the form of representations, and so metalinguistic. We could indeed gerrymander and stipulate such a new metalinguistic meaning for the word “evidence,” at least for theoretical contexts over which we had some control, but the benefits would be unlikely to outweigh the costs. By the general argument mentioned above, it would still not achieve full transparency. Indeed, it promises to be of much less theoretical and practical use than the word “evidence” as currently used. More specifically, the arguments about the capacity to interface with the mathematical structure of probability theory, the diversity of formats in which evidence is presented, and the diachronic and social availability of evidence do not just reflect the way the word “evidence” happens to be currently used: they concern our more general epistemic and cognitive needs, across a wide range of situations from everyday life to scientific theorizing, to which the coarse-grained engineering is already well-adapted. It would make the term more cumbersome and difficult to use, the preserve of a philosophical elite with time on their hands. It would be as misguided and ineffective as most philosophers’ proposals to “re-engineer concepts.”

# 7.8 Disquotational Heuristics

If the semantics of our language, and so of our discourse about evidence, is really coarse-grained, why does it seem so fine-grained to us? Why is it so tempting to judge that, for example, Ignatius’s evidence includes that George Orwell wrote 1984 but does not include that Eric Blair wrote 1984? The thought process is not so hard to reconstruct. Faced with a question of the form “Does S’s evidence include that P?”, by default we simply consider the potential evidence as presented to S in the form of the sentence “P,” and in effect ask whether S has the required sort of epistemic access to a truth as presented by “P.” Arguably, the required sort of epistemic access is simply knowledge, but that does not matter for the present point. What matters is that, since we understand that Ignatius has the required sort of epistemic access (whatever it is) to a truth as presented by the sentence “George Orwell wrote 1984,” we unreflectively judge “Ignatius’s evidence includes that George Orwell wrote 1984.” Likewise, since we understand that Ignatius does not have the required sort of epistemic access (whatever it is) to a truth as presented by the sentence “Eric Blair wrote 1984,” we unreflectively judge “Ignatius’s evidence does not include that Eric Blair wrote 1984.” Normally, it just does not occur to us that there might be a Frege case in the vicinity. Even if the possibility does occur to us, we have no pre-theoretic access to its implications for such judgments. We persist in those judgments because they are not obviously wrong even in a Frege case; pre-theoretically, we have no incentive to do better, and in any case no idea how to do better. The problems with a Fregean approach to semantics have only gradually become clear through decades of reflection in the philosophy of language.

When we are led into thinking of evidence as fine-grained in content, we are in effect guided by a heuristic, an efficient but fallible way of answering a question. It is not just a specific heuristic dedicated to the word “evidence.” Rather, there is a far more general kind of disquotational heuristic, by which one attributes to an agent S an attitude to the proposition that P on the basis of S’s interaction (or lack of it) with the sentence “P,” where the relevant kind of interaction depends on the attitude to be attributed (Williamson 2024). Since two sentences can express the same proposition, and S may interact differently with those two sentences, we may end up equivocating as to whether S has the given attitude to the proposition they both express.

Describing the operation of the heuristic as “disquotation” is strictly misleading, for in the simplest case the user only has to repeat the speaker’s words; there are no quotation marks to strip off. Often, something more is required, such as converting the speaker’s first-person pronoun to the user’s second-person or third-person pronoun or a proper name, and likewise for temporal indexicals such as “now” and “today.” But that is usually done unreflectively, with minimal effort, and is in any case a departure from disquotation, imposed by the nature of indexicality. Nevertheless, I will continue to use the word “disquotational” for want of a better.

Arguably, such a disquotational heuristic is active in Kripke’s notorious puzzle about belief, where Pierre interacts differently with the English sentence “London is pretty” and the French sentence “Londres est jolie,” even though they express the same proposition (Kripke 1979). In that case, the propositional attitude is belief, and the relevant kind of interaction is assent; the specific mindreading heuristic there is for judging what someone believes by what they say. Kripke himself does not present his disquotational principles for belief attribution as merely heuristic in status, but they are arguably better understood as restricted cases of such a heuristic (Williamson 2021, 2024, 181-192).

Heuristics are studied extensively in contemporary psychology. They may be seen in a negative light, as forms of irrationality, and described as “cheap and dirty,” in the tradition of Daniel Kahneman (Kahneman, Slovic, and Tversky 1982), or in a more positive light, as forms of bounded rationality, and described as “fast and frugal,” in the tradition of Gerd Gigerenzer (Gigerenzer, Hertwig, and Pachur 2011). But both traditions agree that fallible heuristics play a major role in human cognition. It is therefore not at all surprising that we rely on fallible heuristics in attributing propositional attitudes.

Some heuristics are built into our perceptual systems; for example, color boundaries in the visual field are used as a guide to the edges of three-dimensional objects; that heuristic explains why camouflage is effective. Disquotational heuristics are presumably built into our mindreading system, which seems to be humanly universal, like perceptual heuristics. In both cases, we typically rely on them unreflectively, not consciously aware that we are relying on a heuristic, though we can become aware of our reliance on them through conscious reflection. Of course, our mindreading capacity uses far more than disquotational heuristics, which are specific to mindreading on the basis of linguistic behavior. We also attribute propositional attitudes to non-human animals and very young children, and indeed to human adults too, to explain their non-linguistic behavior.

Arguably, just as visual illusions result from heuristics built into the human visual system, so philosophical paradoxes result from heuristics built into more general human cognitive systems (Williamson 2024). By contrast, not all our heuristics are humanly universal; a bias may be culture-specific. Some heuristics may also be conscious, such as a doctor’s idiosyncratic heuristic for interpreting X-rays.

Our reliance on fallible heuristics in perception does not justify skepticism about our knowledge of the external world; likewise, our reliance on fallible heuristics in mindreading does not justify skepticism about our knowledge of other minds. But we do need to take a somewhat more critical attitude to our data. In particular, we should be wary of dismissing powerful explanatory frameworks just on the basis of confident judgments—for example, about Frege cases—that can be explained as products of efficient but fallible heuristics which are predicted to go wrong in just such cases.

# 7.9 Coarse-Grained Content, Epistemic Normativity, and Epistemic Dilemmas

Given the non-transparency of evidence, we can expect epistemic norms to be less than fully operational: one is not always in a position to know whether one is complying with a given epistemic norm. When one is not in a position to know what one’s evidence includes, one is also not in a position to know which beliefs are proportionate to one’s evidence. This is a general feature of the human cognitive predicament, and indeed of the cognitive predicament of just about any finite creature. It is something we have to learn to live with. Attempts to get around it by fiddling with the definition of “evidence” or the wording of the norms are futile; they fail to restore transparency and they lead to bad epistemology, as I have argued elsewhere (Williamson 2000).

Coarse-grained individuation reinforces those points, and even leads to something like epistemic dilemmas (Hughes 2025). At least to a first approximation, a dilemma is a case where, whatever one does, one violates some duty or other, and an epistemic dilemma is a case where, whatever one does, one violates some epistemic duty or other.

Recall Ignatius. He has got to the point of asking himself this question, in these words:

Q1
Is Eric Blair George Orwell?

He still sees no rational way of reaching a “Yes” or “No” answer to Q1. In reflecting on Q1, Ignatius asks himself a question about what his own credences should be, in these words:

Q2
Should I be more confident that Eric Blair is Eric Blair than that Eric Blair is George Orwell?

For Ignatius, the natural answer to Q2 is “Yes”: “Eric Blair is Eric Blair” is a logical truth, while he sees no rational way of concluding “Eric Blair is George Orwell.” But, given coarse-grained individuation, answering “Yes” to Q2 seems to involve some kind of probabilistic incoherence, since it would be judging that he should be more confident in a proposition than in that very proposition. Presumably, such incoherence violates an epistemic duty to be rational. On the other hand, suppose that Ignatius does not answer “Yes” to Q2. That too seems to involve some kind of irrationality, since he is ignoring an obvious and relevant asymmetry in his epistemic situation, and again violating the epistemic duty to be rational. On each horn of the apparent dilemma, Ignatius seems to violate an epistemic duty to be rational.

One might hope to avoid all this confusion by moving to metalinguistic analogues of Q1 and Q2:

Q1M
Is the referent of “Eric Blair” the referent of “George Orwell”?
Q2M
Should I be more confident that the referent of “Eric Blair” is the referent of “Eric Blair” than that the referent of “Eric Blair” is the referent of “George Orwell”?

But metalinguistic ascent only postpones the problem. For Ignatius is perfectly confident of the trivial disquotational truths about the two names:

EB
The referent of “Eric Blair” is Eric Blair.
GO
The referent of “George Orwell” is George Orwell.

Given Ignatius’s confidence in EB and GO, the questions Q1M and Q2M are equivalent for him to the questions Q1 and Q2 respectively, so going metalinguistic makes no difference to the underlying problem.

Of course, the potential dilemma was presented quite sketchily, and needs to be probed much more deeply. Still, it does suggest that the line between ignorance and irrationality is much harder to draw than one might think.

# 7.10 Mitigations?

The overall mathematical framework of probability theory does not by itself enforce the semantics of direct reference. For example, one can model the epistemic predicament of an agent like Ignatius by treating “Eric Blair” and “George Orwell” as non-rigid designators, and make “Eric Blair is George Orwell” true at some worlds and false at others. Alternatively, one can achieve a similar effect by treating the names as directly referential and so rigid singular terms for distinct objects while interpreting “is” as expressing, not identity, but a relation of indiscernibility whose extension varies from world to world. The resultant “worlds” may be metaphysically impossible but epistemically possible, as discussed earlier. Such models may be useful for specific purposes, such as simulating the thinking, and especially the decision-making, of an agent who is unclear about the relevant identities. By ad hoc means, we can make sense of various ways of deviating from the real evidential relations.

There is a temptation to try to generalize and unify all such modeling devices into a “universal” framework for modeling evidential relations, or just evidential probability, with the flexibility to include all such deviations. That is a methodological error, and a dead end: the result will be too weak to be useful. It is better to work with a strong theory of genuine evidential relations, and to understand the hard cases as local deviations from the latter, perhaps the results of illusions of genuine evidential relations. Some modeling assumptions are theoretically fruitful without showing anything about the nature of the target phenomena. For instance, the highly successful Lotka-Volterra model of predator-prey population dynamics uses differential equations, and so treats the number of predators (say, foxes) and the number of prey (say, rabbits) as varying continuously, even though we know that the numbers are discrete (Weisberg 2013).

In theorizing about evidential relations, we do better to retain the systematic explanatory power of standard probability theory and standard intensional semantics, while acknowledging that to understand the messy complications of real cognitive life we must often track fine-grained differences at the level of representational form rather than represented content, in ways adapted to the case at hand.
