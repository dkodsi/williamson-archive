Response to Cohen, Comesaña, Goodman, Nagel, and Weatherson on Gettier Cases in Epistemic Logic

Timothy Williamson

Inquiry 56 (1) (2013): 77–96.

Abstract The five commentators on my paper ‘Gettier Cases in Epistemic Logic’ (GCEL) demonstrate how fruitful the topic can be. Especially in Brian Weatherson’s contribution, and to some extent in those of Jennifer Nagel and Jeremy Goodman, much of the material constitutes valuable development and refinement of ideas in GCEL, rather than criticism. In response, I draw some threads together, and answer objections, mainly those in the papers by Stewart Cohen and Juan Comesaña and by Goodman.

I. A Robust Dichotomy of Gettier Cases in Epistemic Logic

At the end of ‘Gettier Cases in Epistemic Logic’ (GCEL),1 I distinguish two types of Gettier case in epistemic logic: those more like Gettier’s original cases, in which the relevant justified true belief that fails to be knowledge follows from a justified false belief, and those more like fake barns cases, in which the justified true belief that fails to be knowledge follows from no justified false belief. Brian Weatherson argues that an argument in Jeremy Fantl and Matthew McGrath’s recent book goes wrong through using the term ‘Gettiered’ in a way ambiguous between just the first type of case and the disjunction of the two types.2 Stewart Cohen and Juan Comesaña observe that their epistemic models contain only Gettier cases of the first type.3 The dichotomy is worth analysing more abstractly.

Consider an epistemic model of the usual sort with an accessibility relation R\mathrm{R}, interpreted in terms of knowledge, and a doxastic accessibility relation S\mathrm{S}, interpreted in terms of justified belief in whatever sense is relevant. For simplicity in the informal interpretation of the models, we may assume that the agent has no unjustified beliefs. Given a world ww, R(w)\mathrm{R}(w) is the set of worlds epistemically accessible from ww and S(w)\mathrm{S}(w) is the set of worlds doxastically accessible from ww. Thus R(w)\mathrm{R}(w) is the strongest proposition known at ww and S(w)\mathrm{S}(w) is the strongest proposition (justifiedly) believed at ww. Given that knowledge entails justified belief, S(w)⊆R(w)\mathrm{S}(w)\subseteq \mathrm{R}(w). There are two possibilities.

First, suppose that S(w)\mathrm{S}(w) is false at ww, and a fortiori not known at ww. Then S(w)∪{w}\mathrm{S}(w)\cup\{w\} is true at ww, because it contains ww, and justifiedly believed at ww, because it follows from S(w)\mathrm{S}(w) and justified belief is deductively closed in such models. Moreover, any true proposition justifiedly believed at ww follows from S(w)∪{w}\mathrm{S}(w)\cup\{w\}. Thus S(w)∪{w}\mathrm{S}(w)\cup\{w\} is the strongest true proposition justifiedly believed at ww. Since R\mathrm{R} is reflexive, S(w)∪{w}⊆R(w)\mathrm{S}(w)\cup\{w\}\subseteq \mathrm{R}(w). There are two sub-possibilities. In one, S(w)∪{w}=R(w)\mathrm{S}(w)\cup\{w\}=\mathrm{R}(w), so ww is the only world in R(w)\mathrm{R}(w) missing from S(w)\mathrm{S}(w), and the agent’s only error at ww is to exclude ww itself; this looks like a very special case. Anyway, justified true belief and knowledge coincide at ww, so there are no Gettier cases of either type at ww. In the other sub-possibility, S(w)∪{w}⊂R(w)\mathrm{S}(w)\cup\{w\}\subset \mathrm{R}(w). Thus there is justified true belief in S(w)∪{w}\mathrm{S}(w)\cup\{w\} at ww, but not knowledge. Since S(w)∪{w}\mathrm{S}(w)\cup\{w\} follows from S(w)\mathrm{S}(w), in which there is justified false belief at ww, this is a Gettier case of the traditional type.4

Second, suppose that S(w)\mathrm{S}(w) is true at ww. Since S(w)\mathrm{S}(w) is the strongest proposition justifiedly believed at ww, all justified beliefs are true at ww. Again, there are two sub-possibilities. In one, S(w)=R(w)\mathrm{S}(w)=\mathrm{R}(w). Then justified true belief and knowledge coincide at ww, so there are no Gettier cases at ww. In the other sub-possibility, S(w)⊂R(w)\mathrm{S}(w)\subset \mathrm{R}(w). Thus there is justified true belief in S(w)\mathrm{S}(w) at ww, but not knowledge. Since S(w)\mathrm{S}(w) does not follow from any false proposition in which there is justified belief at ww, this is a Gettier case of a broadly fake barns type. Call Gettier cases of this type purely veridical, and Gettier cases of the former type impurely veridical.

Robert Stalnaker endorses the axiom Bp→BKp\mathrm{B}p\to \mathrm{B}\mathrm{K}p: ‘if you believe, you believe that you know’.5 That axiom, interpreted in terms of justified belief, excludes purely veridical Gettier cases, when R\mathrm{R} and S\mathrm{S} are the accessibility relations for the operators B\mathrm{B} and K\mathrm{K} respectively. For if Bp\mathrm{B}p is true at ww, by the axiom BKp\mathrm{B}\mathrm{K}p is true at ww, so if all justified beliefs are true at ww, then in particular Kp\mathrm{K}p is true at ww, so the justified true belief in pp is knowledge. Given the similarity of Cohen and Comesaña’s models to Stalnaker’s, it is therefore no surprise that they fail to obtain purely veridical Gettier cases.6 More specifically, suppose that in their models, like those in GCEL, S(<e, f>) = R(<f, f>): one justifiedly believes p at a world if and only if one knows p at the corresponding good world with the same appearance (they suggest no alternative candidate for S). Then their models validate Stalnaker’s axiom. For if Bp is true at <e, f>, then R(<f, f>) = S(<e, f>) ⊆ p, so Kp is true at <f, f>, so KKp is true at <f, f> because their models (like Stalnaker’s) validate the 4 axiom (the KK principle), so S(<e, f>) = R(<f, f>) ⊆ Kp, so BKp is true at <e, f>, as required. By contrast, the Stalnakerian structure does not exclude impurely veridical Gettier cases, as Cohen and Comesaña show.

Of course, the abstract analysis of the two types of Gettier case in epistemic logic shows only their formal consistency. To provide good evidence that such cases really can arise, we need comparatively realistic models. GCEL shows that simple, natural models of the perceptual knowledge of agents with limited powers of perceptual discrimination generate Gettier cases of both types. The models were originally developed for other purposes, and validate some very substantive principles (see Sections II and III). Much weaker assumptions suffice for generating both types of Gettier case.

Here is an impurely veridical Gettier case. Let <f, f> be a good case in which reality matches appearance and all one’s beliefs constitute knowledge, <e, f> a less good case in which appearances and beliefs are the same as in <f, f>, but reality is different enough for one to know less than in <f, f>, and <e∗, f> an even worse case in which appearances and beliefs are the same as before, but reality differs by enough more than in <e, f> for one to know even less, and indeed to have some false beliefs.7 Thus S(<f, f>) = S(<e, f>) = S(<e∗, f>) = R(<f, f>) ⊂ R(<e, f>) ⊂ R(<e∗, f>), and <e∗, f> is not in S(<e∗, f>), for one’s strongest belief S(<e∗, f>) is false at <e∗, f>. The general picture is just that some illusions are worse than others. Since S(<e∗, f>) ⊆ R(<f, f>) ∪ {<e∗, f>}, one has justified true belief in R(<f, f>) ∪ {<e∗, f>} at <e∗, f>. Nevertheless, one does not know R(<f, f>) ∪ {<e∗, f>} at <e∗, f>. For one has that knowledge only if R(<e∗, f>) ⊆ R(<f, f>) ∪ {<e∗, f>}; but then R(<e∗, f>) has only one member not in R(<f, f>), contradicting the fact that, by hypothesis, at least one world in R(<e∗, f>) is not in R(<e, f>), and therefore not in R(<f, f>), and at least one world in R(<e, f>), and therefore in R(<e∗, f>), is not in R(<f, f>). Since R(<f, f>) ∪ {<e∗, f>} follows from R(<f, f>), in which one has justified false belief at <e∗, f>, this Gettier case is impurely veridical. It requires neither of the assumptions that guide the discussion in GCEL: (#1) that one is not omniscient at <f, f>; and (#2) that knowledge decreases monotonically as reality increases in distance from a given appearance. This may speak to Jennifer Nagel’s qualms about both assumptions.

Here is a purely veridical Gettier case. With <f, f> as before, let <g, f> be a case in which appearances and beliefs are the same as in <f, f>, but reality is different enough for <g, f> to be almost but not quite outside S(<f, f>), and for less to be known there than at <f, f>, so R(<f, f>) ⊂ R(<f, g>). Thus at <g, f> although R(<f, f>) is true and justifiedly believed, one does not know R(<f, f>), since not R(<g, f>) ⊆ R(<f, f>). At <g, f> all one’s beliefs are true, because one’s strongest belief S(<g, f>) is true, so this Gettier case is purely veridical. It requires the minor assumption (#1) in GCEL, but not the major assumption (#2). This too may speak to Nagel’s qualms about (#2). Cohen and Comesaña’s rejection of the possibility of cases like <g, f> is criticized in the next section.

The models of perceptual knowledge with unspecific appearances in Jeremy Goodman’s paper generate both types of Gettier case, very similarly to mine.8 Analogous effects are likely in models inspired by Goodman’s considerations about Galton boards. Similarly, models of the main classes of model with which Brian Weatherson compares mine (favourably to mine) also generate both types of Gettier case.9 Thus the occurrence of both types of Gettier case is rather robust with respect to the details of reasonable implementations of the general idea of margins for error.

II. Cohen and Comesaña on Margins for Error

The commentators discuss alternatives of various sorts to the GCEL models. Of those alternatives, Cohen and Comesaña’s are the outliers. In particular, their models are the only ones not to permit Section I’s dichotomy of Gettier cases. Both Goodman and Weatherson have already explained why they find Cohen and Comesaña’s models unsatisfactory; I will briefly give my very similar reasons.

Say that one has cliff-edge knowledge of the real value of the parameter when it is in fact e if and only one either knows that it is at least e or knows that it is at most e. For example, if one is judging by naked eye the height of a tree that is in fact 13.793 metres high, one has cliff-edge knowledge of it only if one knows either that it is not 13.792 metres high or that it is not 13.794 metres high. Given normal limits on human powers of perception and estimation, in a vast range of cases none of us has such cliff-edge knowledge. We need margins for error. In Cohen and Comesaña’s models (and in similar models of Stalnaker’s), by contrast, one has cliff-edge knowledge at many worlds. Weirdly, their models predict that one has cliff-edge knowledge just in case appearances differ severely enough from reality! Specifically, at the world <e, f> in a model of their type, where e is the real value, f the apparent value, and c what they still call the margin for error: if e ≤ f − c one has cliff-edge knowledge that the real value is at least e; if e ≥ f + c one has cliff-edge knowledge that the real value is at most e; if appearance differs from reality by less than c one has no cliff-edge knowledge of the real value. In Cohen and Comesaña’s alternative universe, a hefty difference between appearance and reality is just what one needs for perfect discrimination, on one side or the other. In Weatherson’s terms, this violates the principle that you don’t know more by measuring worse. Moreover, since their models validate the KK principle, whenever one has cliff-edge knowledge that the real value is at least e, one also knows that one knows that it is at least e, and knows that one knows that one knows that it is at least e, and so on, and whenever one has cliff-edge knowledge that the real value is at most e, one also knows that one knows that it is at most e, and knows that one knows that one knows that it is at most e, and so on. Almost equally strangely, Cohen and Comesaña claim that their models capture the margin for error insight, on the grounds that the most one ever gets to know about the real value is that it lies in the interval [f − c, f + c]. But, as Goodman points out, the point of the margin for error is to achieve safety, whereas cliff-edge beliefs are paradigms of danger; the point is not simply to make knowledge unspecific.

Matters would be different if we had perfect knowledge of our maximum error, independently of the current perceptual episode. For then, since perfect knowledge of appearances has been granted for the sake of argument, one could indeed know that the real value lay in the interval [f − c, f + c], even if it was in fact at one of the extremes, and any value outside the interval would be definitively excluded. But that is not the usual human situation. A more realistic view, as Nagel makes clear, is that ‘we learn about our margins of error in roughly the same way as we learn about environmental parameters’. Indeed, Cohen and Comesaña include worlds with more extreme differences between appearance and reality in their models, but give them an utterly implausible epistemological treatment. In effect, for a fixed apparent value f and any interval [g, h] containing [f − c, f + c], they treat the condition that the real value lies in [g, h] as luminous: whenever it obtains, one knows that it obtains—even if the condition obtains in some sceptical scenarios (where g or h is far from f ).

Cohen and Comesaña offer two main reasons for preferring their models to mine. The first is that in my models, unlike theirs, one knows, and may therefore assert, some Moorean-sounding conjunctions of the forms ‘p and I do not believe that I know p’ and (once probabilities are introduced)10 ‘p and I am almost certain that I do not know p’. They are right that such conjunctions sound bad. But, given the assumption that knowledge is what warrants assertion,11 which they are granting for the sake of argument, one can easily explain why such conjunctions sound bad even if the speaker knows them. For they are tantamount to ‘p and I do not believe that I have warrant to assert p’ and ‘p and I am almost certain that I do not have warrant to assert p’ respectively. They resemble the commands ‘Stand to attention!—and I do not believe that I have authority to order you to stand to attention’ and ‘Stand to attention!—and I am almost certain that I do not have authority to order you to stand to attention.’ More generally, performing a speech act A and then adding ‘and I do not believe that I am entitled to perform A’ or ‘and I am almost certain that I am not entitled to perform A’ will sound bad, even if the speaker is in fact entitled to perform A. Whether one is entitled to perform a given speech act is often not a matter on which one has any special first-person authority. One may be the officer commanding a soldier without knowing it: one may not believe that one is in command of him, indeed, one may be certain that one is not in command of him. One may try issuing the command anyway, in the hope that it will be obeyed; the command is in fact quite proper. Then one blurts out one’s doubts about one’s authority in an epistemically impeccable assertion, which is nevertheless unwise man-management. One’s combined speech act sounds bad because the second half blatantly undermines the normally intended effect of the first half on the hearer. Compare: ‘How are you feeling today?—and I have no interest in your answer.’ Similarly, the conjunctive assertion ‘p and I do not believe that I know p’ or ‘p and I am almost certain that I do not know p’ sounds bad because the second conjunct blatantly undermines the normally intended effect of the first conjunct on the hearer, by giving reason not to rely on it, even if the speaker does in fact know both p and that she does not believe that she knows p and is almost certain that she does not know p. Thus Cohen and Comesaña’s first consideration in favour of their models fails, since the bad sound of the conjunctions they invoke has a natural explanation in my models too.12

Cohen and Comesaña’s second main reason for preferring their models is that they do not generate purely veridical Gettier cases. As they point out, fake barns cases have attracted significantly less consensus than more traditional Gettier cases in the epistemological community. However, that too is no advantage for their models. For, first, the majority view in the epistemological community treats fake barns cases as genuine counterexamples to various putatively sufficient conditions for knowledge. Although majority views are sometimes wrong, Cohen and Comesaña do not argue that it is wrong here. They merely invoke the sociological fact of the minority view, which does not outweigh the sociological fact of the majority view. Second, and more important, even if one doubts the majority view, one can still accept the purely veridical Gettier cases generated by margins for error, which are not exactly the same in structure as the original fake barns cases. Thus Cohen and Comesaña’s second consideration in favour of their models also fails.13

Since Cohen and Comesaña’s models have such epistemologically absurd consequences, and their reasons for preferring them evaporate, I turn to the more nuanced and plausible alternatives developed and defended by Jeremy Goodman.

III. Goodman against Improbable Knowing

Goodman’s models share some common features with Cohen and Comesaña’s. In particular, they agree that the agent’s knowledge about the real value of the parameter at issue is maximal over an extended interval of real values (for a given apparent value), not at a single point, as in my models. That is how they block improbable knowing, cases of knowing something which on one’s evidence one almost certainly does not know. More specifically, in Goodman’s models, let f be the central apparent value, x the distance |e − f | between it and the real value e, and b the radius of the safe haven. For Goodman, b> 0. Then if x ≤ b (one is inside the safe haven), what one knows is that the real value is in the interval [f − (b + c), f + (b + c)]; if x> b (one is outside the safe haven), what one knows is that the real value is in the interval [f − (x + c), f + (x + c)]. When c = 0, this almost reduces to a Cohen and Comesaña model, except for the one-sided asymmetry about f which they postulate in the known interval. For Goodman, however, c> 0; unlike Cohen and Comesaña, he does not reject margins for error (in all but name). Thus his models differ from theirs in that, inside the safe haven, one does not know that one is inside the safe haven; one only knows the weaker proposition that the real value is in the wider interval [f − (b + c), f + (b + c)] of non-extraordinary values. That is why his models, unlike theirs, generate purely veridical Gettier cases.

From a mathematical point of view, the feature of Goodman’s models that obstructs improbable knowing looks suspect. Let r(x) be the breadth of one’s ignorance for given x, the length of the shortest interval one knows the real value to lie within. Thus if x ≤ b, r(x) = 2(b + c); if x> b, r(x) = 2(x + c). As a function of x, r(x) is continuous but not everywhere differentiable on Goodman’s assumption that b> 0. It is not differentiable at b, since it has gradient 0 to the left and gradient 2 to the right. By contrast, my models correspond to the limiting case of Goodman’s where b = 0, so r(x) = 2(x + c) throughout, which is differentiable (and continuous) everywhere. The failure of r to be everywhere differentiable on Goodman’s assumptions is more than a slight inelegance. It suggests an ad hoc twist to his models, specifically designed to avoid improbable knowing. Moreover, it sits uneasily with his appeal to Gaussian probability distributions, which lead naturally to models with everywhere differentiable functions. Of course, Goodman’s motivation for his models might dissolve their ad hoc air; we shall see later whether it does so.

One feature of our practice in everyday examples lends some plausibility to safe havens. We prefer not to think in units much smaller than the required margin for error. For example, if, not knowing much about Peter, we speculate about how long it is since he last did his laundry, we do so in whole numbers of weeks or months rather than minutes or seconds. If we only form beliefs in such coarse-grained units, our knowledge will typically remain constant across wide ranges of circumstances. But this is no very deep feature of the epistemic phenomena, nor does Goodman treat it as such. For once outside the safe haven in his models, our knowledge decreases, because r(x) increases, strictly and continuously as a function of x, rather than going up in a step function. Thus the safe haven is not supposed to reflect a coarse-grained choice of units. Once we bear that in mind, we should find puzzling the claim that over an extended region there is no epistemic cost at all to increasing distance between the central apparent value and the real one. We must therefore examine Goodman’s own reasons for making the claim.

Goodman’s initial reason concerns unspecific appearances. An appearance is unspecific just in case it is veridical over an extended region of real values: in each case, the parameter is as it appears to be. For example, although the tree appears to have a height in a given interval, there is no single height it appears to have. By contrast, GCEL treats appearances as maximally specific, which has the advantage of simplifying the models, reducing the number of modelling choices and thereby the scope for gerrymandering results.

A first thought is that there is no extra epistemic cost for a mismatch between appearance and reality whenever an appearance is veridical, so in a model with unspecific appearances the agent’s knowledge of the real value is maximal throughout the extended interval of veridicality. If the interval is wide enough, such a model will not exhibit improbable knowing. But that is not Goodman’s thought. For he insists, concerning how things in fact appear to be, that they should be known to be that way throughout the interval of maximal knowledge. The latter claim and the first thought jointly entail that whenever the real value is in the interval of veridicality, it is known to be in that interval. But such knowledge leaves no margin for error: it corresponds to the special case of Goodman’s models for c = 0, an extreme he explicitly avoids. It returns us to models relevantly like Cohen and Comesaña’s, whose defects have already been explained. Goodman’s working assumption is that c> 0, in which case the safe haven of maximal knowledge is narrower than the interval of veridicality. Thus the epistemic cost of increasing proximity to values outside the interval of veridicality starts rising before the real value leaves the interval of veridicality. So Goodman cannot and does not appeal to the first thought to resolve puzzlement about the lack of epistemic cost for increasing proximity to values outside the interval of veridicality when the real value is still within his safe haven.

Goodman’s concern is that what appears to obtain should be known to obtain over an extended interval of normal cases. He quotes me endorsing a similar desideratum. But it may need some refinement concerning knowledge that things are exactly as they appear. Consider visual perception of shape. In normally good circumstances I look at a sheet of paper with a black circle on a white background. To me, the black patch looks perfectly circular. It does not look imperfectly circular (almost but not quite perfectly circular). Nevertheless, in any actual case, the patch is at best imperfectly circular. Even if by some miracle the patch is in fact perfectly circular, I cannot know by looking that it is perfectly circular: my eyesight and visual judgement lack the perfection such knowledge would require. Similarly, two lines may look perfectly straight and perfectly parallel, but I cannot know by looking that they are indeed so. Such cases are quite common and normal. They require only very mild watering-down of the original desideratum: I can still know by looking that the patch is at least approximately circular. If the word ‘look’ also has some other sense in which the patch only looks at least approximately circular, then that ambiguity merely reinforces the need for some refinement of the original desideratum.14 In at least one ordinary sense, perceptual appearances are often more specific than our perceptual knowledge can be. Moreover, we have some awareness of our epistemic limitations, and often resist the temptation to form perceptual beliefs as specific as the way things appear to us. We refrain from believing that things are exactly as they appear, and just believe that they are approximately as they appear. Given our self-restraint in belief formation, it may still be normal for our perceptual beliefs to constitute knowledge.

Such examples of specific appearances give me what I need to argue for improbable knowing, without even appealing to possible creatures with perceptual systems very different from our own. In my models, one never believes that the real and apparent values are identical: one just believes that they are close. Although one’s strongest belief about the real value constitutes knowledge only in the very best case, one’s less specific beliefs about it constitute knowledge over a wide range of cases. It is not abnormal for beliefs about the real value to constitute knowledge in my models.

In the final section of his paper, Goodman allows specific appearances for the sake of argument, but even so argues against improbable knowing on other grounds. His general idea, drawing on work by Maria Lasonen-Aarnio, is that ‘a belief is justified only if it manifests a disposition to know in normal circumstances’.15 He implements that idea in the models at issue as the constraint that ‘What you justifiably believe is known in all normal worlds with the same appearances’, formalized thus:

Disposition to Know:
If |f−e|≤c|f - e| \leq c, then R(<e,f>)⊆S(<e*,f>)\mathrm{R}(<e,f>) \subseteq \mathrm{S}(<e^{\ast},f>)

As he points out, my models violate Disposition to Know. For what one justifiably believes in a case is what one knows in the best case with the same appearance: S(<e∗, f>) = R(<f, f>). Thus Disposition to Know implies that if |f − e| ≤ c, then R(<e, f>) ⊆ R(<f, f>). In my models, R(<e, f>) becomes larger as e diverges from f. Thus Disposition to Know fails whenever 0 < |f − e| ≤ c.

Let us grant the plausible-sounding general idea that a belief is justified only if it manifests a disposition to know in normal circumstances, at least for the sake of argument. Is Disposition to Know the proper implementation of it in my models?

One question concerns the term ‘normal’. If it means something like ‘good’, with no presumption that most close worlds are normal, then perhaps only worlds of the form <f, f> are normal. In that case the proper implementation would simply be the constraint that R(<f, f>) ⊆ S(<e∗, f>), which my models trivially satisfy.16 However, let us also grant that normal conditions should not be so rare, and that for some positive constant c∗, normal conditions obtain if and only if |f − e| ≤ c∗, where c∗ ≤ c. That yields this potential weakening of Disposition to Know:

Weak Disposition to Know:
If |f−e|≤c*|f - e| \leq c^{\ast}, then R(<e,f>)⊆S(<e*,f>)\mathrm{R}(<e,f>) \subseteq \mathrm{S}(<e^{\ast},f>)

Since c∗ > 0, my models violate even Weak Disposition to Know.

Weak Disposition to Know shares with Disposition to Know the assumption that, in implementing the general idea that a belief is justified only if it manifests a disposition to know in normal circumstances, we should take it to mean that a belief in a proposition p is justified only if it manifests a disposition to know p itself in normal circumstances. But that reading makes the original idea much less plausible on the intended sense of justification (Section IV discusses alternative conceptions of justification in a more general setting). Consider a working engineer who has been properly trained to rely in her calculations on the complicated mathematical formulas in a handbook, because their proofs are too difficult for all but specialist mathematicians. The handbook is in general authoritative, but as a result of a minor misprint one formula p is slightly inaccurate. Presumably, the engineer’s belief in p would count as justified in the relevant sense (in which some false beliefs are justified). But her belief in p, a purely mathematical proposition, does not manifest a disposition to know p in normal circumstances, because p is a necessary falsehood. Although the sequence of marks that actually expresses p could in principle have expressed a different and true proposition, in all normal circumstances it expresses p. The natural fallback here is to point out that, although the engineer’s justified belief in p does not manifest a disposition to know p itself in normal circumstances, it manifests a disposition to know other propositions in normal circumstances: for example, all the correct formulas in the handbook. Many similar examples could be given, including ones where the believed proposition is contingent, but false in all normal circumstances.

A more liberal understanding of the original idea is therefore required: a belief in a proposition p is justified only if it manifests a disposition to know a suitable variety of relevant truths in normal circumstances. But this more liberal idea does not mandate Weak Disposition to Know, let alone Disposition to Know. For S(<e∗, f>) is the strongest thing one believes at <e∗, f>. One’s belief in S(<e∗, f>) is just one instance of a general policy of believing all propositions q for which S(<e∗, f>) ⊆ q: masses of those propositions are known at all worlds <e, f> in which |f − e| ≤ c, or at least at all worlds in which |f − e| ≤ c∗ (R(<e, f>) ⊆ q). Thus my models can be reconciled with the more plausible version of the idea that a belief is justified only if it manifests a disposition to know in normal circumstances.

The policy of believing at <e, f> all supersets of S(<e∗, f>) aims at maximizing knowledge (not true belief). A more cautious policy might put more weight on minimizing belief that fails to constitute knowledge, but in doing so would give up some potential knowledge, such as of R(<f, f>). But it is not at all clear that models implementing the more cautious policy are more faithful to actual human beings. Both cognitive science and common sense suggest that we are more prone to overestimate our cognitive capacities than to underestimate them. Nor is it clear that the knowledge-maximizing policy is at all unreasonable. Improbable knowing is quite consistent with the general idea that a belief is justified only if it manifests a disposition to know in normal circumstances, charitably understood.

One more line of argument in Goodman’s paper should be mentioned. He invokes the Gaussian distribution of noise and random errors in many situations, of which he takes the Galton board as representative.17 In that case, he complains, the analogue of my models ‘incorrectly predicts that what we can know about where the ball will land is a function of where it will in fact land’. I agree that the analogue of my models makes that prediction, and that it is presumably false. As Goodman very plausibly argues, ‘What we can know about where the ball will land depends not only on where it will land but also on the details of its trajectory.’ However, this is just an instance of a generic problem for a model-building methodology. To make models intelligible and computationally tractable, one omits all but a few key variables. One thereby represents the relationships between the included variables as independent of the excluded variables, which they usually are not. Complicating the model to include some previously excluded variables will modify its predictions to some extent, while still omitting some relevant variables. The process can be iterated indefinitely. That is the nature of modelling.

In principle, Goodman recognizes these inevitable limitations of the model-building methodology, and recognizes that I recognize them. So what is their specific significance for models of improbable knowing? He objects that the complexities of processes such as a ball falling down a Galton board falsify constraint (#2) in GCEL, that one’s ignorance is an increasing function of the gap between appearance and reality. Of course they falsify (#2). It is exactly the sort of idealization one obtains by omitting all but a few key variables, and was intended as such. Indeed, Goodman’s own models make almost the same idealization, since they satisfy the very similar constraint that one’s ignorance is a non-decreasing function of the gap between appearance and reality. It too is falsified by the very complexities of random walks down a Galton board that Goodman emphasizes. But what evidence have we that complicating the models to take some of these complexities into account would eliminate the prediction of improbable knowing?

As Goodman also recognizes, one could construct a measure of the eccentricity of trajectories on the Galton board, and replace (#2) by the constraint that one’s ignorance is an increasing function of the eccentricity of the ball’s trajectory (##2), or its analogue for the relation between appearance and reality, and build more complex analogues of the GCEL models. A minor contrast with the latter is that the eccentricity measure would attain its minimum on more than one trajectory, since the image of any trajectory under left-right reflection about the centre of the board is another trajectory of presumably equal eccentricity. Nevertheless, the trajectory is as near as can be to vertical in comparatively few cases. One knows most when the ball’s trajectory is of least eccentricity, but presumably one does not know that its trajectory is of least eccentricity. One knows only that its trajectory is of eccentricity at most k, where k is some not too great constant. We may reasonably expect the number of trajectories of least eccentricity to be small compared with the number of trajectories of eccentricity at most k. Since by hypothesis the new models validate the constraint (##2), they make it improbable on one’s evidence at trajectories of least eccentricity that one knows that the ball’s trajectory is of eccentricity at most k, even though one does in fact know that its trajectory is of eccentricity at most k. Thus improbable knowing recurs in the new models that take path-dependence into account.

According to Goodman, the problem with the strategy just sketched is that

it is hard to see a natural parameter [TW: eccentricity measure] in the Galton board case of which our ignorance is a function; one can always concoct pairs of trajectories that agree on the parameter but intuitively differ as regards what we can know.

Perhaps so, but the general difficulty of finding a completely satisfying measure of eccentricity is no evidence that improbable knowing would not occur. One can easily construct moderately natural measures of eccentricity that are moderately well correlated with ignorance and generate cases of improbable knowing in just the way explained. Of course, one could always write the absence of improbable knowing into a model by hand, along the general lines of Goodman’s models. But absent a serious motivation for doing so, that would just be gerrymandering, of no significant evidential value. As we have seen, Goodman’s considerations about path-dependence and Gaussian distributions, interesting though they are in themselves, provide no independent evidence against improbable knowing. We saw earlier that his other considerations, about unspecific appearances, normality, and the justification of belief, also leave us without good reason to write the absence of improbable knowing into a model. It is more reasonable to be guided by the simpler, more natural models and so postulate improbable knowing, in the reasonable expectation that complicating the models to take into account further variables in natural ways will retain improbable knowing in a suitably refined form.

IV. Knowledge First, and Cohen and Comesaña on Justification and Excusability

Gettier cases are interesting because they constitute a crucial obstacle to an epistemological project of internalist inspiration: to analyse knowledge in terms of belief and its norms, perhaps eked out with some auxiliary elements (such as causation), all conceived as somehow more basic than knowledge. Internalists may treat truth and justification as both norms of belief, albeit of different kinds. Whether an alleged Gettier case presents an internal difficulty for the internalist project depends on whether the belief at issue is justified by internalist standards. Internalists cannot resolve their own difficulty by noting that the belief is not justified by some externalist standard.

Typically, internalists contrast justification with knowledge by claiming that, unlike knowledge, justification supervenes on appearances. In the good case, I know that I have hands. In the corresponding bad case, appearances to me are the same and I still believe that I have hands, but I lack hands and therefore do not know that I have hands. Uncontroversially, in the good case my belief that I have hands is justified. Therefore, by internalist standards, in the bad case my belief that I have hands is justified too. GCEL’s modelling of justified belief reflects those internalist standards. Specifically, if two worlds in a GCEL model have the same appearance constituent (i.e., are of the form <e, f> and <e∗, f>), then one has the same justified beliefs at those worlds (because S(<e, f>) = R(<f, f>) = S(<e∗, f>)). Moreover, one has a justified belief in a proposition p at a world in a GCEL model if and only if one knows p at some world with the same appearance in the model. For if one has a justified belief in p at <e, f>, then S(<e, f>) ⊆ p, so R(<f, f>) ⊆ p, so one knows p at <f, f>, which has the same appearance as <e, f>. Conversely, if one knows p at some world <e∗, f> with the same appearance as <e, f>, then R(<e∗, f>) ⊆ p, so R(<f, f>) ⊆ p because R(<f, f>) ⊆ R(<e∗, f>) by the constraint (#2), so S(<e, f>) ⊆ p, so one has a justified belief in p at <e, f>. Of course, internalists will not analyse having a justified belief in p as being in a case with the same appearance as one in which one knows p, because they regard justified belief as more basic than knowledge; in particular, they do not want to make all analyses of knowledge in terms of justified belief circular. Nevertheless, they may still regard having a justified belief in p as at least roughly equivalent to being in a case with the same appearance as one in which one knows p. Although Section III’s engineering example indicates that the two sides are not in general perfectly equivalent, internalists should still accept the identification of S(<e, f>) with R(<f, f>) as good enough for present modelling purposes. GCEL’s treatment of justified belief is a concession to internalism for the sake of argument, not a Trojan horse for externalism.

Of course, I am no internalist. On my own view of justification, which GCEL mentions only to put it aside for dialectical purposes, a belief is fully justified if and only if it constitutes knowledge, so justification no more supervenes on appearances than knowledge does. Thus, in the bad case, one’s false belief that one has hands is not fully justified, because it does not meet the norm for belief. However, one has a first-rate excuse for violating the relevant norm, because one is in a case with the same appearance as one in which one satisfies the norm. One is in no position to know that one is violating the norm. Internalists wrongly identify justified belief with belief excusable in such a way.18 For dialectical purposes, GCEL grants that identification in describing the models.

Unfortunately, in their response Cohen and Comesaña badly misunderstand what I take the excuse to be. They misread a passage from an earlier article of mine as saying that in the bad case one’s excuse for falsely believing p is that one believes that one knows p.19 The passage says no such thing. I wrote ‘the victim of a paradigmatic sceptical scenario is not to be blamed for forming false beliefs under the misapprehension that they constitute knowledge’, but what one is not to be blamed for is not generally the same as the excuse in virtue of which one is not to be blamed: ‘She is not to be blamed for missing the appointment; she had been knocked down by a bus.’ Clearly, in the sceptical scenario one’s excuse involves the nature of the scenario, in which appearances are the same as in the good case in which the same beliefs constitute knowledge; one is in no position to know that one is violating the norm for belief. As a result of their misreading, Cohen and Comesaña labour the obvious point, which I of course have always accepted, that believing that one knows p is not by itself a good excuse for falsely believing p, since one’s belief that one knows may itself be utterly irrational. They also correctly observe that internalists do not classify all sorts of excuses for belief as justifications, for example the excuse that the agent was emotionally overwrought. But the GCEL models only concern excuses of the sort internalists themselves classify as justifications. The more serious result of Cohen and Comesaña’s misreading is that they miss the overall dialectical context of GCEL.

In any case, Cohen and Comesaña argue directly against my positive view of justification. I argue that, since a belief is fully justified only if it constitutes knowledge, no false belief is fully justified. For Cohen and Comesaña, justified belief in the relevant sense is rational belief. They claim that my view is false of rational belief:

It entails that one cannot have a fully rational false belief. But falsity is not a failure of rationality. Rationality requires one to conform one’s beliefs to one’s evidence. When one’s evidence is misleading, rationality will require one to adopt a false belief.

They do not say what they take one’s evidence to be. I defend the equation E = K, on which one’s evidence is simply what one knows.20 In their discussion, they do not mention the equation, let alone try to argue against it. Given E = K, the requirement to conform one’s beliefs to one’s evidence is the requirement to conform one’s beliefs to one’s knowledge. Believing only what one knows is then a natural understanding of that requirement. Misleading evidence is still possible, because truths can make a falsehood probable. The known truth that one appears to have hands can make the falsehood that one has hands probable. However, since truths never entail a falsehood, they never fully support a falsehood (support it in the strongest way); they support it, if at all, only partially. Rationality sometimes requires one to adopt a false belief only if it sometimes requires one to adopt a belief not fully supported by one’s evidence; Cohen and Comesaña give no argument that rationality ever requires that. Nor is the equation E = K the only view of evidence to yield the point that one’s evidence never fully supports a falsehood; any view of evidence on which only truths are evidence has the same consequence. For one’s evidence fully to support a falsehood, the evidence must contain falsehoods, in which case some truths are inconsistent with one’s evidence: hardly an attractive view.21

In a sceptical scenario, beliefs only partially supported by one’s evidence appear to be fully supported by it. One appears to be fully conforming one’s beliefs to the evidence, but really is conforming only partially. Hence one appears to be fully conforming to the standard of rationality, when really one is deviating from it, even if only slightly. Thus the subject in the bad case is being slightly irrational. Given that the demands of rationality are not perfectly luminous, such cases are only to be expected.

Cohen and Comesaña offer one more objection to the difference in justification I discern between the good and bad cases. They use my analogy between the justification of belief and the justification of action. Here is their example: ‘Suppose you shoot and kill a terrorist about to blow up a school. I shoot at a terrorist about to blow up a school but miss. I did my best, so we are equally blameless.’ They argue that we are equally justified in trying to kill the terrorist, the fact that you succeeded while I failed constituting no difference in justification between us.22 They conclude: ‘By analogy, you are no better justified than I in trying to know p, i.e. believing p, simply because you succeeded and I did not.’

They treat believing p as an attempt to know p because they are granting, at least for the sake of argument, that believing aims to be knowing. However, this is an odd way to construe the analogy. Typically, before one tries to do something, one already wants to do it, and one’s desire is causally relevant to one’s attempt: for example, before I shot at the terrorist, trying to kill him, I already wanted to kill him, and my desire to kill him was causally relevant to my attempt to kill him. On Cohen and Comesaña’s understanding of the correspondence between belief and action, the analogues for belief of those points about action are these: before I believed p, I already wanted to know p, and my desire to know p was causally relevant to my belief in p. But that sounds like a case of wishful thinking!

A more open-minded style of inquiry starts with the desire to know whether P, not the desire to know that P.23 An attempt to know whether P may indeed result in a false belief that P. That outcome is worse than mere failure to form a belief either way, through lack of relevant evidence. It is like my shooting at the terrorist, missing him, and killing an innocent bystander instead. Even if I was justified in trying to kill the terrorist, and did my best, it does not follow that I was justified in killing the innocent bystander. I was not justified in killing her. I have only an excuse—‘I was trying to kill the terrorist’—which her friends and relatives, and the police and the courts may or may not accept. If you were justified in killing the terrorist, we differ dramatically with respect to justification, contrary to Cohen and Comesaña’s claim.

In an intermediate case, involving the justification of both belief and action, I mistake an innocent person for a terrorist about to blow up a school, shoot at him, and kill him. A film was being made, but I could not see the cameras, and shot the actor who was playing the part of a terrorist about to blow up the school. I was not justified in killing him. I have only an excuse—‘I thought he was a real terrorist’—which his friends, relatives, and fans, and the film company, the police, and the courts may or may not accept. Just as I have some sort of excuse, but not justification, for killing an innocent person, so I have some sort of excuse, but not justification, for the false belief that he was a real terrorist, on the basis of which I took the action (vigilantes prefer internalist epistemology). When the analogy between the justification of belief and the justification of action is pursued more carefully than by Cohen and Comesaña, it tells against, not for, their claim of equality of justification between the good and bad cases.

The upshot is that Cohen and Comesaña have failed to raise any difficulty for externalism about justification. Of course, as already emphasized, the models in GCEL are committed to no such externalism. Indeed, the interpretation of B as an operator for justified rather than blameless belief is a concession to internalism, dialectically motivated.

How can we breathe a more externalist spirit into the models? It is not just a matter of reinterpreting B as an operator for blameless rather than justified belief. If we want to get more realistic, we should complicate the models to allow for the non-luminosity of appearances and beliefs: as Nagel bring outs, agents are not really omniscient, even potentially, about how things appear to them and what they believe.

A more general issue concerns the role of the apparent value f as a basic constituent of a model <e, f> on a par with the real value e. From an externalist perspective, it would be more satisfying for appearances to emerge from aspects of the model concerned solely with knowledge and reality.

Fortunately, in this respect, some GCEL models have a promising feature. Say that a proposition p in a model is independent of appearances just in case the truth of p at a world depends only on the real value at that world, more formally, just in case for all values e, f, and f∗ of the relevant parameter, p is true at <e, f> if and only if p is true at <e∗, f>. Then one can prove that if all the same propositions independent of appearances are known at two worlds in a model over the real numbers of the sort described in GCEL, then the apparent value in those worlds is the same. In other words, the appearance component of a model supervenes on what is known about the reality component.24 Both internalists and externalists can accept this supervenience thesis (under the simplified conditions of the models) while disagreeing on its interpretation: to a first approximation, for internalists, the reality and appearance components explain the facts about knowledge of the reality component, while for knowledge-first externalists the facts about knowledge of the reality component explain the appearance component. The GCEL models over the real numbers permit both interpretations.

As for belief, all GCEL models validate an explicit definition of it in terms of knowledge: what one believes is what one does not know one does not know (Bp = ¬K¬Kp).25 Of course, the equivalence does not hold universally outside the simplified conditions of the models. For example, babies neither believe that the battle of Hastings was in 1066 nor know that they do not know that the battle of Hastings was in 1066. Nevertheless, the approximate equivalence may be a useful first step towards understanding belief in terms of knowledge.26 Again, the models permit both internalist and externalist takes on the equivalence. A proper exploration of possible constraints in epistemic logic inspired by knowledge-first epistemology would take at least another paper.
