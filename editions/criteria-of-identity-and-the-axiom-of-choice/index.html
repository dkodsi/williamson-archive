Criteria of identity and the Axiom of Choice

Timothy Williamson

The Journal of Philosophy 83 (1986): 380–394.

At times, even the best philosophical theories must resort to Procrustean techniques. A frequent occasion is the attempt to confer respectability on some class of entities by giving them that obscure privilege, a “criterion of identity”.1 For such a thing is, minimally, a relation that meets certain formal constraints, which the only obvious candidate may fail: it must be hacked into the shape appropriate to a criterion of identity, if the search for one is not to be given up altogether. Many theories of personal identity face such problems, whose logical structure this paper analyzes and generalizes.

Identity, that relation which each thing has only to itself, is probably clearer than any concepts in terms of which one might seek to explain it. However, although one should not expect an analysis of the bare relation x=yx = y, given a function ff one might in suitable circumstances have the right to expect an analysis of the relation f(x)=f(y)f(x) = f(y). For, unless ff is one-one, the latter relation between xx and yy is not itself identity. Thus one might be able to reach a nontrivial conclusion of the form:

(1)

∀x∀y(f(x)=f(y)↔Rxy)\forall x\, \forall y\, (f(x) = f(y) \leftrightarrow Rxy)

Here the things whose identity the criterion is for—the f(x)f(x)s—are in principle distinct from the things it relates—the xxs (the variables ‘xx’ and ‘yy’ range over the arguments, but not necessarily the values, of ff). In certain cases, (1) assumes philosophical interest. Frege gave a paradigmatic example: let ‘xx’ and ‘yy’ range over lines, f(x)f(x) be the direction of xx, and RR the relation of parallelism; then (1) states the criterion of identity for directions (74–79). For it seems that, if we could not think of directions as the direction of lines, then we could not think of them at all, that our thought about directions depends on a conceptually prior grasp of what it is for two lines to be parallel. I do not seek to clarify these familiar notions; I merely note their prevalence. On another interpretation of (1), ‘xx’ and ‘yy’ range over sets, f(x)f(x) is the cardinality of xx, and RR is the relation of one-one correspondence; then (1) states the criterion of identity for cardinal numbers (not quite as Frege conceived them). Or ‘xx’ and ‘yy’ can range over spatiotemporal locations, f(x)f(x) can be the table (if any) at xx, and RR some physical relation such that (1) states a criterion of identity for tables. This case seems to differ in rationale from the first two, since in it xx is more, not less, abstract than f(x)f(x), but such differences will not affect the analysis below. Again, ‘xx’ and ‘yy’ can range over particular experiences, f(x)f(x) can be the subject of xx, and RR some physical or psychological relation such that (1) states a criterion of identity for subjects of experience.

Since identity is an equivalence relation (it is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive), (1) requires RR to be an equivalence relation too. In fact, without further information about ff, that is all that (1) requires of R, since one can easily show that if RR is an equivalence relation, there is a function ff that makes (1) true. However, such an RR can sometimes be hard to find. For example, let ‘xx’ and ‘yy’ range over color samples and f(x)f(x) be the perceived color of xx. It is tempting to take RR as the relation of indistinguishability (in color): but, since the indistinguishability of xx from yy and of yy from zz does not entail the indistinguishability of xx from zz, this relation is formally ruled out as a candidate for R, because it is not transitive. If ‘xx’ and ‘yy’ range over person-stages and f(x)f(x) is the person of whom xx is a stage, otherwise promising interpretations of RR have difficulty with the person who, when old, remembers being middle-aged and, when middle-aged, remembers being young, but, when old, does not remember being young, or with the results of supposed personal fission or fusion. Again, if f(x)f(x) is a physical magnitude of which (for whatever reason) one is trying to give an operational definition, the test for the identity of f(x)f(x) and f(y)f(y) is likely to give a positive result when xx and yy differ by sufficiently little (otherwise our measuring instruments would have to be infinitely precise): since the sum of many little differences is not itself little, the favored candidate for RR will not be transitive.2 In some of these cases, however, the unsuccessful candidate relation SS, in spite of its formal ineligibility, may still seem to us central to the identity of f(x)f(x)s; then we must somehow exhibit an RR, to make (1) true, as a modification of SS. In other words: given a relation SS, which is not an equivalence relation, how can we best approximate it by an equivalence relation RR, thus preserving as much as possible of the original intuition [which could be stated by putting ‘SS’ for ‘RR’ in (1)]?

Beneath the philosophical task of understanding the identity conditions of certain entities lies the logical task of defining a clear sense in which an equivalence relation RR can approximate a relation SS, which is not itself an equivalence relation. We cannot insist that SS be both necessary and sufficient for R, but we do have an obvious pair of fall-back positions: either that S be sufficient for RR but not necessary, or that it be necessary but not sufficient. For neither of the following formulas by itself requires S to be an equivalence relation, given that RR is:

(2)

∀x∀y(Sxy→Rxy)\forall x\, \forall y\, (Sxy \rightarrow Rxy)

(3)

∀x∀y(Rxy→Sxy)\forall x\, \forall y\, (Rxy \rightarrow Sxy)

By accepting (2) or (3) [and (1)], we preserve one or other half of the oversimple intuition that SS is the criterion of identity for f(x)f(x)s.

In (2) and (3), ‘SS’ should be regarded as a constant and ‘RR’ as a variable; for we are seeking conditions on equivalence relations RR in terms of SS which will make (1) plausible. For this, (2) or (3) alone would be too weak. For we could satisfy (2) by taking RR to be the tautologous relation and (3) by taking it to be identity, and neither of these equivalence relations is likely to be a plausible criterion of identity for f(x)f(x)s. In either case, we should expect to be able to find a closer approximation to SS.3 We say that RR approximates SS at least as well as R′R' does if and only if whenever R′R' and SS agree, so do RR and SS:

(4)

∀x∀y((R′xy↔Sxy)→(Rxy↔Sxy))\forall x\, \forall y\, ((R'xy \leftrightarrow Sxy) \rightarrow (Rxy \leftrightarrow Sxy))

R approximates S better than R’ does if and only if RR approximates S at least as well as R’ does and R’ does not approximate S at least as well as RR does. When S is fixed, both of these second-order relations are obviously transitive; approximating S better than is irreflexive and asymmetric; approximating S at least as well as is reflexive, and one can show that if each of RR and R’ approximates S at least as well as the other does, then RR and R’ are equivalent: for simplicity, I shall assume that equivalent relations are identical, so that approximating S at least as well as is antisymmetric. Given a notion of an eligible candidate, two promising constraints on RR come immediately to hand, a weak one and a strong one. The weak constraint (which resembles Pareto optimality) is that no eligible candidate should approximate S better than RR does; the strong constraint is that RR should approximate S better than any other eligible candidate does. We have in turn two possible definitions of an eligible candidate R to hand: either as an equivalence relation that obeys (2) or as an equivalence relation that obeys (3). To use a mathematical analogy: we can choose either the greatest lower bound or the least upper bound. Of course, our choice is likely to be determined by philosophical considerations: S will sometimes be plausible as a sufficient condition for f(x)=f(y)f(x)=f(y), sometimes as a necessary condition, rarely as both. The two cases will be treated in turn.

(A) Suppose that SxySxy is plausible as a sufficient condition for f(x)=f(y)f(x)=f(y). For example, let SS be a relation on person-stages xx and yy of an unrivaled and very high degree of the right sort of psychological resemblance and continuity: S will fail to be transitive (consider a person who changes gradually); it will be plausible as a sufficient, but not as a necessary, condition for personal identity. The standard move at this point is—if one still regards S as the key to personal identity—to take as a criterion of identity the ancestral of SS: xx and yy are stages of the same person if and only if there is a finite chain beginning with x and ending with yy, each member of which has S to the next.4 A few details aside, we can use the preceding analysis to show that this is in fact the general solution for this case. Since we are taking SxySxy as sufficient for f(x)=f(y)f(x)=f(y), we define an A-eligible candidate as an equivalence relation RR that obeys (2). The relation in (4) of approximating S at least as well as, restricted to A-eligible candidates, can then be shown to be equivalent simply to the extensional inclusion of RR in R′R'. Thus RR obeys the weak A-constraint if and only if RR is an equivalence relation that includes SS and does not properly include any equivalence relation that also includes S; R obeys the strong A-constraint if and only if RR is an equivalence relation that includes S and is included in every equivalence relation that includes S. We now define in terms of SS a relation that uniquely obeys the strong A-constraint; a fortiori, it also uniquely obeys the weak A-constraint. When SS is reflexive and symmetric, the defined relation will simply be its ancestral. We recursively define a relation SiS_i for each natural number ii, and then define RR in terms of them, as follows:

S0xyS_0xy if and only if x=yx = y

Si+1xyS_{i+1}xy if and only if ∃z(Sixz&(Szy∨Syz))\exists z\, (S_i xz \mathbin{\&} (Szy \vee Syz))

RxyRxy if and only if ∃iSixy\exists i\, S_i xy

It is elementary to check that: RR is an equivalence relation; RR obeys (2); if R′R' is an equivalence relation that obeys (2), R′R' includes RR. Thus RR obeys the strong A-constraint; it does so uniquely because if R′R' obeys the strong A-constraint, RR and R′R' include each other and so are identical. Thus if we seek a criterion of identity for f(x)f(x)s which makes SxySxy sufficient and as near as it can be to necessary for f(x)=f(y)f(x) = f(y), our desiderata unambiguously single out RR, as defined above. The second case is more difficult. For in case (A) we seek the smallest equivalence relation that includes SS, that is, the intersection of all equivalence relations that include SS, and the intersection of a set of equivalence relations is itself an equivalence relation; in case (B) we seek the largest equivalence relation that is included in SS, that is, the union of all equivalence relations that are included in SS, and the union of a set of equivalence relations need not itself be an equivalence relation.

(B) Suppose that SxySxy is plausible as a necessary condition for f(x)=f(y)f(x)=f(y). For example, let SS be a sort of physical or psychological connectedness which a stage of either of the results of an hypothetical case of personal fission has to a stage of the person when about to undergo the fission but not to a stage of the other result: S will thus fail to be transitive; it will be plausible as a necessary, but not as a sufficient, condition for personal identity.5 Or again, let S be indistinguishability in color, as a relation on color samples: S will fail to be transitive; it will be plausible as a necessary, but not as a sufficient, condition for identity of perceived color.6 The same can be said of operational definitions of physical magnitudes, where S will be a relation of indistinguishability by our measuring instruments. Suppose that we still regard SS as the key to the identity of f(x)f(x)s, and take SxySxy as necessary for f(x)=f(y)f(x)=f(y). Consequently, we define a B-eligible candidate as an equivalence relation RR that obeys (3). The relation in (4) of approximating S at least as well as, restricted to eligible candidates, can then be shown to be equivalent simply to the inclusion of R′R' in RR. Thus RR obeys the weak B-constraint if and only if RR is an equivalence relation that is included in S and is not properly included in any equivalence relation that is also included in SS; RR obeys the strong B-constraint if and only if RR is an equivalence relation which is included in SS and which includes every equivalence relation that is included in S. We must now ask whether at least one relation obeys the weak or strong B-constraint and, if so, whether more than one does.

We may assume S to be reflexive; for otherwise there will be no B-eligible candidates [if RR is reflexive and obeys (3), S is reflexive]. We may also assume SS to be symmetric; for an equivalence relation is included in S if and only if it is included in the symmetric relation Sxy&SyxSxy\mathbin{\&}Syx. If Sxy is not already reflexive and symmetric, we replace it by the reflexive and symmetric relation x=y∨(Sxy&Syx)x=y\vee(Sxy\mathbin{\&}Syx). In all the examples above, SS was reflexive and symmetric. Thus we may further assume SS not to be transitive; for otherwise it would be an equivalence relation, in which case our problem would not arise, since (in the absence of any other obstacle) S could itself be taken as the criterion of identity for f(x)f(x)s.

The position now turns out to be as follows.7 We can show that no relation obeys the strong B-constraint. If we assume the Axiom of Choice [that for any set XX of nonempty sets, there is a function ff such that for every x∈Xx\in X, f(x)∈xf(x)\in x], we can show that several nonequivalent relations obey the weak B-constraint. If we do not assume the Axiom of Choice, we cannot in general show that any relation obeys the weak B-constraint, although we may be able to show this for particular values of SS: for the assumption that, for every reflexive symmetric relation SS, some relation obeys the weak B-constraint in respect of S is actually equivalent to the Axiom of Choice.8 What is the philosophical significance of these results?

The Axiom of Choice, though once controversial, is now for most working mathematicians an element of their normal equipment, if not a frequently used one.9 I take this to be a sensible attitude. However, I shall not argue for the plausibility of the Axiom here. Those who reject it are in the following position. If they wish to treat SS as a necessary and as nearly as possible sufficient condition for the identity of f(x)f(x)s (whatever they happen to be), then they are assuming that the relation f(x)=f(y)f(x)=f(y) obeys the weak B-constraint in respect of SS. Since they reject the Axiom of Choice, they will allow that in some formally analogous cases no relation obeys the weak B-constraint in respect of S: they are therefore under some obligation to explain why, in the case at issue, they think that there is such a relation. Sometimes, we may simply be told that there is such a relation because f(x)=f(y)f(x)=f(y) is it; but then the motive for seeking a criterion for the identity of f(x)f(x)s becomes obscure, if we have this independent grasp of the relation f(x)=f(y)f(x)=f(y). If any interesting explanation of the identity of f(x)f(x)s in terms of xxs is to be given, we seem to require an argument in terms of xxs, not of f(x)f(x)s, for the existence of a relation obeying the weak B-constraint in respect of S. If the set of xxs is countable, such an argument can be given; for the existence of a choice function for subsets of a countable set can be proved without appeal to the Axiom of Choice, and so the use of the Axiom in proving the existence of a relation obeying the weak B-constraint was unnecessary in this case. If the xxs are empirical items, however, it may in turn be hard to prove the existence of a one-one correspondence between xxs and natural numbers without appeal to the Axiom of Choice. The progress of the argument will depend upon the details of the individual case, but clearly, those who take approach (B) to the identity of f(x)f(x)s and refuse to use the Axiom of Choice have a good deal of work to do. For they face the apparently live possibility that their concept of f(x)f(x)s is incoherent, in that no relation meets what is demanded of the criterion of identity for f(x)f(x)s.

The application of either the Axiom of Choice or the above substitutes for it requires us to treat relations as in some sense set-theoretic entities, such as sets of ordered pairs. This raises a problem: a criterion of identity is usually expected to work for all possible worlds, not just the actual one; now the existence of a set plausibly requires the existence of its members, but then the set of ordered pairs that works as a criterion of identity for one world will not work for another, since it will not relate objects that exist in the latter world but not in the former. To overcome this problem, one could think of a relation as being realized by different sets of ordered pairs in different worlds, and, as it were, apply the set-theoretic arguments to each world separately. However, that would leave two further problems unsolved: first, it would not give us any help with transworld identities; secondly, since more than one set of ordered pairs would obey the weak B-constraint in respect of SS in any world where S failed to be transitive, some kind of transworld Axiom of Choice would be required to form a complete relation by choosing one appropriate set of ordered pairs from each world (for this reason, we cannot dispense with the Axiom in the case of personal identity merely on the grounds that only finitely many fissions and fusions will occur: for even excluding possible worlds in which infinitely many occur, some form of Choice is generally required to choose one member from each of an infinite collection of finite sets). A bolder approach would be to apply the Axiom directly to a class of ordered pairs of possible individuals and worlds. Having noted but not solved these problems, I shall assume for the sake of argument that relations are either extensional entities to which the Axiom of Choice can be applied or intensional entities to which some plausible, intensional variant of the Axiom of Choice can be applied.

Given an extensional or intensional version of the Axiom of Choice, several nonequivalent relations obey the weak B-constraint in respect of any given reflexive, symmetric, nontransitive relation SS, and no relation obeys the strong B-constraint in respect of SS. Thus if the identity of f(x)f(x)s is determined only as such that SS is a necessary and as nearly as possible sufficient condition for it, then the identity of f(x)f(x)s is indeterminate [unless there is a better way than (4) of measuring the approximation of one relation by another]. Less ontologically: if we try to specify f(x)=f(y)f(x)=f(y) as the relation that obeys the strong B-constraint in respect of SS, we fail because there is no such relation; if we try to specify f(x)=f(y)f(x)=f(y) as the relation that obeys the weak B-constraint in respect of S, we fail because there are many such relations. We can even prove something stronger. Let TT be any equivalence relation for which S is necessary: then some relation RR for which T is sufficient obeys the weak B-constraint in respect of S. That is: we can make any (positive) judgments of identity we like among f(x)f(x)s, providing that they are consistent with the principle that Sxy is necessary for f(x)=f(y)f(x)=f(y), and some criterion of identity for f(x)f(x)s, with apparently as much claim to be correct as any has, will make those judgments come out true. Since the discussion has been rather abstract, let us apply it to particular cases.

Suppose we are seeking a criterion of identity for perceived color. If the perceived color of xx and the perceived color of yy are identical, we cannot perceptually distinguish xx from yy in respect of color: so the (perceptual) indistinguishability (in color) of xx and yy is necessary for the identity of the perceived colors of xx and yy. Conversely, we should not want our criterion of identity for perceived colors to give rise to cases where x and y were deemed to have distinct perceived colors, but anything that is deemed to have the same perceived color as xx is indistinguishable from anything that is deemed to have the same perceived color as yy (in particular, xx is indistinguishable from yy). For then the distinction between the perceived colors of xx and yy would seem to have no perceptual basis: we should not be talking of perceived color. So we demand that our criterion of identity for perceived colors not give rise to cases of that anomalous kind. This demand, however, is simply equivalent to the demand that the equivalence relation of having the same perceived color (RR) should obey the weak B-constraint in respect of indistinguishability (SS) (for in the above case the criterion of identity that was like ours except that it identified the perceived colors of xx and yy would be an equivalence relation that would be included in SS and would properly include our criterion of identity, so the latter would not obey the weak B-constraint; conversely, if an eligible candidate RR did not obey the weak B-constraint, it would by definition be properly included in an equivalence relation R’ which was in turn included in SS, and the cases where R’xy held and Rxy did not would be anomalous). Now SS is a reflexive, symmetric, nontransitive relation; so the previously mentioned results apply. Several nonequivalent relations obey the weak B-constraint; none obeys the strong one. We have several possible criteria of identity to choose among. To see the extent of the choice, consider any three color samples a, b, and c for which transitivity breaks down: a is indistinguishable from b and b from c but not a from c. Define two relations on color samples, as follows:

TxyTxy if and only if x=y∨(x=a&y=b)∨(x=b&y=a)x=y\vee(x=a\mathbin{\&}y=b)\vee(x=b\mathbin{\&}y=a)

T′xyT'xy if and only if x=y∨(x=b&y=c)∨(x=c&y=b)x=y\vee(x=b\mathbin{\&}y=c)\vee(x=c\mathbin{\&}y=b)

Both TT and T′T' are equivalence relations which are included in SS. Thus there are possible criteria of identity RR and R′R' for perceived colors—relations that obey the weak B-constraint in respect of SS— such that TT is sufficient for RR and T′T' for R′R'. Hence RR holds between aa and bb, and consequently not between bb and cc (for it is an equivalence relation which is included in SS); similarly, R′R' holds between bb and cc, and consequently not between aa and bb. In other words, wherever SS itself breaks down as a criterion of identity (because of its nontransitivity), there is (so far as we can see) an element of arbitrariness as to what we should replace it by. Given two nonequivalent relations that obey the weak B-constraint in respect of SS, in virtue of what could one be the true criterion of identity for perceived colors and the other a false one? We are being pushed toward the view that, wherever the transitivity of indistinguishability in color breaks down, judgments about the identity of perceived colors lack determinate truth values and, thence, to the view that the concept of the perceived color of an object is not well defined.

The same structure of considerations can be applied to the case of personal identity. Suppose that we seek a criterion of personal identity in some relation between person-stages, under the following philosophical assumptions. We find the idea of overlapping persons absurd, so we are always prepared to speak of the person to whom a given stage belongs. For x and y to be stages of the same person, we regard it as necessary that in some sense (whose physical and psychological components we can specify) either x should have developed out of yy or yy should have developed out of xx; this relation S is reflexive and symmetric. If fissions or fusions occur, S will not be transitive. Our only further demand on a criterion of personal identity is that it should not give rise to cases where xx and yy are deemed to be stages of different people, but every stage that is deemed to be of the same person as x has S (the relation that matters for personal identity) to every stage that is deemed to be of the same person as y (in particular, x has S to y)—for then the supposed distinction between the persons to whom x and yy belong would be a mere caprice, running contrary to the central intuition that if xx and yy are SS-related, they are stages of the same person unless some fact about S-relations prevents them from being so. Given these assumptions, the search for a criterion of personal identity is the search for a relation that obeys the weak B-constraint in respect of S. If S is nontransitive, several nonequivalent relations obey the weak B-constraint; none obeys the strong one. For example, suppose that fission occurs: let x be a stage before the split, and yy and zz be stages of the two results, so that they have SS to xx but not to each other. Then there will be two criteria of personal identity, both meeting the required constraint, one of which makes xx and yy but not x and z stages of the same person, the other of which reverses these judgments (there may also be criteria that meet the required constraint and make neither y nor z stages of the same person as x, but only if other fissions or fusions occur). In virtue of what could one be the true criterion of personal identity and the other a false one? We are being pushed toward the view that judgments of personal identity can lack determinate truth values and, thence, to the view that the concept of the person to whom a given stage belongs is not well defined.

One might look for finer-grained considerations that would single out some of the relations that obey the weak B-constraint from the others. For example, one might try to minimize the cardinality of that part of the extension of SS which is disjoint from the extension of the criterion of identity at issue. However, the source of the divergence between criteria of identity is essentially symmetrical—two things related to a third thing but not to each other—so there is reason to think that nonarbitrary finer-grained considerations will make little difference.

Thus if one thinks of the identity of certain entities as what the necessary condition S approximates to, one will be led to think that, until an arbitrary choice has been made among criteria of identity that meet the weak B-constraint, it is indeterminate what entities one is thinking about. Even once the choice has been made, one can hardly regard this way of articulating the world—that is, of partitioning the xxs, on which the relation S is defined—as more insightful or realistic than the alternatives. Worse, in cases where the Axiom of Choice is required to guarantee the existence of relations obeying the weak B-constraint, we shall be unable to specify any of them uniquely and, thus, unable to choose one of them: the indeterminacy will be ineliminable. Nor have we any reason to think that the relations that do obey the weak B-constraint will be at all natural: they may be gruesomely arbitary, in which case they will not give a satisfying rationale for the identity of f(x)f(x)s.

(B’) One could avoid that arbitrary choice if one abandoned the assumption that only one item of the kind for which a criterion of identity is sought can correspond to a given item of the kind that the criterion of identity will relate. If a homogeneous object can have several perceived colors, or several persons can overlap, then one does not have the same problem of competing criteria of identity. Indeed, it is doubtful that one has a criterion of identity, for there is no well-defined function ff, and so terms of the form ‘f(t)f(t)’ can no longer appear in judgments of identity; I shall speak of a criterion of unity for ffs (persons, perceived colors, . . .) rather than of identity for f(x)f(x)s. Now the relation f(x)=f(y)f(x)=f(y) cannot be replaced by the relation that x has to y when there is an ff to which they both belong: for, given three objects xx, yy, and zz, we shall want to know whether there is an ff to which all three belong, whereas even if we know that for each two of xx, yy, and zz there is an ff to which both belong, we still do not know whether these ffs are the same for each pair.10 More generally, given a set of xxs, we shall want to know whether there is an ff to which they all belong. We require a criterion for whether a set of stages includes all and only those which belong to a certain person or for whether a set of color samples includes all and only those which have a certain perceived color. Indeed, since such questions can be raised when some of the stages or color samples do not exist in the same possible worlds as others, we had better speak of properties rather than of sets. Thus, whereas a criterion of identity was a relation on xxs, a criterion of unity is a property of properties of xxs. For example, given a property F, we want to know whether there is a person such that F is (equivalent to) the property of being a stage of that person, or whether there is a perceived color such that F is (equivalent to) the property of having that color.

Given these differences, we can preserve our understanding of ffs in terms of S. As before, we can think of the xxs that belong to a given ff as all having S to each other, and as excluding an xx only if this first condition would otherwise be violated. The first condition is simply:

(5)

∀x∀y((Fx&Fy)→Sxy)\forall x\, \forall y\, ((Fx \mathbin{\&} Fy) \rightarrow Sxy)

The second condition is that, whenever F’ obeys (5) when put in place of F, and F’ includes F, then F includes F’. (5) differs from (3) only in having the relation Fx&FyFx\mathbin{\&}Fy in place of the equivalence relation Rxy. Indeed, the present approach can be seen as a variation of the previous one, in which a B’-eligible candidate is defined as a relation of the form Fx&FyFx\mathbin{\&}Fy (rather than an equivalence relation) which is included in SS. A relation of the form Fx&FyFx\mathbin{\&}Fy can even be defined as a relation RR that obeys the condition:

(6)

∀x∀y(Rxy↔(Rxx&Ryy))\forall x\, \forall y\, (Rxy \leftrightarrow (Rxx \mathbin{\&} Ryy))

Note, however, that whereas a B-eligible candidate is supposed to be a criterion for the identity of f(x)f(x)s in general, a B’-eligible candidate is supposed to be criterion for the unity of one particular ff. Still, we can define weak and strong B’-constraints in the usual way in terms of B’-eligible candidates. Strictly, these will be constraints on the relation Fx&FyFx\mathbin{\&}Fy; for simplicity, we speak of constraints on the property FF. FF obeys the weak B’-constraint if and only if it obeys the two conditions that we have just laid down; F obeys the strong B’-constraint if and only if it obeys (5) and whenever a property F’ obeys (5) when put in place of FF, FF includes F′F'.11

Close analogues of the previous results, including the connection with the Axiom of Choice, can be proved for these redefined notions. As before, we take S to be reflexive and symmetric but not transitive. We can show that no property obeys the strong B’-constraint. If we assume the Axiom of Choice, we can show that several nonequivalent properties obey the weak B’-constraint. This point means only that, for example, there are persons and perceived colors. Fortunately, we can prove (using the Axiom of Choice) a stronger result, analogous to that for equivalence relations, of much greater interest. Let G be any property that obeys (5) when put in place of F: then some property F for which G is sufficient obeys the weak B’-constraint in respect of S. Thus if we are given a set of stages all of which are connected in the right way, we can be sure that there is at least one person to whom they all belong; similarly, given a set of color samples no two of which are distinguishable in color, we can be sure that there is at least one perceived color that they all share. If, on the other hand, we do not assume the Axiom of Choice, we cannot in general show that any F obeys the weak B’-constraint, although we may be able to show this for particular values of SS; for the assumption that, for every reflexive symmetric relation S, some property obeys the weak B’-constraint in respect of SS is actually equivalent to the Axiom of Choice. Thus those who take approach (B′) to the unity of ffs and refuse to use the Axiom of Choice have a good deal of work to do, unless they are prepared to swallow such apparently counterintuitive possibilities as that several person-stages, all of which are connected to each other in the way necessary for personal unity, nevertheless do not belong to any person, or that a perceived color sample should have no perceived color whatsoever. Many of the philosophical remarks about the connections with the Axiom of Choice for case (B) remain valid for case (B′).

There are, however, some philosophically significant differences between the two cases. Suppose that we are given the Axiom of Choice: then, where the analysis of the identity of f(x)f(x)s in terms of equivalence relations RR obeying (3) leads to the indeterminacy of f(x)f(x)s, the analysis of the unity of ffs in terms of properties F obeying (5) leads to the multiplicity of ffs. Different equivalence relations obeying the weak B-constraint constituted rival conceptions of f(x)f(x)s; different properties obeying the weak B’-constraint merely constitute different ffs. However, these ffs will overlap where S fails to be transitive. Consider again color samples x, y, and z such that x is indistinguishable from y and y from zz, but not x from zz. For approach (B), there were conceptions of the identity of perceived colors such that the perceived color of y was identical with that of x but not with that of zz, and rival conceptions such that the perceived color of y was identical with that of zz but not with that of x. For approach (B’), there are perceived colors that x and y share and other perceived colors that yy and zz share—the only strangeness being that y, uniform in appearance, should have several different perceived colors. One might say that the first approach buys theoretical light at the expense of metatheoretical darkness, whereas the second buys metatheoretical light at the expense of theoretical darkness.

The precise interpretation of the results must vary with the philosophical theory within which they are to be interpreted. I shall not attempt to survey all the possible variations. Clearly, a crucial determinant will be the degree to which the nature and existence of f(x)f(x)s or ffs is supposed to be merely derivative from xxs, as related by S. If their nature and existence is nonderivative, then perhaps we could have direct knowledge of it, which we could use as a short cut, to avoid reliance on the Axiom of Choice; moreover, we might be able to argue that one of the competing criteria of identity is the right one, or perhaps that only some of the overlapping properties that obey the weak B’-constraint correspond to genuine ffs. At the other extreme, philosophers in the tradition of Russell and Carnap would treat f(x)f(x)s or ffs as logical constructions—that is, very likely, as sets—of xxs, in which case whatever assumptions they make about xxs, as related by S, must be plausible in their own terms, without reference to f(x)f(x)s or ffs. Special mention should be made of the problems for the nominalist who wishes to explicate f(x)f(x)s or ffs as some kind of mereological sums of xxs: for example, taking stages of a person’s life as literal parts of that person. Such a philosopher, refusing to invoke sets, seems to require a mereological version of the Axiom of Choice. How should such an axiom be formulated? What plausibility would it have? In particular, might it not undermine the claim that a nominalist ontology creates fewer epistemological problems than do its rivals?

We may be tempted to feel that f(x)f(x)s or ffs are more trouble than they are worth and to conclude that there are no such things. However, though we may be able to say with relative equanimity that there are no perceived colors, it is harder for us to suppose that there are no such things as ourselves—and what else do we mean by a ‘person’? A more prudent response might be to abandon the project of understanding f(x)f(x)s or ffs in terms of xxs and an S that obeys (3) or (5). After all, (2) gave neater results.

Moral: As approximate criteria of identity, sufficient conditions beat necessary ones.

Appendix: Proof Sketches

In this appendix, a relation on a set XX will be taken as simply a set of ordered pairs of elements of XX. ZF or NBG set theory with individuals suffices for the proofs.12 Corresponding theorems can also be proved for class forms of the Axiom of Choice.

(I) The following propositions are equivalent:

(a)

The Axiom of Choice.

(b)

Let SS be a reflexive symmetric relation on a set XX and TT an equivalence relation on XX such that T⊆ST \subseteq S; then there is an equivalence relation RR on XX such that T⊆R⊆ST \subseteq R \subseteq S and for any equivalence relation R′R' on XX, if R⊆R′⊆SR \subseteq R' \subseteq S then R=R′R = R'.

(c)

Let SS be a reflexive symmetric relation on a set XX; then there is an equivalence relation RR on XX such that R⊆SR \subseteq S and for any equivalence relation R′R' on XX, if R⊆R′⊆SR \subseteq R' \subseteq S then R=R′R = R'.

Proof (a) ⇒\Rightarrow (b). The Axiom of Choice is equivalent to Zorn’s lemma (a nonempty partially ordered set in which every chain has an upper bound has a maximal element).13 The set YY of equivalence relations R′R' on XX such that T⊆R′⊆ST \subseteq R' \subseteq S is nonempty (it includes TT), partially ordered by inclusion, and the union of any chain in YY is an upper bound in YY for that chain. Let RR be a maximal element of YY.

(b) ⇒\Rightarrow (c). Let TT in (b) be the identity.

(c) ⇒\Rightarrow (a). Let ZZ be a set of nonempty sets. Define XX as

{(x,y):y∈Z,x∈y∪{Z}}.\{(x,y): y \in Z,\ x \in y \cup \{Z\}\}.

Define a reflexive symmetric relation SS on XX to hold between (w,x)(w,x) and (y,z)(y,z) iff x=zx=z and either w=yw=y or w=Zw=Z or y=Zy=Z. Let RR be as in (c). For y∈Zy \in Z, suppose that ((Z,y),(u,v))∈R((Z,y),(u,v)) \in R and ((Z,y),(u′,v′))∈R((Z,y),(u',v')) \in R: then v=v′=yv=v'=y (because R⊆SR \subseteq S) and, since ((u,v),(u′,v′))∈R⊆S((u,v),(u',v')) \in R \subseteq S, either u=u′u=u' or u=Zu=Z or u′=Zu'=Z. Thus the RR-equivalence class of (Z,y)(Z,y) contains at most two elements. Suppose that it contains only (Z,y)(Z,y). Since yy is nonempty, let x∈yx \in y. By the Axiom of Regularity, x≠Zx \ne Z. Thus only (x,y)(x,y) has SS to (x,y)(x,y); so the RR-equivalence class of (x,y)(x,y) is {(x,y)}\{(x,y)\}.

Let R′=R∪{((Z,y),(x,y)),((x,y),(Z,y))}R' = R \cup \{((Z,y),(x,y)), ((x,y),(Z,y))\}; then R′R' is an equivalence relation on XX such that R⊆R′⊆SR \subseteq R' \subseteq S but R≠R′R \ne R', which contradicts the choice of RR. Thus for y∈Zy \in Z, the RR-equivalence class of (Z,y)(Z,y) has the form {(Z,y),(x,y)}\{(Z,y),(x,y)\}, where x∈yx \in y; mapping yy to xx gives a choice function for ZZ.

(II) The following propositions are equivalent:

(a)

The Axiom of Choice.

(b)

Let SS be a reflexive symmetric relation on a set XX and GG a subset of XX such that G2⊆SG^2 \subseteq S; then there is a subset FF of XX such that G2⊆F2⊆SG^2 \subseteq F^2 \subseteq S and for any subset F′F' of XX, if F2⊆F′2⊆SF^2 \subseteq F'^2 \subseteq S then F=F′F=F'.

(c)

Let SS be a reflexive symmetric relation on a set XX; then there is a subset FF of XX such that F2⊆SF^2 \subseteq S and for any subset F′F' of XX, if F2⊆F′2⊆SF^2 \subseteq F'^2 \subseteq S then F=F′F=F'.

Proof (a) ⇒\Rightarrow (b). The set YY of subsets F′F' of XX such that G2⊆F′2⊆SG^2 \subseteq F'^2 \subseteq S is nonempty (it includes GG) and partially ordered by inclusion, and the union of any chain in YY is an upper bound in YY for that chain. By Zorn’s Lemma, let FF be a maximal element in YY.

(b) ⇒\Rightarrow (c). Let GG in (b) be the null set.

(c) ⇒\Rightarrow (a). The Axiom of Choice is equivalent to Hausdorff’s Maximal Principle (a partially ordered set has a maximal chain).14 If ≤\leq partially orders XX, let S={(x,y):x∈X,y∈X,x=y∨x≤y∨y≤x}S = \{(x,y): x \in X, y \in X, x=y \vee x\leq y \vee y\leq x\}; SS is reflexive and symmetric. FF, as in (c), is a maximal chain for (X,≤)(X,\leq).
