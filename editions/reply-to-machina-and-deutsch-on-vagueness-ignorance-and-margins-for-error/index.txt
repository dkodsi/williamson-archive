# Reply to Machina and Deutsch on Vagueness, Ignorance, and Margins for Error

Timothy Williamson

Acta Analytica 17 (29) (2002): 47–61.

In their paper “Vagueness, Ignorance, and Margins for Error” Kenton Machina and Harry Deutsch criticize the epistemic theory of vagueness. This paper answers their objections. The main issues discussed are: the relation between meaning and use; the principle of bivalence; the ontology of vaguely specified classes; the proper form of margin for error principles; iterations of epistemic operators and semantic compositionality; the relation or lack of it between quantum mechanics and theories of vagueness.

Keywords: Vagueness; epistemicism; bivalence; margin for error; coordinated attack; compositionality; quantum mechanics.

Kenton Machina and Harry Deutsch [2002] make several mutually independent criticisms of the epistemic theory of vagueness and the arguments for it advanced in my book [1994]. I will rebut their criticisms one by one.1 My sections follow theirs in the obvious way.

# ‘Williamson’s approach’

Machina and Deutsch are willing to agree with me that the extensions of typical vague predicates supervene on ‘a mishmash of linguistic conventions, overlapping linguistic communities, and changing linguistic patterns and community memberships, in interaction with a changing world’, where ‘No one in these communities knows exactly where the boundaries of the extensions are, and if forced to make a decision, they do not agree with one another’.2 Unlike me, they think that such a description ‘almost implies [sic] that precise extension boundaries do not exist’. Although they do not quite say that the description is inconsistent with epistemicism, they do present it as strong evidence against the view. But they do not explain why they count the description as any evidence at all against epistemicism. They do not even try to argue that epistemicism implies (or ‘almost implies’) some description of the use of vague predicates that is inconsistent with the one quoted above.

Machina and Deutsch may have been misled by their own terminology. They repeatedly characterise the epistemicist as holding that a vague predicate has precise boundaries. Since the terms ‘vague’ and ‘precise’ are normally used as a contrasting pair, that initial characterisation already presents epistemicism as committed to an internal incongruity. The description of the supervenience base for the extension of a vague predicate merely makes vivid what the use of a vague predicate is like. But what does it mean to call the boundaries of a vague predicate, rather than the vague predicate itself, ‘precise’? Presumably, Machina and Deutsch mean (on behalf of the epistemicist) that everything either falls within the boundaries or fails to do so. According to the epistemicist, it is a matter of logic that all boundaries are precise in that sense. Only an anti-epistemicist who already assumes that only precise predicates have such boundaries should regard the word ‘precise’ as an apt term for such boundaries themselves.3 Let us call them, more neutrally, ‘excluded middle boundaries’. Then the epistemicist holds that excluded middle boundaries can supervene on messy, fluctuating, heterogeneous, casual use. Since Machina and Deutsch do not claim that such use (described non-semantically) itself violates the law of excluded middle, it is quite unclear why they suppose it to constitute evidence that boundaries that supervene on it violate that law.

Someone might accept a pair of claims like these:

(Ia)
If an object $\mathrm{o}$ falls under a predicate $P$, then some local feature of the supervenience base makes $\mathrm{o}$ fall under $P$.
(Ib)
If an object $\mathrm{o}$ does not fall under a predicate $P$, then some local feature of the supervenience base makes $\mathrm{o}$ not fall under $P$.

To fall under a predicate here is to fall within the boundaries of its extension; to make $\mathrm{o}$ (not) fall under $P$ is to be sufficient for $\mathrm{o}$ (not) to fall under $P$. The notion of a local feature of the supervenience base is intended to be quite informal; a local feature is relatively specific (not the whole supervenience base) and has obvious relevance to whether the object falls under the predicate. In a borderline case, we may suppose, no local feature makes $\mathrm{o}$ fall under $P$ and no local feature makes $\mathrm{o}$ not fall under $P$. If we can contrapose on (Ia) and (Ib), then $\mathrm{o}$ does not fall under $P$ and does not not fall under $P$, which is a contradiction. At any rate, someone who accepted (Ia) and (Ib) might well be suspicious of the claim that vague predicates have extensions with excluded middle boundaries. However, the epistemicist rejects (Ia) and (Ib). The extension of a vague predicate may depend on global features of the supervenience base. The supervenience relation may be governed by default principles: if the supervenience base is globally insufficient for $\mathrm{o}$ to fall under $P$, it is thereby globally sufficient for $\mathrm{o}$ not to fall under $P$.4

Machina and Deutsch do not commit themselves to (Ia) and (Ib), nor do they commit themselves to any other principles that would explain why they regard the nature of the supervenience base as any evidence at all that vague predicates lack excluded middle boundaries. They find the postulation of such boundaries implausible, but they provide no non-question-begging argument against it from the nature of the supervenience base.

# ‘The argument from “classical logic”’

That everything either falls under a predicate or fails to do so is an instance of the law of excluded middle, which is a theorem of classical logic. That logic has massive theoretical virtues such as simplicity, strength, entrenchment and success in applications. Machina and Deutsch insist that classical logic is not sacrosanct. They ‘urge a spirit of exploration and flexibility’; why not? But the epistemicist does not claim that classical logic is sacrosanct, in the sense that it is methodologically vicious to consider alternatives. In my book I consider various non-classical logics in detail and argue, independently of classical assumptions, that none of them provides an adequate explanation of vagueness (in particular, they all come to grief on higher-order vagueness). It would be methodological madness to abandon classical logic at the first sign of trouble, but if the phenomena of vagueness present sufficiently strong evidence against it, we might reasonably look for alternatives. Although Machina and Deutsch treat the nature of the supervenience base for vague predicates as strong evidence against classical logic, we saw in the previous section that they provide no justification for doing so. They fail to remove the initial presumption in favour of classical logic.5

The anti-epistemicist typically denies that vague predications are bivalent. The epistemicist replies by arguing that, under plausible assumptions, the denial of bivalence leads to contradiction. According to Machina and Deutsch, we can ‘see (without looking at its details) that [the epistemicist argument] must fail’ (their italics). Their argument is merely that if $\mathrm{P}$ is not bivalent, ‘nothing about this situation requires $\mathrm{P}$ to have more than one truth value’. But this claim takes for granted that the situation is indeed possible, which is just what the epistemicist’s argument calls into question. So we do better to ignore their attempt at a pre-emptive strike, and examine what they say when they do look at the details of the epistemicist argument.

The epistemicist aims to reduce the following supposition to absurdity:

(a)
It is not the case that either $\mathrm{P}$ is true or $\mathrm{P}$ is false.

Then the epistemicist invokes standard biconditionals that govern the notions of truth and falsity. Machina and Deutsch present them in somewhat nonstandard forms, but they will do for present purposes:

(b)
There exists some statement $\mathrm{Q}$ such that $\mathrm{P}$ is true if and only if $\mathrm{Q}$.
(c)
For that very same $\mathrm{Q}$, $\mathrm{P}$ is false if and only if not $\mathrm{Q}$.

From (a), (b) and (c) a contradiction follows (compare Williamson [1994]: 188-98).

‘To make clear what goes wrong’, Machina and Deutsch tell us to ‘rewrite’ (a) along these lines:

(a′)
It is not the case that either $\mathrm{P}$ is fully true or $\mathrm{P}$ is fully false.

Once that is done, (b) and (c) must also be rewritten to engage with (a′):

(b′)
There exists some statement $\mathrm{Q}$ such that $\mathrm{P}$ is fully true if and only if $\mathrm{Q}$.
(c′)
For that very same $\mathrm{Q}$, $\mathrm{P}$ is fully false if and only if not $\mathrm{Q}$.

They argue that (b′) and (c′) form an uncompelling pair, because the standard principles for truth and falsity do not apply to full truth and full falsity. They are doubtless right about that, unless the word ‘fully’ is redundant in ‘fully true’ and ‘fully false’. Without (b′) and (c′), the argument against (a′) fails.

Why should we rewrite (a) as (a′)? According to Machina and Deutsch, we should do so in order ‘to not beg the question against degree [of truth] theories’; they add ‘Williamson should not object; this merely clarifies what (a) is saying’. But Williamson does object. Machina and Deutsch do not claim that predications of ‘true’ and ‘false’ are meaningless unless implicitly or explicitly qualified by ‘fully’. On the contrary, they say of what is in effect a particular instance of (b) that it ‘seems perfectly unexceptionable’, and that ‘both the degree theory and the lack of truth value theory can agree that [it] is completely true’, and contrast it with the corresponding instance of (b′), to which they object; thus ‘true’ unqualified is not equivalent to ‘fully true’.6 Their point is not that ‘true’ and ‘false’ can only mean ‘fully true’ and ‘fully false’; (a) can be read at face value, as not equivalent to (a′). Rather, their point must be that what the epistemicist needs to argue against is (a′), not (a).

The epistemicist can happily agree that the style of argument in question works against (a) and not against (a′). It was only ever intended to work against (a). Machina and Deutsch offer no objection to the argument against (a). In their terminology of statements, the natural understanding of bivalence is as the principle that every statement is either true or false. Thus they offer no objection to the epistemicist argument against the denial of any particular instance of bivalence. Their point is just that the argument does not refute another claim, (a′), that some anti-epistemicists make. Exactly this point is made in the book ([1994]: 194-5) about the analogue of (a′) with ‘definitely’ in place of ‘fully’. As argued there, we need an explanation of the new operator (‘fully’ or ‘definitely’) before we can even judge whether (a′) or its analogue is inconsistent with epistemicism; if ‘fully’ meant something epistemic, the epistemicist might even accept (a′). Of course, many advocates of degrees of truth will try to explain ‘fully’ in non-epistemic terms; the book presents detailed arguments against anti-epistemic accounts of degrees of truth ([1994]: 113-38, 154-64), to which Machina and Deutsch offer no response. In brief, what they attack is not the epistemicist reductio ad absurdum of the denial of a particular instance of bivalence, construed as (a), but only an argument that I have never proposed.

Machina and Deutsch tack on a further objection to epistemicism, that ‘it yields an incredibly overgrown ontology’ because ‘We now not only have the class of even numbers or of odd numbers but also the class of small numbers and of numbers somewhat larger than 2!’. This argument confuses new ways of determining classes with new classes. We can form the description ‘class of natural numbers referred to on posters in London during the nineteenth century’, which is not synonymous with any class term in a purely mathematical language; nevertheless, using it clearly does not inflate our ontology, because we know that it designates a class that is also designated by some purely mathematical description, although for empirical reasons we do not know which one.7 Similarly, according to the epistemicist, since not all natural numbers are small and some are somewhat larger than 2, the least number principle assures us that some number $m$ (we cannot know which one) is the least non-small natural numbers and some number $n$ (again, we cannot know which one) is the least natural number somewhat larger than 2; since any natural number smaller than a small number is itself small and any natural number larger than a number somewhat larger than 2 is itself somewhat larger than 2, it follows that the class of small numbers and the class of numbers somewhat larger than 2 are simply $\{i : i < m\}$ and $\{i : i \ge n\}$ respectively. For epistemicists, $m$ and $n$ are not new natural numbers, just old ones under new descriptions. Likewise, $\{i : i < m\}$ and $\{i : i \ge n\}$ are not new classes, just old ones under new descriptions. Mathematicians were already committed to those old classes. Unless Machina and Deutsch are already assuming that epistemicism is false, they cannot complain of any ontological growth here.

Machina and Deutsch’s examples may be peculiarly ill-suited to their purpose. Perhaps some other vague terms (such as ‘religion’) do allow us to single out some other classes that we cannot in practice single out in precise terms. Even so, that would merely mitigate an expressive limitation of our precise vocabulary. The new classes would be of the same general kind as the old ones. Similarly, when we expand a standard language $\mathrm{L1}$ for first-order set theory by adding precise predicates not coextensive with any predicate of $\mathrm{L1}$ (such as a coded truth predicate for $\mathrm{L1}$), we can formulate instances of set existence principles (such as the axiom schema of separation) for new sets, but that merely mitigates the expressive limitations of $\mathrm{L1}$. Epistemicism does not crazily imply that a class with $c$ members can have more than $2^c$ subclasses. It does not inflate our ontology in any relevant sense. Interestingly, Machina and Deutsch make no attempt to explain how a non-epistemic theory of vagueness might handle vague class terms without ontologically inflationary consequences.

# ‘Inexact knowledge and margins for error’

In [1994], I used principles about margins for error to explain our ignorance in borderline cases. Such principles can take this general form:

(W)
If $x$ is similar enough to $y$, and $x$ is known to be $\mathrm{F}$, then $y$ is $\mathrm{F}$.

The required degree of similarity depends on the circumstances; it is the margin for error. The intuitive idea is that if $x$ is similar enough to $y$ and $y$ is not $\mathrm{F}$ then a judgement that $x$ is $\mathrm{F}$ would not be reliable enough to count as knowledgeable. (W) implies a kind of inexactness in knowledge. Not all such inexactness flows from vagueness; the epistemicist proposes that vagueness is a distinctively conceptual source of inexactness. (W) explains our failure to know that $x$ is $\mathrm{F}$ when $x$ is a borderline case, and therefore similar enough to some $y$ that is not $\mathrm{F}$.

Machina and Deutsch argue that Type W principles fail in some examples, in which they must be replaced by principles of this form:

(D)
If $x$ is similar enough to $y$, and $x$ is known to be $\mathrm{F}$, then $y$ is not known not to be $\mathrm{F}$.8

By itself, (D) is too weak to explain all our ignorance in borderline cases, for if $x$ is $\mathrm{F}$ and similar enough to $y$, which is not $\mathrm{F}$, although (D) implies that we do not both know that $x$ is $\mathrm{F}$ and know that $y$ is not $\mathrm{F}$, it does not imply that we neither know that $x$ is $\mathrm{F}$ nor know that $y$ is not $\mathrm{F}$.

Many details of Machina and Deutsch’s argument in relation to (W) and (D) are puzzling. They characterize Type W principles in terms of the notion of being “right on the borderline” [between $\mathrm{F}$ things and non-$\mathrm{F}$ ones] and insist that ‘The notion of being “right on the borderline” makes sense only when there is an ordering scheme for the relevant cases’. The formulations in the text show that neither (W) nor (D) relies on any notion of being “right on the borderline” that would require a [relevant linear?] ordering of cases (yet Machina and Deutsch are aware that similarity does not require a linear ordering of cases). Rather than trying to sort out the details of this part of their argument, and make them relevant to what epistemicists actually say, let us consider an example that appears to make trouble even for Type D principles.

Define a natural number $n$ to be effish if and only if $n$ is either even or much greater than 100. The second disjunct makes ‘effish’ vague; it has borderline cases, numbers that are neither clearly effish nor clearly not effish. Nevertheless, we know that 0 is effish (since it is even) and that 1 is not effish (since it is neither even nor greater than 100); between 0 and 1 ‘effish’ has a known sharp cut-off. If 0 and 1 are sufficiently similar, both (W) and (D) fail for ‘effish’ in place of ‘$\mathrm{F}$’. Intuitively, what such examples show is that a predicate can be vague in some regions of conceptual space and precise in others. The epistemicist would therefore expect the required margins for error to be wide in the former regions and to shrink to nothing or almost nothing in the latter regions. On this understanding, Machina and Deutsch’s examples are quite consistent with (W). Replacing (W) by (D) achieves nothing; what matters is to interpret ‘sufficiently similar’ with sufficient flexibility. (W) itself is only a schema: it gets its bite only in specific instantiations. The epistemicist always acknowledged that in complex cases the width of the margin would vary in complex ways ([1992]: 225-6; [1994]: 226-30). Nevertheless, (W) is not trivial, for it gives a general form for explaining our ignorance in borderline cases.

The complexities in such examples are not specific to epistemicism. Any theory of vagueness must account for them. The identifiable cut-off point for the vague term ‘effish’ is a prima facie counter-example to many claims made by non-epistemic theorists of vagueness. Like epistemicists, they must formulate their theories in sufficiently flexible ways.

# ‘A technical difficulty for Williamson’s margin for error principles’

A simple margin for error principle about a sorites series is this (for each natural number $n$):

(MFE)
If $\mathrm{C}(n\text{ grains make a heap})$ then $n-1$ grains make a heap.

‘$\mathrm{C}$’ here abbreviates ‘clearly’, where a borderline heap is one that is neither clearly a heap nor clearly not a heap. As Machina and Deutsch acknowledge, we could also read ‘$\mathrm{C}$’ as ‘it is known that’ without materially affecting the argument. (MFE) is not the margin for error principle most directly related to the vagueness of the word ‘heap’, for (MFE) is not even meta-linguistic ([1994]: 232). Nevertheless, (MFE) is derivable from a simple model of what is clear or unclear in the sorites series, a model that I am willing to endorse at least as a first approximation. Machina and Deutsch claim to reduce (MFE) to absurdity. What they do not emphasize is that the derivation on which they rely was in effect already presented and analysed in my original discussion ([1992]: 240-1).

Let us first get a comparatively minor point out of the way. Machina and Deutsch assume that for each natural number $m$ I accept $\mathrm{C}^m(\mathrm{MFE})$, the result of prefixing (MFE) by $m$ iterations of the ‘clearly’ operator $\mathrm{C}$. ‘It would be quite mysterious if he didn’t’, they say. In support, they argue that since I hold that one arrives at (MFE) by philosophical reflection, I must assign it a priori status, and therefore treat it as an informal theorem, consequently subject to the analogue for $\mathrm{C}$ of the necessitation rule in modal logic: if $\mathrm{A}$ is a theorem, then so too is $\mathrm{CA}$. I do regard (MFE) as broadly a priori, but fear that it lacks the informal logical status of a theorem. The argument for (MFE) is unfortunately neither as general nor quite as compelling as those for basic structural principles about clarity, such as that it implies truth ($\mathrm{CA} \to \mathrm{A}$; what is clearly the case is the case). Some uncertainty attends any substantive philosophical reflection. Thus, quite unmysteriously, I am not committed in the way that they propose to $\mathrm{C}^m(\mathrm{MFE})$. Indeed, I am inclined to think that $\mathrm{C}^m(\mathrm{MFE})$ is false for large values of ‘$m$’, although true for small values. Strictly speaking, this is already enough to block their attempted reductio as they present it. However, it does not yet speak to their underlying worry, which concerns the existence of large but finite bounds on the iteration of the ‘clearly’ operator $\mathrm{C}$. A large bound on $m$ in $\mathrm{C}^m(\mathrm{MFE})$ would presumably puzzle Machina and Deutsch as much as does the bound that they discuss. The argument for their bound depends on the non-existence of a bound on $m$ in $\mathrm{C}^m(\mathrm{MFE})$.9 To simplify the argument, I therefore grant them for present purposes $\mathrm{C}^m(\mathrm{MFE})$ for all natural numbers $m$ and $n$.

Given the assumptions now in play, and the epistemicist’s logic for the operator $\mathrm{C}$, Machina and Deutsch point out that if for some natural number $n$ $\mathrm{C}^m(n\text{ grains make a heap})$ holds for every natural number $m$, then it follows that 0 grains make a heap. Since 0 grains do not make a heap, for every natural number $n$ there is a bound $k$ such that $\mathrm{C}^m(n\text{ grains make a heap})$ fails for every natural number $m \ge k$. Machina and Deutsch find this result absurd. Why?

Our authors are particularly surprised that the upper bound established on $m$ in $\mathrm{C}^m(n\text{ grains make a heap})$ depends on $n$. The larger $n$ is, the larger the upper bound on $m$. They call this ‘a very bizarre result, one without any intuitive content that we can discern’. Rhetorically, they ask ‘Why should more $\mathrm{C}$’s […] be allowed to prefix “20,000 grains make a heap” than “10,000 grains make a heap”?’. But their question has an easy answer. It is even more thoroughly obvious that 20,000 grains make a heap than that 10,000 grains make a heap. Our use of ‘heap’ would have to be even more different in the relevant respects to falsify ‘20,000 grains make a heap’ than to falsify ‘10,000 grains make a heap’. On the margin for error model, each iteration of $\mathrm{C}$ slightly reduces the range of cases in which $\mathrm{C}^m(n\text{ grains make a heap})$ holds, by eroding away an outer margin of cases. In general, $\mathrm{CA}$ holds only in those cases in which $\mathrm{A}$ holds that are not too close for clarity to cases in which $\mathrm{A}$ fails. After enough iterations, nothing is left (on the simple model). Since ‘20,000 grains make a heap’ holds in a wider range of cases than does ‘10,000 grains make a heap’, more iterations are needed to reduce the former range than the latter to nothing. Thus the dependence of the bound on $m$ in $\mathrm{C}^m(n\text{ grains make a heap})$ has a clear intuitive content.

Machina and Deutsch offer another objection to the existence of a large bound on $m$. They claim that, ‘for ordinary cognitive agents’, iterations of $\mathrm{C}$ after some point become semantically redundant. According to them, ‘such iterations, beyond some intuitive number, do not have distinct meanings’. To put it in terms of knowledge, as they are willing to do, they hold that for some natural number $m$, the operators $\mathrm{K}^m$ and $\mathrm{K}^{m+1}$ are synonymous, where $\mathrm{K}$ abbreviates ‘it is known that’ and the exponent indicates the number of iterations. If we understand ‘it is known that’ as ‘everyone knows that’, with reference to the people in a given group, then Machina and Deutsch’s claim appears to be contradicted by standard examples in epistemic logic, independently of vagueness. Consider, for example, the coordinated attack problem:

Two divisions of an army, each commanded by a general, are camped on two hilltops overlooking a valley. In the valley awaits the enemy. It is clear that if both divisions attack the enemy simultaneously they win the battle, while if only one division attacks it will be defeated. As a result, neither general will attack unless he is absolutely sure that the other will attack with him. In particular, a general will not attack if he receives no messages. The commanding general of the first division wishes to coordinate a simultaneous attack (at some time the next day). The generals can communicate only by means of messengers. Normally it takes a messenger one hour to get from one encampment to the other. However, it is possible that he will get lost in the dark or, worse yet, be captured by the enemy. Fortunately, on this particular night, everything goes smoothly. How long will it take them to coordinate an attack?10

The problem is that the first general does not know that the second general has received the message to attack until he receives the second general’s acknowledgement, and the second general does not know that the first general knows that he (the second general) has received the original message until the second general receives the first general’s acknowledgement of the first acknowledgement, and the first general does not know that the second general knows that the first general knows that the second general has received the original message until the first general receives the second general’s acknowledgement of the first general’s first acknowledgement, and so on. Consequently, no member of the following series entails any later member: ‘The original message was received’, ‘Both generals know that the original message was received’, ‘Both generals know that both generals know that the original message was received’, ‘Both generals know that both generals know that both generals know that the original message was received’, … Moderately intelligent human cognitive agents can appreciate the failure of such entailments, even though they have computational difficulties in intuitively grasping all but the first few members of the series (and the generals might somehow muddle through). Obviously, the argument does not depend on vagueness. Thus the iteration of epistemic operators does not in general reach a point after which further iteration is semantically redundant. Do Machina and Deutsch suppose that ‘clearly’ and ‘it is known that’ (not now understood as ‘everyone knows that’) are somehow exceptional in this respect? If so, they do not explain why.

Machina and Deutsch’s claim of semantic redundancy is in any case inconsistent with highly plausible principles of compositional semantics, such as this:

(COMP)
For any declarative English sentences $\mathrm{S1}$ and $\mathrm{S2}$, ‘It is known that’$\text{\textasciicircum}\mathrm{S1}$ is synonymous with ‘It is known that’$\text{\textasciicircum}\mathrm{S2}$ only if $\mathrm{S1}$ is synonymous with $\mathrm{S2}$.11

Equivalently, any difference in meaning between $\mathrm{S1}$ and $\mathrm{S2}$ implies a difference in meaning between $\mathrm{KS1}$ and $\mathrm{KS2}$. For example, let $\mathrm{S}$ be the sentence ‘It is raining’. According to Machina and Deutsch, for some number $m$, $\mathrm{K}^m$ is synonymous with $\mathrm{K}^{m+1}$. Therefore, $\mathrm{K}^m\mathrm{S}$ is synonymous with $\mathrm{K}^{m+1}\mathrm{S}$. Therefore, by (COMP), $\mathrm{K}^{m-1}\mathrm{S}$ is synonymous with $\mathrm{K}^m\mathrm{S}$. By another $m-1$ applications of (COMP), they will eventually reach the conclusion that $\mathrm{S}$ is synonymous with $\mathrm{KS}$: ‘It is raining’ is synonymous with ‘It is known that it is raining’. But that conclusion is patently false; when there is ignorance about the weather, $\mathrm{S}$ can be true while $\mathrm{KS}$ is false. Thus Machina and Deutsch are presumably committed to denying (COMP). Yet (COMP) has an obvious semantic motivation, one that is quite independent of vagueness, indeed quite independent of the specifically epistemic meaning of ‘It is known that’. The meaning of ‘It is known that’$\text{\textasciicircum}\mathrm{S}$ is a structured whole composed from the meanings of its constituents, in the first instance the operator ‘It is known that’ and the sentence $\mathrm{S}$. Unless $\mathrm{S1}$ and $\mathrm{S2}$ are synonymous, ‘It is known that’$\text{\textasciicircum}\mathrm{S1}$ and ‘It is known that’$\text{\textasciicircum}\mathrm{S2}$ have different meanings in structurally the same argument place, and therefore themselves fail of synonymy. Hence a premise of Machina and Deutsch’s second argument against bounds on the iteration of the relevant epistemic operators violates compelling principles of compositional semantics. Consequently, both their arguments fail to show that the consequences extracted from (MFE) are absurd.12

# ‘Other models of inexact knowledge’

In a brief final section, Machina and Deutsch propose Heisenberg’s Uncertainty Principle, non-epistemically interpreted, as an alternative model of vagueness and inexact knowledge. How do they apply the quantum mechanical model to these new cases? Unfortunately, their sketchy remarks leave that unclear. Of course, if epistemicists went to great lengths to preserve bivalence and classical logic in the face of vagueness, only to give them up when confronted by quantum phenomena, then epistemicism would lose much of its attraction. But Machina and Deutsch do not begin to establish that quantum mechanics, non-epistemically interpreted, rules out the combination of bivalence and classical logic. They do not discuss non-classical quantum logic, whose heyday seems in any case to be long past. Nor do they suggest that quantum mechanics excludes semantic principles such as (a) and (b) from which bivalence follows by classical logic. After all, quantum mechanics is not even about semantics. Accepting classical logic and bivalence, one could still consistently hold that a particle is located in a region without being located at any point in that region; that is just a claim about the nature of the location relation, and it is independent of epistemicism about vagueness. Machina and Deutsch neither propose a deviant quantum logic nor exhibit any inconsistency between epistemicism about vagueness and a non-epistemic interpretation of quantum mechanics without a deviant quantum logic. Thus they have not yet identified an anti-epistemicist model of inexact knowledge.

Machina and Deutsch’s invocation of quantum mechanics fails to address a more urgent problem for them: how to reconcile the ‘intuitive appeal’ that they concede to margin for error principles with their rejection of their consequences, the bounds on the iteration of epistemic operators. They never explain how to treat margin for error principles and the arguments for bounds on the model of quantum mechanics.

No theory of vagueness, epistemic or non-epistemic, has made any significant contribution that I know of to the understanding of the quantum world. That is not a criticism of theories of vagueness, for they deal with something different. To assimilate the baffling phenomena of the quantum world to mere vagueness is to miss what is most distinctive and peculiar about them, and thereby radically to underestimate the problems that they pose. By comparison, vagueness is an easy problem which, for all that Machina and Deutsch show, epistemicists have already solved.
