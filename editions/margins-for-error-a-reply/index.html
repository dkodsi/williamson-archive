Margins for error: a reply

Timothy Williamson

The Philosophical Quarterly 50 (198) (2000): 76–81.

When you know at a glance, without counting, that a hall contains at least fifty people, you have inexact knowledge. Its inexactness depends on how you acquired it, not just on its content, for you could have come to know that the hall contains at least fifty people by counting and deduction. Inexactness is a kind of roughness inherent in perception, memory and other less than perfectly accurate means of acquiring and retaining knowledge. I have argued that inexact knowledge satisfies margin for error principles of the form: if one knows in a given case, one does not falsely believe in sufficiently close cases.1 For instance, normally if the hall contains just fifty people and you judge at a glance that it contains at least fifty people, you might very easily have made that judgement even if the hall had contained one person less, in which case your belief would have been false. By a margin for error principle, you know at a glance that the hall contains at least fifty people only if in fact it contains more than fifty. Without a margin for error, your judgement is too unreliably based to constitute knowledge. The width of the required margin, how close in which respects a case must be to count as close enough, depends on the circumstances; a general formula is too much to expect. That inexact knowledge satisfies margin for error principles is not built into the meaning of ‘inexact’. Rather the term is introduced informally, by examples; that all or most of them satisfy margin for error principles is a substantive theoretical claim. If it is true, most human knowledge satisfies margin for error principles.

Some inexactness is conceptual, not perceptual, in origin. Margin for error principles distinctive of conceptual inexactness help explain our ignorance of the boundaries of vague concepts, on the epistemicist view that they have sharp boundaries. Thus the principles strengthen the case for epistemicism, although they do not entail epistemicism and it does not entail them (see Vagueness pp. 230–7).

In a recent paper Peter Mott disputes both my epistemicist account of vagueness and my claim that inexact knowledge satisfies margin for error principles. His direct discussion there of vagueness does not pretend to be more than perfunctory; I do not comment on it here, having replied to similar objections elsewhere. Mott’s detailed arguments are intended to refute my claim that inexact perceptual knowledge satisfies margin for error principles. As he says, if that claim were refuted, a defence of epistemicism based on margin for error principles for inexact conceptual knowledge would be much less plausible. Mott’s two arguments make the same mistake: they ignore the inexactness of knowledge of inexact knowledge.2

Mott’s first argument (pp. 498–9) concerns iterated knowledge. He imagines Steven, Anna and John looking at a tree 15 feet high. Their margin for error is 5 feet:

1.

If Steven, Anna or John knows that the tree is at most n+5n + 5 feet high then the tree is at most n feet high.

(1) depends on their eyesight and visual judgement and the conditions of observation. Steven judges out loud that the tree is at most 25 feet high. Anna hears him and judges out loud that Steven knows that the tree is at most 25 feet high. John hears Anna and Steven and judges that Anna knows that Steven knows that the tree is at most 25 feet high. Mott argues that (1) implies the implausible restriction on mutual knowledge that John does not know that Anna knows that Steven knows that the tree is at most 25 feet high. For by (1), if Steven knows that the tree is at most 25 feet high then it is at most 20 feet high. Given that Anna knows this conditional and that her knowledge is closed under deduction, if Anna knows that Steven knows that the tree is at most 25 feet high then Anna knows that it is at most 20 feet high. By (1), if Anna knows that the tree is at most 20 feet high then the tree is at most 15 feet high. Thus if Anna knows that Steven knows that the tree is at most 25 feet high then it is at most 15 feet high. Given that John knows this conditional and that his knowledge is closed under deduction, if John knows that Anna knows that Steven knows that the tree is at most 25 feet then John knows that it is at most 15 feet high. By (1), if John knows that the tree is at most 15 feet high then it is at most 10 feet high. Thus if John knows that Anna knows that Steven knows that the tree is at most 25 feet high, then it is at most 10 feet high. But the tree is 15 feet high, so John does not know that Anna knows that Steven knows that the tree is at most 25 feet high.

Mott applies (1) to Anna’s knowledge when she has heard Steven and to John’s knowledge when he has heard Anna. Thus ‘know’ in (1) must include knowledge gained from the others’ judgements, which might reduce the margin. Mott does not distinguish the new margin from that for unaided perceptual knowledge.

He tacitly assumes that John, Anna and Steven have mutual knowledge of their margins for error. If Anna does not know (1), because for all she knows Steven is much better at judging heights than he really is, then Anna may know that Steven knows that the tree is at most 25 feet high without herself knowing that it is at most 20 feet high. Similarly, even if both Anna and John do know (1), John may not know that Anna knows (1); perhaps John does not know how well Anna is acquainted with Steven. So although we can argue from the premise that Anna knows (1) (given deductive closure) to the conclusion that if Anna knows that Steven knows that the tree is at most 25 feet high then it is at most 15 feet high, in those circumstances John may know (1) without knowing this conditional. Thus even if John knows that Anna knows that Steven knows that the tree is at most 25 feet high, John may not know that it is at most 15 feet high, contrary to Mott’s argument.

Mott conceals his assumption that (1) is mutual knowledge in his use of the rule from ⊢ϕ\vdash\mathrm{\phi} to infer ⊢KAϕ\vdash \mathrm{K}_A\mathrm{\phi}, with (1) and its consequences as ϕ\mathrm{\phi} (pp. 498, 504; ‘KA\mathrm{K}_A’ for ‘A knows that’). He does not explain what ‘⊢\vdash’ means here. (1) is a contingent claim about the limitations of Steven, Anna and John as judges of height by eye; it does not pretend to be a theorem of logic. Mott’s rule iterates: from ⊢ϕ\vdash\mathrm{\phi} one may infer ⊢KAKBKC…ϕ\vdash \mathrm{K}_A\mathrm{K}_B\mathrm{K}_C\ldots\mathrm{\phi} for subjects A, B, C, …. The rule is valid if ‘⊢\vdash’ means ‘it is mutual knowledge that’. But margin for error principles systematically undermine perfect mutual knowledge. To assume in arguing against them that (1) is mutual knowledge is illegitimate. Steven, Anna and John do not just have inexact knowledge of the height of the tree; they have inexact knowledge of their own and others’ inexact knowledge. This inexact knowledge should satisfy further margin for error principles of the form:

2.

If Anna knows that Steven’s margin for error is at least i feet then Steven’s margin for error is at least i+ji + j feet.

But (1) does not entail that (2) holds for j=5j = 5; the required value may be smaller.

Steven, Anna and John may spend so much time together that they know one another as judges of height as well as they know themselves. That reduces the intersubjective case to the intra-subjective case, which Mott also invokes. Analogous points apply. Even if I conduct experiments to test my reliability, my knowledge of my own margins for error will remain inexact and subject to margin for error principles. For the margin varies with the height of the tree (it is smaller for trees less than 10 feet high), which is just what I am trying to judge.3 Moreover, since margins are defined using the vague concept of knowledge, we have no means of measuring them exactly even given the height of the tree. (1) does not entail that Anna cannot know that she knows that she knows that the tree is at most 25 feet high.

Mott may have been misled by simplified models of inexact knowledge that idealize away inexactness in knowledge of margins for error. Such models already exhibit many structural traits distinctive of inexact knowledge: in particular, however many iterations of knowledge operators ϕ\mathrm{\phi} already contains, if ϕ\mathrm{\phi} is true in a range of cases, KAϕ\mathrm{K}_A\mathrm{\phi} is true in a narrower range. Margin for error principles do limit how many iterations of knowledge we can have. Mott allows (p. 498) that there may be finite bounds to iterations of knowledge. His aim is to show that the specific bounds imposed by margin for error principles are unrealistic. The argument succeeds only if its auxiliary assumptions are realistic, otherwise they may be responsible for unrealistic results. Mott’s assumption of mutual knowledge of the margin for error is unrealistic. The argument fails.

Mott’s second argument uses a model of inexact knowledge on which one makes a point estimate x of some quantity, believes that one’s estimates in such circumstances are in error by at most δ\mathrm{\delta}, and arrives at the interval judgement that the true value is between x−δx - \mathrm{\delta} and x+δx + \mathrm{\delta}. Without endorsing the original estimate, one uses the fact that one made it as evidence to be combined with one’s known propensities to error, treating one’s estimates as the outputs of just another inaccurate measuring instrument. Not all inexact knowledge is like that: I know by eye that the building opposite is less than 200 feet high without estimating its height. Nevertheless, inexact knowledge can take Mott’s form. Margin for error principles still hold.

I judge by eye that

3.

The tree is between 50 and 60 feet high.

On Mott’s model, (3) is the conclusion of a deductive inference from two premises:

4.

I estimated that the tree is 55 feet high

5.

If I estimated that the tree is 55 feet high then it is between 50 and 60 feet high.

In the circumstances, I know (3) if and only if I know both (4) and (5). My knowledge of (4) can be treated as exact for present purposes. If my knowledge of (5) is exact, then my knowledge of (3) is also exact, and the case is irrelevant; the issue is whether inexact knowledge satisfies margin for error principles. My knowledge of (5) is inexact, for I have only the haziest idea of my reliability as an estimator; there is almost no limit to how far out my estimates are on a bad day. Thus, on my view, (5) satisfies a margin for error principle. It says that I know (5) only if I am not too close to a situation in which (5) is false (I estimated that the tree is 55 feet high and it is not between 50 and 60 feet high), for example:

6.

If I know (5), and I estimated that the tree is 55 feet high, then it is between 51 and 59 feet high.

Even if I know (4), (5) and (6) I cannot thereby come to know that the tree is between 51 and 59 feet high, because that would require knowledge that I know (5), which I may lack. (6) yields a margin for error principle for knowledge of (3):

7.

If I know (3) then the tree is between 51 and 59 feet high.

For in the circumstances I know (3) only if I know (5) and (4) is true (since known).

To state his argument (p. 501), Mott defines a notion of ‘personal margin of error’:

By saying that your personal margin of error when judging the height of trees by eye in normal circumstances is 5 feet, three things are meant: (a) if you estimate that the tree is n feet high then it is in fact n±5n \pm 5 feet high; (b) if you estimate that the tree is n feet high then you know it is between n−5n - 5 and n+5n + 5 feet; and (c) there is no smaller number than 5 that satisfies the first two conditions.

Since knowledge entails truth, (a) is redundant given (b). Mott imagines a situation in which your personal margin of error is 5 feet and you estimate a 50-foot tree to be 55 feet high. By (b), you know that it is between 50 and 60 feet high. Thus (7) fails.

Mott has provided a counter-example to margin for error principles only if the imagined situation is possible. His stipulation includes (Kε\mathrm{K}\mathrm{\varepsilon}) for ε=5\mathrm{\varepsilon} = 5, by (b):

Kε\mathrm{K}\mathrm{\varepsilon}.

If you estimate that the tree is n feet high then you have inexact knowledge that it is n±5n \pm 5 feet high; the tree is 50 feet high; you estimate that the tree is 50+ε50 + \mathrm{\varepsilon} feet high.

(‘Inexact’, or else there is no counter-example to the claim that inexact knowledge satisfies margin for error principles.) Since knowledge entails truth, (Kε\mathrm{K}\mathrm{\varepsilon}) entails

Tε\mathrm{T}\mathrm{\varepsilon}.

If you estimate that the tree is n feet high then it is n±5n \pm 5 feet high; the tree is 50 feet high; you estimate that the tree is 50+ε50 + \mathrm{\varepsilon} feet high.

Clearly (Tε\mathrm{T}\mathrm{\varepsilon}) is impossible if ε>5\mathrm{\varepsilon} > 5. Since (Kε\mathrm{K}\mathrm{\varepsilon}) entails (Tε\mathrm{T}\mathrm{\varepsilon}), (Kε\mathrm{K}\mathrm{\varepsilon}) is also impossible if ε>5\mathrm{\varepsilon} > 5. Mott assumes that (Kε\mathrm{K}\mathrm{\varepsilon}) is possible if ε=5\mathrm{\varepsilon} = 5, which implies that (Tε\mathrm{T}\mathrm{\varepsilon}) is also possible for ε=5\mathrm{\varepsilon} = 5. Thus he in effect assumes that strengthening ‘It is n±5n \pm 5 feet high’ in (Tε\mathrm{T}\mathrm{\varepsilon}) to ‘You have inexact knowledge that it is n±5n \pm 5 feet high’ in (Kε\mathrm{K}\mathrm{\varepsilon}) makes no difference to the boundary between possibility and impossibility. That is merely to assume what had to be proved. On the view under attack, inexact knowledge is just what makes (Kε\mathrm{K}\mathrm{\varepsilon}) impossible when ε=5\mathrm{\varepsilon} = 5.

Mott offers no argument for the possibility of (Kε\mathrm{K}\mathrm{\varepsilon}) when ε=5\mathrm{\varepsilon} = 5. An argument from the possibility of (Tε\mathrm{T}\mathrm{\varepsilon}) when ε=5\mathrm{\varepsilon} = 5 would beg the question. He seems to suppose that the issue is whether you can estimate a 50-foot tree to be 55 feet high (p. 502). Of course you can; an error of any magnitude is possible. The question is whether you can make an error of 5 feet while also satisfying the first clause of (Kε) when ε = 5. Since Mott has provided no reason to think that you can, his argument fails.

He reformulates his argument showing some recognition of the difference between (Kε\mathrm{K}\mathrm{\varepsilon}) and (Tε\mathrm{T}\mathrm{\varepsilon}) (pp. 502–3). He defines your reliability R as the least number i such that for all n if you estimate by eye in normal circumstances that a tree is n feet high then it is n±in \pm i feet high; your confidence C is the least number i such that for all n if you estimate by eye in normal circumstances that a tree is n feet high then you know that it is n±in \pm i feet high. He identifies R with your margin for error in my sense. He also seems to assume that when you estimate by eye in normal circumstances that a tree is n feet high you never know that it is n±in \pm i feet high for i<Ci < \mathrm{C}, for he speaks of n±Cn \pm \mathrm{C} as ‘the interval within which you know’ (p. 503). These assumptions are unfounded. Suppose that one day in normal circumstances a blip occurred; you made an enormous error E when you estimated the height of a tree by eye. Thus E≤R\mathrm{E} \leq \mathrm{R} and E≤C\mathrm{E} \leq \mathrm{C}. Mott’s assumptions imply that for i<Ei < \mathrm{E} you never know of a tree n feet high that it is n±in \pm i feet high. Since E is enormous, you would know almost nothing about the height of trees by eye. My account of inexact knowledge has no such consequences. Your margin for error can be much smaller than R and C. Margins for error provide necessary conditions for knowing in various circumstances; Mott’s confidence C provides a sufficient condition for knowing. He defines C as the least number i satisfying a condition of the form (∀x)α(x,i)(\forall x)\mathrm{\alpha}(x,i); he cannot assume (∀x)¬α(x,i)(\forall x)\neg\mathrm{\alpha}(x,i) for i<Ci < \mathrm{C}. He makes a similar mistake when he writes (p. 502) ‘If he judges on the basis of his estimate that it is 50 ± 2 feet he will not know it, because his personal margin for error is 5 feet’.

Mott goes on to argue for the false conclusion that my view is committed to the inequality C≥2R\mathrm{C} \geq 2\mathrm{R}. I shall not discuss his argument in detail because it depends on the confusions just noted. Although in the relevant cases margin for error principles do imply the weaker inequality C>R\mathrm{C} > \mathrm{R}, its consequences are quite acceptable.

Mott’s discussion fits some cases of exact knowledge. An instrument measures a quantity Q, outputting an estimate O. The error |O−Q||\mathrm{O} - \mathrm{Q}| can be anything up to ε\mathrm{\varepsilon} and cannot be more than ε\mathrm{\varepsilon}; we know that by exact means, and nothing more about |O−Q||\mathrm{O} - \mathrm{Q}|. If Q is in fact O−ε\mathrm{O} - \mathrm{\varepsilon}, we know that Q is at least O−ε\mathrm{O} - \mathrm{\varepsilon} with no margin for error. Since we know that on the basis of our exact knowledge of the possible values of |O−Q||\mathrm{O} - \mathrm{Q}|, it is no counter-example to the claim that inexact knowledge requires margins for error. That what we know is that Q lies in a certain interval does not make our knowledge inexact, for inexactness depends on the method by which one acquires the knowledge: given exact knowledge that it is precisely midnight, one could derive exact knowledge that it is between 11.55 p.m. and 12.05 a.m. If our knowledge of |O−Q||\mathrm{O} - \mathrm{Q}| were like most empirical knowledge, we would not know that it was at most ε\mathrm{\varepsilon} when it was in fact exactly ε\mathrm{\varepsilon}. Just as in his first argument, Mott neglects the inexactness of our knowledge of our inexactness.

How much one knows about the accuracy of an estimate depends on how accurate that estimate actually is. Mott recognizes the simplest version of the point: if the error is greater than ε\mathrm{\varepsilon}, one does not know that it is not greater than ε\mathrm{\varepsilon}. He does not recognize that the dependence goes further than that. If the probability that the tree is n±5n \pm 5 feet high given that one estimates that it is n feet high is at least .95, he envisages that if one estimates that a tree n±5n \pm 5 feet high is n feet high, one knows that it is n±5n \pm 5 feet high (p. 501). But probabilistically reliable true belief is not sufficient for knowledge: one does not know at the time of purchase that one’s lottery ticket will not win. In general, whether one knows depends on whether one’s error was safely below the limit one believes it to be below. If one is too close to believing falsely that it was below the limit, one does not know that it was below the limit.

On Mott’s model, if a distant tree is 53 feet 8 inches high, in bad light someone with bad eyesight and bad judgement of heights may know at a casual glance that it is more than 53 feet 7 inches high by making a sufficiently inaccurate estimate. Inexact knowledge is not like that.
