On the Structure of Higher-Order Vagueness

Timothy Williamson

Mind 108 (429) (1999): 127–143.

Discussions of higher-order vagueness rarely define what it is for a term to have nth-order vagueness for n > 2. This paper provides a rigorous definition in a framework analogous to possible worlds semantics; it is neutral between epistemic and supervaluationist accounts of vagueness. The definition is shown to have various desirable properties. But under natural assumptions it is also shown that 2nd-order vagueness implies vagueness of all orders, and that a conjunction can have 2nd-order vagueness even if its conjuncts do not. Relations between the definition and other proposals are explored; reasons are given for preferring the present proposal.

I

People can be bald or not bald. That classification is vague. People can be definitely bald or definitely not bald, but they can also be neither definitely bald nor definitely not bald. That classification is vague too. People can be definitely definitely bald or definitely definitely not bald or definitely neither definitely bald nor definitely not bald, but they can also be neither definitely definitely bald nor definitely not definitely bald, or neither definitely definitely not bald nor definitely not definitely not bald, or neither definitely neither definitely bald nor definitely not bald nor definitely not neither definitely bald nor definitely not bald. That classification is vague too. People can be … . Vagueness in the first classification is first-order vagueness in “bald”, vagueness in the second classification is second-order vagueness in “bald”, and so on. Higher-order vagueness is nth-order vagueness for some n>1. We have as much reason to acknowledge higher-order vagueness as we have to acknowledge first-order vagueness; the difficulty of applying the higher-order classifications to a sorites series is of just the same kind as the difficulty of applying the first-order classification, which was what led us to recognize the problem of vagueness in the first place.

The terminology of “nth-order vagueness” presupposes that a hierarchy of orders of vagueness has been defined. The foregoing paragraph contains no such definition. Instead, it left a string of dots with “and so on”. We naturally assume that we could supply an explicit definition if the need arose. One is given below, although it is not the only possible way of filling in the dots. This explicitness enables us to confront some troubling questions about the structure of the hierarchy, to which intuition turns out to be a much less useful guide than it initially feels. The aim of this paper is to clarify these structural issues without addressing deep questions about the nature of vagueness.

By far the best developed framework for the analysis of higher-order vagueness is to be found in classical propositional modal logic with the necessity operator read as “definitely”. To preserve the formal analogy with “necessarily”, “Definitely P” must be understood as “It is definite that P”, not as “It is definite whether P”; the latter is equivalent to “Either it is definite that P or it is definite that not P”. This formalism is consistent with both an epistemic and a supervaluationist view of vagueness. Both can make sense of the equation “necessity = truth in all possible worlds” by reinterpreting it. As a first approximation, for the supervaluationist, definiteness is truth under all sharpenings of the language consistent with what speakers have already fixed about its semantics (“admissible sharpenings”); for the epistemicist, definiteness is truth under all sharp interpretations of the language indiscriminable from the right one. In both cases, we hold everything precise constant as we vary the interpretation. For example, if it is precise how many hairs Jack has on his head, we do not vary the number as we evaluate “Jack is definitely bald”. What we vary is, for example, how many hairs it takes not to be bald.

Formally, higher-order vagueness corresponds to contingency in which worlds are possible. The relativized equation “necessity in world w = truth in all worlds possible in w” can be reinterpreted too. For the supervaluationist, definiteness under a sharpening s is truth under all sharpenings admitted (deemed admissible) by s; a sharpening t may be admitted by a sharpening s1s_1 and not by a sharpening s2s_2 (Fine 1975, p. 146; Williamson 1994, pp. 156–9). For the epistemicist, definiteness under an interpretation i is truth under all interpretations indiscriminable from i; an interpretation j may be indiscriminable from an interpretation i1i_1 and not from an interpretation i2i_2 (Williamson 1994, pp. 230–44, 270–2). This paper is not concerned with the choice between epistemicism and supervaluationism; it uses their shared formal framework. As for other views of vagueness, particularly those that reject classical logic, it is for their advocates to attempt an analysis of higher-order vagueness with comparable rigour and detail.1 Such analyses, if forthcoming, would most likely face problems analogous to those discussed here.

The object language will consist of sentence letters pp, qq, rr, …, the usual truth-functors (including the truth and falsity constants ⊤\top and ⊥\bot) and the “definitely” operator Δ\mathrm{\Delta}. α\mathrm{\alpha}, β\mathrm{\beta}, γ\mathrm{\gamma}, … are metalinguistic variables over all sentences of the object-language. For simplicity, predicate and quantifier structure is suppressed. Although we may interpret the sentence letters as containing such structure, we will investigate vagueness in sentences without attempting to apportion it amongst their subsentential constituents. Δ\mathrm{\Delta} is used to represent vagueness within the object-language. Of course we can also represent vagueness metalinguistically, but in the case of higher-order vagueness that would involve a cumbersome hierarchy of metalanguages.2

On the formal semantics, PC, MP, K and RN hold automatically.

T embodies the platitude that it is semantically guaranteed that whatever is definitely so is so. T follows from the assumption that the accessibility relation R is reflexive. For the supervaluationist, every sharpening admits itself, otherwise it would be self-defeating; for the epistemicist, every interpretation is indiscriminable from itself. The principles PC, K, T, MP and RN define the analogue of the modal logic KT, also known as T, and as M.

B is the least obvious of the principles listed above. It follows from the assumption that the accessibility relation R is symmetric. For the epistemicist, R is something like indiscriminability, which is symmetric. For the supervaluationist, a sharpening s has R to a sharpening t just in case s admits t; if sharpenings deem admissible just those sharpenings that differ from them by at most some fixed amount, then R will again be symmetric. Intuitively, the crucial feature of a sorites series is that successive members differ at most slightly, and differing at most slightly seems to be a reflexive, symmetric, non-transitive relation. Although these considerations in favour of B are by no means decisive, we will provisionally include it as contributing to a particularly simple conception of the semantics, and call it into question again if it proves to have dubious consequences. The addition of B to KT gives the modal logic KTB, also known as B.

II

We can think of higher-order vagueness as follows. We have a first-order classification of states of affairs according to whether α\mathrm{\alpha} or ¬α\neg\mathrm{\alpha} holds. Vagueness in this first-order classification is first-order vagueness in α\mathrm{\alpha}. In discussing it, we make a second-order classification of states of affairs according to whether members of the first-order classification definitely hold, definitely fail to hold or are borderline cases. Vagueness in this second-order classification is second-order vagueness in α\mathrm{\alpha}. More generally, we have an (n+1)th-order classification according to whether members of the nth-order classification definitely hold, definitely fail to hold or are borderline cases. Vagueness in the nth-order classification is nth-order vagueness in α\mathrm{\alpha}.

Let us be more precise. Define a classification as a set of sentences closed under all truth-functors. More precisely, S5 permits vagueness and is the weakest extension of KT closed under MP and RN to forbid second-order vagueness.

III

We might expect that, just as a sentence can in principle have first-order vagueness without second-order vagueness (as S5 illustrates), so for any nn a sentence could in principle have nth-order vagueness without (n+1)(n+1)th-order vagueness. At this point, however, things become much less intuitive. For suppose that Δ\mathrm{\Delta} is semantically equivalent to a sequence Σ\mathrm{\Sigma} of jj occurrences of ¬\neg and kk occurrences of Δ\mathrm{\Delta} in some order, where j≥0j\geq0 and k≥2k\geq2, in the sense that ⊨Δβ↔Σβ\vDash\mathrm{\Delta}\mathrm{\beta}\leftrightarrow\mathrm{\Sigma}\mathrm{\beta} for every sentence β\mathrm{\beta}. Then we can show that α\mathrm{\alpha} has second-order vagueness only if α\mathrm{\alpha} has nth-order vagueness for every natural number nn. We need only show that if α\mathrm{\alpha} is (n+1)(n+1)th-order vague then α\mathrm{\alpha} is (n+2)(n+2)th-order vague (n≥1n\geq1). Suppose that α\mathrm{\alpha} is (n+2)(n+2)th-order precise, so α\mathrm{\alpha} is (n+k)(n+k)th-order precise, so ℂn+k{α}\mathbb{C}_{n+k}\{\mathrm{\alpha}\} is precise. Let β∈ℂn{α}\mathrm{\beta}\in \mathbb{C}_n\{\mathrm{\alpha}\}. Thus Σβ∈ℂn+k{α}\mathrm{\Sigma}\mathrm{\beta}\in \mathbb{C}_{n+k}\{\mathrm{\alpha}\}. Since ℂn+k{α}\mathbb{C}_{n+k}\{\mathrm{\alpha}\} is precise, Σβ\mathrm{\Sigma}\mathrm{\beta} is precise. Since Δβ\mathrm{\Delta}\mathrm{\beta} is semantically equivalent to Σβ\mathrm{\Sigma}\mathrm{\beta} and precision is closed under semantic equivalence, Δβ\mathrm{\Delta}\mathrm{\beta} is precise. Thus Δβ\mathrm{\Delta}\mathrm{\beta} is precise whenever β∈ℂn{α}\mathrm{\beta}\in \mathbb{C}_n\{\mathrm{\alpha}\}; hence ℂn+1{α}\mathbb{C}_{n+1}\{\mathrm{\alpha}\} consists of truth-functions of precise sentences and therefore is precise, so α\mathrm{\alpha} is (n+1)(n+1)th-order precise. Contrapositively, if α\mathrm{\alpha} is (n+1)(n+1)th-order vague, α\mathrm{\alpha} is (n+2)(n+2)th-order vague. Now in KTB (although not in KT) there is a sequence Σ\mathrm{\Sigma} of this kind, for ⊨Δβ↔Δ¬Δ¬Δβ\vDash\mathrm{\Delta}\mathrm{\beta}\leftrightarrow\mathrm{\Delta}\neg\mathrm{\Delta}\neg\mathrm{\Delta}\mathrm{\beta}; the left-to-right direction is just a special case of the B schema, and the other direction follows easily from the B schema by manipulation with K and RN. Other such sequences Σ\mathrm{\Sigma} exist in many other systems; for instance, ⊨Δβ↔ΔΔβ\vDash\mathrm{\Delta}\mathrm{\beta}\leftrightarrow\mathrm{\Delta}\mathrm{\Delta}\mathrm{\beta} in S4. For the theory of higher-order vagueness, KTB is the most interesting case. There, the hierarchy of orders of vagueness collapses: either α\mathrm{\alpha} is precise, or α\mathrm{\alpha} has first-order vagueness but not higher-order vagueness, or α\mathrm{\alpha} has vagueness of all orders.7

Could we avoid the result? We could meddle with the definition of nth-order vagueness. In particular, we can define a hierarchy of borderline cases and borderline cases of borderline cases. Say that α\mathrm{\alpha} has (n+1)(n+1)th-order borderline-vagueness if and only if Bnα\mathrm{B}^n\mathrm{\alpha} is vague (compare Fine 1975, pp. 140–1, 146). Since Bn−1α∈ℂn{α}\mathrm{B}^{n-1}\mathrm{\alpha}\in \mathbb{C}_n\{\mathrm{\alpha}\}, α\mathrm{\alpha} has nth-order borderline-vagueness only if α\mathrm{\alpha} has nth-order vagueness (n≥1n\geq1). The converse holds when nn is 1 or 2 but not otherwise, whether we work in KT or KTB (Appendix (iii) and (iv)). Formally, orders of borderline-vagueness do not collapse in the way that orders of vagueness collapsed above, even in KTB. We can show that for any nn, in some model of KTB pp has mth-order borderline-vagueness when and only when m≤nm\leq n (Appendix (ii)). For example, consider a model with W={0,1,2,3}\mathrm{W}=\{0,1,2,3\} and iRji\mathrm{R}j just in case the absolute difference |i−j|≤1|i-j|\leq1; note that R\mathrm{R} is reflexive and symmetric, so all theorems of KTB are validated. More generally, the semantic equivalence of Δα\mathrm{\Delta}\mathrm{\alpha} and Δ¬Δ¬Δα\mathrm{\Delta}\neg\mathrm{\Delta}\neg\mathrm{\Delta}\mathrm{\alpha} should mean that no vagueness is lost in the ascent from order 2 to higher orders. Borderline-vagueness discriminates too coarsely because it lumps together the definite cases and the definite non-cases.8

An alternative approach is to retain the definition of nth-order vagueness in terms of ℂn\mathbb{C}_n (as will be done for the rest of this article) but question the B schema. For both epistemicism and supervaluationism, the justification of B involved some handwaving. Although indiscriminability is indeed a symmetric relation, treating it as the accessibility relation oversimplifies what the epistemic analysis involves. Speakers are not presented with several interpretations of their language and the challenge of discriminating between them; rather, they make utterances which they might easily still have made even if the right interpretation of their language had been slightly different (Williamson 1994, pp. 231–4). This relation between interpretations is not obviously symmetric; if jj is an easy counterfactual alternative to ii, it does not automatically follow that ii is an easy counterfactual alternative to jj. Relations of epistemic accessibility are certainly not in general symmetric. Faced with misleading evidence, one may appear to oneself to know something which is in fact false; if xx is one’s actual situation and yy the situation one appears to oneself to be in, then yy is accessible from xx, because for all one knows in xx one is in yy, but xx is not accessible from yy, because in yy one knows something that is false in xx (compare Hintikka 1962, p. 106, and Humberstone 1988). Sceptical arguments may go wrong by falsely assuming epistemic symmetry (Williams 1978, pp. 309–13; Williamson 1995, pp. 536–7). Moreover, the “definitely” operator can be given a subtler epistemic semantics in terms of an infinite family of accessibility relations; on this semantics, B is invalidated and KT rather than KTB is the right system (Williamson 1994, pp. 272–5). The justification of B is no more fully compelling for the supervaluationist.9

If B is abandoned, pp can have vagueness of all orders below nn and precision thereafter for any n≥1n\geq1. One can see this by considering a model where W={1,…,n}\mathrm{W}=\{1,\ldots,n\} and iRji\mathrm{R}j just in case either |i−j|≤1|i-j|\leq1 or j=1j=1; R\mathrm{R} is nonsymmetric when n≥3n\geq3, for 3R13\mathrm{R}1 but not 1R31\mathrm{R}3. Since R\mathrm{R} is at least reflexive, all the theorems of KT are validated. For [[p]]={1,…,n−1}\lbrack\!\lbrack p\rbrack\!\rbrack=\{1,\ldots,n-1\} we can show that ℂk{p}\mathbb{C}_k\{p\} is precise if and only if n≤kn\leq k, so pp has vagueness of all orders below nn and precision thereafter, as advertised (Appendix (i)). Unfortunately, the models used in the proof lack any intuitive rationale; they were not what one had dimly in mind if one expected that vagueness could cut out at any finite order. As for the semantics based on an infinite family of accessibility relations, its justification has no obvious connection with higher-order vagueness, and it invalidates B only in somewhat recherché cases. Moreover, the failure of B would require something vague to hold without definitely not definitely not holding, a situation with little intuitive resonance. The standard considerations against the symmetry of epistemic accessibility are hard to relate to the special kind of epistemic accessibility relevant here; vagueness does not require appearances to be positively misleading, as when one appears to know something that is in fact false. If there is a connection, perhaps it is this: one may need time to become clear about something; even if Jack is in fact bald, it may never become clear to one that it will never become clear to one that Jack is not bald (compare Williamson 1998, pp. 104–5, 120–1). The epistemic possibility that further reflection might clarify matters would therefore be crucial, contrary to the usual assumption that in borderline cases further reflection is obviously futile. The issue is a delicate one. For the time being, the possibility in principle of second-order vagueness without nth-order vagueness for all nn must remain highly problematic. For both epistemicists and supervaluationists, a more securely fine-grained understanding of accessibility than we presently possess is required to resolve the matter satisfactorily. The question is left open here.

IV

There are other puzzles about the structure of higher-order vagueness. We noted in §II that if some sentences are precise, then any compound of them using only Δ\mathrm{\Delta} and truth-functors is also precise. We might expect the same principle to hold for higher-order precision too. Certainly if α\mathrm{\alpha} is (n+1)(n+1)th-order precise then Δα\mathrm{\Delta}\mathrm{\alpha} is (n+1)(n+1)th-order precise, for ℂ1{Δα}⊆ℂ2{α}\mathbb{C}_1\{\mathrm{\Delta}\mathrm{\alpha}\}\subseteq \mathbb{C}_2\{\mathrm{\alpha}\}, so by construction ℂn{Δα}⊆ℂn+1{α}\mathbb{C}_n\{\mathrm{\Delta}\mathrm{\alpha}\}\subseteq \mathbb{C}_{n+1}\{\mathrm{\alpha}\} for any n≥1n\geq1, so if α\mathrm{\alpha} is (n+1)(n+1)th-order precise then Δα\mathrm{\Delta}\mathrm{\alpha} is nth-order precise and therefore (n+1)(n+1)th-order precise. It is also easy to show that ¬α\neg\mathrm{\alpha} is nth-order vague if and only if α\mathrm{\alpha} is nth-order vague. But we cannot show that if α\mathrm{\alpha} and β\mathrm{\beta} are second-order precise then α&β\mathrm{\alpha}\mathbin{\&}\mathrm{\beta} is second-order precise, even in KTB. To see this, consider a model with W={0,1,2,3}\mathrm{W}=\{0,1,2,3\} and iRji\mathrm{R}j just in case |i−j|≤1|i-j|\leq1; since R\mathrm{R} is reflexive and symmetric, all theorems of KTB are validated. Let [[p]]={0,2}\lbrack\!\lbrack p\rbrack\!\rbrack=\{0,2\} and [[q]]={0,3}\lbrack\!\lbrack q\rbrack\!\rbrack=\{0,3\}. Thus [[Δp]]=[[Δ¬p]]=[[Δq]]=[[Δ¬q]]={}\lbrack\!\lbrack\mathrm{\Delta} p\rbrack\!\rbrack=\lbrack\!\lbrack\mathrm{\Delta}\neg p\rbrack\!\rbrack=\lbrack\!\lbrack\mathrm{\Delta} q\rbrack\!\rbrack=\lbrack\!\lbrack\mathrm{\Delta}\neg q\rbrack\!\rbrack=\{\}; hence pp and qq are first-order vague but not higher-order vague, since all cases are definitely borderline for them. But [[p&q]]={0}\lbrack\!\lbrack p\mathbin{\&} q\rbrack\!\rbrack=\{0\}, so [[¬Δ¬(p&q)]]={0,1}\lbrack\!\lbrack\neg\mathrm{\Delta}\neg(p\mathbin{\&} q)\rbrack\!\rbrack=\{0,1\}, so [[Δ¬Δ¬(p&q)]]={0}\lbrack\!\lbrack\mathrm{\Delta}\neg\mathrm{\Delta}\neg(p\mathbin{\&} q)\rbrack\!\rbrack=\{0\}; thus p&qp\mathbin{\&} q is nth-order vague for all nn.10 But how can a conjunction have higher-order vagueness if its conjuncts do not? Where does the higher-order vagueness come from? Surely conjunction itself is not vague.

Should we seek ways of eliminating such models? The simple linear structure seems very natural. Working with borderline-vagueness would make no difference, for p and q lack higher-order borderline-vagueness (because it implies higher-order vagueness) while p & q has higher-order borderline-vagueness (because it has second-order vagueness, and nth-order vagueness fails to imply nth-order borderline-vagueness only for n > 2).

Can we construct intuitive examples of higher-order vague conjunctions without higher-order vague conjuncts? The trick in the model is that all cases are borderline for p and q separately while p & q definitely fails in a vague range of cases. It is not easy to see how a simple sentence could acquire meaning without definite application anywhere. But we can assign p and q complex interpretations. Here is a fanciful example. Imagine that we use the integers to form an extremely vague popularity scale. Very popular individuals have highly positive popularity ratings; very unpopular individuals have highly negative popularity ratings. The scale is so vague that an individual’s rating never clearly falls within a range of less than ten. Now read p and q thus:

p:

John’s popularity rating is either even and highly positive or odd and not highly positive.

q:

John’s popularity rating is either even and highly negative or odd and not highly negative.

Whether John’s popularity rating is highly positive or not, it is quite indefinite whether it is odd or even; thus every case is borderline for pp. Similarly, every case is borderline for qq. As for p&qp\mathbin{\&} q, it implies that John’s popularity rating is even only if it is both highly positive and highly negative, which it cannot be. Thus p&qp\mathbin{\&} q is equivalent to “John’s popularity rating is odd and neither highly positive nor highly negative”. Although this cannot definitely hold, it can definitely fail to hold, when it is definite that John’s popularity rating is either highly positive or highly negative. Moreover, this clause is itself vague; if John is popular enough for it to be borderline definite that his popularity rating is highly positive, Δ¬(p&q)\mathrm{\Delta}\neg(p\mathbin{\&} q) will also be borderline. Thus p&qp\mathbin{\&} q has higher-order vagueness even though its conjuncts do not.

There is the possibility in principle of higher-order vagueness in a truth-functional compound without higher-order vagueness in its constituents. A more promising approach is to question that which makes the possibility look problematic: the structural analogy between higher-order vagueness and (first-order) vagueness. A conjunction is vague only if at least one of its conjuncts is vague, but it may be misleading to think of higher-order vagueness in α\mathrm{\alpha} as a species of vagueness in α\mathrm{\alpha}. Higher-order vagueness in α\mathrm{\alpha} is first-order vagueness in certain sentences containing α\mathrm{\alpha} (compare Fine 1975, p. 141). The sentences whose first-order vagueness constitutes higher-order vagueness in a conjunction are not in general sentences whose first-order vagueness constitutes higher-order vagueness in any of its conjuncts. For example, first-order vagueness in Δ¬(p&q)\mathrm{\Delta}\neg(p\mathbin{\&} q) constitutes second-order vagueness in p&qp\mathbin{\&} q, but not in pp or in qq. We tend to imagine higher-order vagueness as a subtle kind of infection or impurity, which cannot be present in a compound sentence without being present in one of its parts, and surely is not present in &\mathbin{\&} itself. The image is not even accurate for first-order vagueness, since a precise conjunction can have vague conjuncts. Even if we imagine the infection in one conjunct as somehow neutralizing the infection in the other, that does not prepare us for an infected conjunction without infected conjuncts. It is time to abandon the infection model of higher-order vagueness.
