Hyperintensionalist metaphysics as a case study in overfitting

Timothy Williamson

In Aaron Segal and Nicholas F. Stang (eds.), Systematic Metaphysics: Historical and Contemporary Perspectives (2026), 139–156. Oxford: Oxford University Press.

One form of systematicity in metaphysics comes from articulating metaphysical theories within a clear, general, and uniform logical framework. The framework constrains the theories into logical coherence, which is a kind of systematicity. Recent decades have seen a trend in analytic metaphysics towards less constraining logical frameworks. They reduce the logical pressure towards systematicity. This chapter is a critical examination of that trend. I have developed its arguments in more detail elsewhere (Williamson 2024).

According to a fashionable narrative, philosophy is in the midst of a revolution. In its cartoon version, the story goes like this. Early analytic philosophy—from Frege to Quine—relied on a coarse-grained extensional logical framework. Around 1960, through the work of Saul Kripke and others, it was overthrown in favour of a more fine-grained intensional logical framework, implemented by possible worlds semantics. That was the intensional revolution. Much more recently, through the work of Kit Fine and others, the intensional framework has in its turn been overthrown in favour of a still more fine-grained hyperintensional logical framework, sometimes implemented by impossible worlds semantics. That is the hyperintensional revolution. It is where we are now.

The epicentre of this earthquake has been in analytic metaphysics (Nolan 2014). Very roughly, for extensionalists, there is no metaphysical difference without an actual difference in extension; for intensionalists, there is no metaphysical difference without a possible difference in extension; for hyperintensionalists, there are metaphysical differences with no possible difference in extension. These distinctions need to be made much more precise, but for the purposes of this chapter we can understand them informally. In general, intensional frameworks tend to constrain less tightly than extensional frameworks, and hyperintensional frameworks tend to constrain less tightly than intensional frameworks.

Friends of hyperintensionalism typically argue for it by presenting apparently decisive counterexamples to intensionalism. I will explain some general causes for concern about such an example-driven methodology, and about the direction of hyperintensionalist theorizing. In brief, it has the profile of the pathology known in the model-building tradition as overfitting. The charge might be rebutted if the alleged counterexamples to intensionalism were genuinely decisive. However, I will explain the crucial pre-theoretic judgements on the examples as the products of fallible heuristics that are unreliable in just such cases. The ‘hyperintensional revolution’ is a failed coup.

∗∗∗

Much of analytic philosophy employs the method of conjectures and refutations. A universal generalization is conjectured to be necessary. The conjecture stands until refuted by a counterexample, a falsified instance, typically in the form of an imagined case. The mere possibility of the case suffices to falsify the conjectured necessity.

The logic is impeccable; the trouble is that not every apparent counterexample is what it seems. In terms of the philosophy of science, the method is just a form of naïve falsificationism, Popper without the qualifications, where a single counterexample suffices to refute a universal generalization. In the terminology of Joshua Alexander and Jonathan Weinberg (2014), naïve falsificationism is an error-fragile method. One or two small initial errors can do major long-term damage, because no adequate mechanism is in place for catching them. They can lead to the abandonment of a true theory, under the impression that it has been refuted by counterexamples.

In natural science, one defence against the risks of naïve falsificationism is the insistence that experiments must be repeatable. In philosophy, an analogue of that requirement is enforced simply by the mechanism that the community will not accept a counterexample merely on the proposer’s say so: others have to reach the same verdict non-deferentially. That filters out idiosyncratic aberrations. But not all errors are idiosyncratic. In natural science, an experiment may be poorly designed: for example, it may ignore an unknown systematically interfering factor, and so not show what it seems to show. In that case, repeating the experiment will produce the same result, and not alert anyone to the underlying error. Can there be such systematic errors in judging philosophical examples too?

‘Systematic’ here means more than ‘non-idiosyncratic’. A cultural bias, for example one originating in a specific religion, is not merely idiosyncratic, but that is not what I have in mind. Rather, my question is whether susceptibility to specific kinds of error could somehow be built into normal human cognitive capacities, and so be something like a human universal.

That fear may sound paranoid, a distant sceptical scenario. But there are specific reasons to take it seriously. For much human cognition relies on useful but fallible heuristics, cognitive shortcuts which work well mostly but not always.

At a first pass, a heuristic is a rule of thumb for solving problems of some type. The application of the rule may or may not be wholly unconscious. Even if it involves conscious activity, one may or may not know what rule one is applying, and one may or may not think of it as a heuristic. Even on reflection, it may not be obvious to one that one is relying on a heuristic, still less what heuristic it is.

The function of a heuristic is to provide a way of solving problems of the relevant type that is fast, easy, efficient, and reliable enough to be useful. The way must be feasible in real time. It can be reliable enough to be useful without being perfectly reliable. Reliability here is equated with the probability that the way provides a correct solution, where the standard of correctness is built into the specification of the problem. Heuristics can be described negatively, as ‘cheap and dirty’, in the tradition of Daniel Kahneman (Kahneman, Slovic, and Tversky 1982), or more positively, in the tradition of Gerd Gigerenzer, as ‘fast and frugal’ (Gigerenzer, Hertwig, and Pachur 2011).

Heuristics, as I understand the term, can be culturally acquired, or even idiosyncratic. For example, medical experts may develop heuristics for interpreting X-rays. But in many of the most important cases, heuristics may be innate products of evolution, adaptive and virtually universal to humans. For example, visual illusions are likely to result from such heuristics built into the human visual system, and probably the visual systems of many other species too. In particular, the visual system uses colour contrasts as guides to the boundaries of three-dimensional things in our environment, which is why it can be tricked by camouflage. Obviously, what heuristics are responsible for such illusions is a matter for psychological investigation.

Postulating a new heuristic does not come free. To be initially plausible, a candidate heuristic should be simple, quick, efficient, and useful. On reflection, it would be strange if we didn’t use something like that heuristic.

We may be tempted to refine a postulated heuristic by adding exception clauses, restrictions, and qualifications, to increase its reliability. We should resist that temptation, for the ‘refined’ heuristic is likely to be psychologically unrealistic, since it increases computational times and costs of application, for a comparatively small gain in reliability. Those increases will be drastic if they require conscious reflection, which is very slow by neural standards, and liable to create a bottleneck in processing. In the midst of action, a prompt, moderately reliable answer is usually better than a very reliable answer which comes too late, or than no answer at all.

Just as perceptual heuristics can cause perceptual illusions, so more general cognitive heuristics can cause philosophical paradoxes. Here are four potential examples.

Using the persistence heuristic, we ignore small differences: updating leaves most of our information as it was, by default. The heuristic is massively labour saving and reasonably reliable. Like most heuristics, persistence is defeasible: it can be inhibited, for instance by our awareness of a nearby boundary. It applies whether the information is encoded in precise or vague terms, but when they are vague we are unaware of a nearby boundary, so inhibition is not triggered. Consequently, we are tempted to accept tolerance principles to the effect that the application of a given vague term is insensitive to small differences. Such principles generate sorites paradoxes (Williamson 2024).

Using the suppositional heuristic, we assess a conditional as we would its consequent on the supposition of its antecedent. This enables us to articulate information built into our capacity for forming expectations offline, calibrated by experience. It is our primary way of assessing conditionals but it can lead to inconsistent results, and so is not perfectly reliable. Its implicit inconsistency explains our difficulty in determining the semantics of conditionals (Williamson 2020).

Using a disquotational heuristic, we defeasibly assess ‘S\mathrm{S} believes that P\mathrm{P}’ by whether S\mathrm{S} assents to ‘P\mathrm{P}’. As Kripke’s arguments in ‘A Puzzle about Belief’ in effect show, our reliance on the assent heuristic can easily lead us into inconsistency in Frege puzzles (Kripke 1979, Williamson 2024).

Disquotational heuristics of another kind for truth and falsity are central to our use of the terms ‘true’ and ‘false’, but in special cases they generate the Liar and other semantic paradoxes.

Reliance on unacknowledged heuristics may have distorted our philosophical understanding—in particular, of vagueness, conditionals, belief, and truth. What look like clear counterexamples to philosophical theories may be the misleading artefacts of fallible heuristics. Below, I will explain how ‘counterexamples’ to intensionalism may depend on just such artefacts.

The questionable data are not just used to argue against intensionalism in general; they may also be used to argue for a specific hyperintensional theory, by inference to the best explanation. But if the data to be explained are errors, explaining their truth is not a virtue.

Naturally, one cannot simply dismiss data in metaphysics on the generic grounds that they might be artefacts of a heuristic, any more than one can simply dismiss data in natural science on the generic grounds that they might be artefacts of measurement error. That is just a recipe for scepticism. Rather, one must provide specific positive evidence that such an error is a serious possibility in the case at issue. When the alleged error is supposed to result from the use of a heuristic, one should specify the heuristic and give evidence that humans use it and that it is error prone in such cases.

∗∗∗

Philosophers often respond to apparent counterexamples to a theoretical framework by moving to a more permissive framework. The new framework permits all the possibilities the old framework permitted, and more besides. Exactly that happens in the transition from intensionalism to hyperintensionalism. The new framework is welcomed as more ‘flexible’ than the old one. Models within the new framework have more degrees of freedom than models within the old framework: more moving parts are adjustable independently of each other, permitting more possibilities. Scientists standardly define a model’s degree of freedom as the number of independent parameters in the model.

Philosophers who welcome the increase in degrees of freedom often seem unaware that their attitude is diametrically opposed to that prevalent in the natural and social sciences. For scientists normally and quite explicitly aim to minimize degrees of freedom in their models.

A striking feature of this opposition is how much the two sides have in common. Both seek to do justice to the data, to whatever is known independently of the specific theories in dispute. Each side justifies its stance on degrees of freedom by the need to make sense of the data.

At first sight, the philosophers’ preference for flexible theoretical frameworks and models with many degrees of freedom is easier to sympathize with, more open minded, indeed more ‘empirical’. But it is also more naïve.

One reason why scientists want to minimize degrees of freedom is that a model’s degree of freedom is a crude measure of its complexity. Understanding why degrees of freedom are to be minimized is connected to understanding why simple models are to be preferred.

A reason often stated for minimizing degrees of freedom is that with enough degrees of freedom ‘one can model anything’: models come too cheap, because modelling is too easy. But what is wrong with modelling being so easy? The answer is related to the need to guard against erroneous ‘data’ (the scare quotes will henceforth be taken as read). If one can easily model whatever data come in, then one can smoothly accommodate any random new data point one happens to receive: no difficulty will occur to warn one of a potential error. Not even the most grossly erroneous data point will stick out as anomalous.

Consequently, tolerance of models with many degrees of freedom typically results in unstable theorizing. At each stage, the current model matches the data collected so far, but once more data are gathered, the match usually fails, and the old model is rejected in favour of a new one, with even more degrees of freedom. Scientists are familiar with the danger of falling into this pathological type of theorizing; they call it overfitting (Forster and Sober 1994; for textbook discussion, see Burnham and Anderson 2010). Through hard experience in curve fitting, scientists have learnt that profligacy with degrees of freedom has a way of ending badly.

If degrees of freedom are strictly limited, the fit with the data will almost certainly be rougher at each stage, but the theorizing is likely to be more stable and robust, converging on a comparatively simple model. Moreover, erroneous data are more likely to be identified as outliers, because the model does not accommodate them. Even when no specific error has been identified, an apparent counterexample to an accepted theory may be treated as a mere anomaly (in something like Thomas Kuhn’s sense), in the absence of a better theory that accommodates both the apparent counterexample and the other data. A theory that does so just by helping itself to more degrees of freedom is unlikely to be considered better.

Admittedly, the definition of degrees of freedom in terms of independent parameters is less rigorous than it may sound. It is right in spirit, and usually works in practice. But the definition is not fully general or precise. After all, as Cantor showed, one can code any ordered n-tuple of real numbers (representing n degrees of freedom) as a single real number (representing one degree of freedom). For present purposes, a more informal understanding of ‘degrees of freedom’ will do. It facilitates generalizations to philosophical methodology. In most cases, we cannot expect literally to count degrees of freedom.

An approach that always adjusts theory to data, helping itself to more degrees of freedom whenever needed, treats one observation as enough to refute a theory. From a scientific perspective which values limiting degrees of freedom, much counterexample-led theory change in philosophy moves in a retrograde direction. Nor do the data motivating such moves look reassuringly solid. The judgements at issue may well result from fallible heuristics on which we unreflectively rely. That is no reason to just dismiss the judgements, any more than it is a reason to just dismiss perceptual judgements, but it is a reason to avoid a philosophical strategy that is highly vulnerable to errors in those judgements.

Hyperintensionalists typically implement their view with formal models in a framework of hyperintensional semantics, negatively to prove that it involves no collapse into intensionalism or inconsistency, and positively to make it rigorous and systematic.

From the general perspective of scientific model building, one of the most salient features of hyperintensional semantics is the massive increase in degrees of freedom it brings. By introducing the possible world parameter, intensional semantics already involves a significant increase in degrees of freedom over extensional semantics, but hyperintensional semantics multiplies that increase alarmingly. Perhaps an even more alarming feature of hyperintensional semantics is that its practitioners seem unaware of this multiplication of degrees of freedom as a cost of their approach (see also Williamson 2021).

Although no form of hyperintensional semantics has so far become canonical, two approaches are especially popular: impossible worlds semantics and truthmaker semantics. I will briefly explain each approach in turn, and how it increases the degrees of freedom of semantic models.

Impossible worlds semantics generalizes intensional semantics by keeping the world parameter but lifting the constraints on truth at a world (Rantala 1982, Berto and Jago 2019). Thus for any set Γ\Gamma whatever of sentences of the object language, there is a world w in a model M\mathrm{M} where the set of sentences true at w in M\mathrm{M} is exactly Γ\Gamma. Such an approach may seem attractive for treating conditionals with impossible antecedents, like ‘If the Russell set both is and is not a member of itself, dialetheism is refuted’, since the antecedent will be true at some worlds in some models, so the conditionals need not be treated as vacuous.

The increase in degrees of freedom is blatant for impossible worlds semantics. It must not be confused with any metaphysical qualms about the ‘reality’ of impossible worlds, which may depend on illicitly projecting David Lewis’s modal realism onto the semantic framework. Instead, what multiplies the degrees of freedom is a more specific feature of the semantic framework: its abandonment of compositionality. In any standard semantic model, each non-logical atomic sentence is treated in effect as a variable, to which the model must assign a semantic value; in that sense, each non-logical atomic sentence automatically adds its own degree of freedom. In an extensional model, the semantic value is a truth value; in an intensional model, the semantic value is a function from possible worlds to truth values. But in both extensional and intensional models, the semantic values of complex expressions are determined from the semantic values of their constituents; thus, complex expressions introduce no further degrees of freedom of their own.

To take the simple case of negation, ¬A\neg \mathrm{A} is true in an extensional model if A\mathrm{A} is false in the model, otherwise ¬A\neg \mathrm{A} is false in the model; ¬A\neg \mathrm{A} is true at a world w in an intensional model if A\mathrm{A} is false at w in the model, otherwise ¬A\neg \mathrm{A} is false at w in the model. The impossible worlds approach gives up these constraints, in order to allow worlds at which both A\mathrm{A} and ¬A\neg \mathrm{A} are true, and other worlds at which neither A\mathrm{A} nor ¬A\neg \mathrm{A} is true. It has no semantic clause to determine the semantic value of ¬A\neg \mathrm{A} in terms of the semantic value of A\mathrm{A}. Thus, even if two sentences A\mathrm{A} and B\mathrm{B} are true at exactly the same worlds in a model, ¬A\neg \mathrm{A} and ¬B\neg \mathrm{B} may be true at different worlds in the model. Exactly analogous phenomena occur for other operators, such as conjunction and disjunction.

Normally, a model will include some worlds marked as ‘possible’, where the usual compositional rules apply, and one of those worlds will be marked as ‘actual’, where truth in the model is identified with truth at the actual world in the model. Nevertheless, some of the compositional rules will determine the evaluation of complex sentences at the actual world in terms of what happens at impossible worlds—else including the impossible worlds in the model would be pointless. With respect to the impossible worlds, the semantic value of each complex expression must be put in by hand. Thus, compositionality fails over the model as a whole. Each complex expression represents its own degree of freedom. It is like a strange kind of mathematics in which the value of ‘x+yx + y’ is not determined by the values of ‘x’ and ‘y’. Compositionality is often held to be the central constraint of semantics as a discipline, but impossible worlds semantics gives it up without comment. I have encountered philosophers who advocated the use of impossible worlds semantics without even being aware that it abandons semantic compositionality until I pointed it out to them.

Admittedly, some theorists of impossible worlds do tweak their semantics to restore compositionality (Berto and Jago 2019, discussed in Williamson 2024). But they do so by generic tricks that in effect trivialize compositionality, by redefining what counts as ‘meanings’. Semanticists have long known that such tricks can be played (Szabó 2000). For compositionality to be fruitful in semantics, one must respect the spirit as well as the letter of the constraint; lip service is not enough. Instead, compositionality should be maintained without gerrymandering.

From a model-building perspective, the distinctive value of model building is undermined by the extent to which, with respect to impossible worlds, the theorist must put every aspect of the model in by hand. The aim is to learn something from a model, by making some simple assumptions and then watching where they lead mathematically. But if one must continually guide the model by hand, it will tell one only what one wants to hear.

Another popular hyperintensional approach is truthmaker semantics, which introduces a parameter for a (partial) state of the world (Fine 2017). A model maps each atomic sentence to the set of states that verify it—its truthmakers. To handle negation, the model may also map each atomic sentence to the set of states that falsify it—its falsemakers. States are normally partial; a given state may neither verify nor falsify a given sentence. Since states are closed under fusion, some states may also be inconsistent as well as incomplete: a given state may both verify and falsify a given sentence. Verification and falsification for complex sentences are defined recursively in terms of verification and falsification for simpler sentences and the mereological structure of states. For example, on a simple version of the semantics, a state verifies a disjunction if and only if it verifies at least one disjunct, and falsifies a conjunction if and only if it falsifies at least one conjunct. Thus the truthmakers of ‘Lauren is in London or Lauren is not in London’ are the truthmakers and the falsemakers of ‘Lauren is London’, while the truthmakers of ‘Paul is in Paris or Paul is not in Paris’ are the truthmakers and the falsemakers of ‘Paul is in Paris’. Consequently, the two disjunctions have different sets of truthmakers, and so differ semantically, even though they are necessarily equivalent—for truthmaker semantics is not intended to undermine classical logic, though validating classical logic in the truthmaker framework is by no means automatic. The truthmaking semantics facilitates the definition of hyperintensional operators. For example, it makes sense of a connective ≡\equiv for semantic equivalence, where A≡B\mathrm{A} \equiv \mathrm{B} is true if A\mathrm{A} and B\mathrm{B} have the same truthmakers and the same falsemakers, and false otherwise; thus any truthmaker or falsemaker for one but not the other of A\mathrm{A} and B\mathrm{B} might be a falsemaker for A≡B\mathrm{A} \equiv \mathrm{B} (what its truthmakers are we may leave truthmaker theorists to decide). Consequently, if A\mathrm{A} is ‘Lauren is in London or Lauren is not in London’ while B\mathrm{B} is ‘Paul is in Paris or Paul is not in Paris’, A≡B\mathrm{A} \equiv \mathrm{B} is false but A≡A\mathrm{A} \equiv \mathrm{A} true, despite the intensional equivalence of A\mathrm{A} and B\mathrm{B}; thus ≡\equiv is a hyperintensional operator.

By contrast with impossible worlds semantics, truthmaker semantics respects compositionality, and is much less profligate with degrees of freedom. Nevertheless, it does increase degrees of freedom quite severely. Most obviously, it introduces a state parameter into semantic evaluation. This parameter should not simply be treated as a generalization of the world parameter of intensional semantics, with states as partial worlds, for that may return us to impossible world semantics: the fusion of a truthmaker for a sentence A\mathrm{A} and a falsemaker for A\mathrm{A} makes the contradiction A∧¬A\mathrm{A} \wedge \neg \mathrm{A} true, in a standard truthmaker framework. Even with the state parameter, a separate world parameter may be needed too, to track the modal dimension. Furthermore, if each atomic sentence must be mapped to an independent set of falsemakers as well as to a set of truthmakers, that doubles the degrees of freedom per atomic sentence. Thus, truthmaker semantics carries a significant cost in degrees of freedom too.

In response, hyperintensionalists will argue that hyperintensionality is a well-established phenomenon, so we must be prepared to pay whatever an adequate semantic treatment of it costs. This brings us to the key question: how well established is hyperintensionality? It must be very well established indeed for hyperintensionalism not to constitute a case of overfitting.

As already noted, those who argue for hyperintensionality usually do so by presenting examples. At first sight, their arguments are often quite convincing.

In a cheap sense, however, quotational contexts are already hyperintensional. For example, (1) is true and (2) false:

(1)
‘Paul is in Paris or Paul is not in Paris’ begins with a ‘P’.
(2)
‘Lauren is in London or Lauren is not in London’ begins with a ‘P’.

However, such cases are usually felt to be beside the point, and hyperintensional semantics is not designed to deal with them, because quotation is in some sense not a semantic operator at all, but a syntactic one. Although greater clarity on the nature of quotation is desirable, we need not linger on it here.

More interesting are attitude ascriptions. For example, if Patricia has never heard of Lauren but is wondering whether Paul is in Paris, we might judge (3) true and (4) false:

(3)
Patricia believes that either Paul is in Paris or Paul is not in Paris.
(4)
Patricia believes that either Lauren is in London or Lauren is not in London.

On such grounds, belief ascriptions are often claimed to be hyperintensional. A reason for doubting such claims is that they may well rely on something like the disquotational heuristic for belief ascription, whose fallibility Kripke in effect demonstrated. For example, presented with the sentence ‘Either Paul is in Paris or Paul is not in Paris’, Patricia assents; presented with the sentence ‘Either Lauren is in London or Lauren is not in London’, she asks ‘Who’s Lauren?’. More generally, many of our assessments of attitude ascriptions may rely on linguistically mediated heuristics which give inconsistent results in Frege puzzles. If intensionalists about attitude ascriptions are correct, the sentences ‘Either Paul is in Paris or Paul is not in Paris’ and ‘Either Lauren is in London or Lauren is not in London’ are in effect two linguistic guises for the same proposition, so the pair (3)/(4) itself constitutes a Frege puzzle. Thus, using such pairs to argue against intensionalism about attitude ascriptions is liable to be dialectically ineffective; the apparent hyperintensionality may turn out to be an artefact of heuristics.

More positively, the natural hypothesis that we use willingness to say ‘P\mathrm{P}’ as a heuristic for belief that P\mathrm{P} explains many features of our practice of belief ascription, both in ordinary cases and in Frege puzzles, although of course we can also ascribe belief on the basis of non-linguistic behaviour. Language users who did not use speech behaviour for mindreading purposes would be strange indeed.

In any case, many contemporary hyperintensionalists deny that hyperintensionality is confined to broadly representational matters such as attitude ascriptions. They want to do hyperintensional metaphysics. Just as Kripke and others in the intensional revolution argued that modal matters such as physical and metaphysical contingency and non-contingency are objective intensional features of the world, independently of how it is represented, so contemporary hyperintensionalists argue that essence, grounding, constitution, and other such matters are objective hyperintensional features of the world, independently of how it is represented. That is why there is so much current excitement around hyperintensionality. I will therefore focus on alleged examples of non-representational hyperintensionality.

One class of cases comprises counterpossibles, counterfactual conditionals with impossible antecedents. Counterfactual conditionals are normally read non-epistemically, so if counterpossibles are hyperintensional, they are cases of non-representational hyperintensionality. I will not discuss counterpossibles in detail here, because I have done so elsewhere (Williamson 2020). I simply note that if we apply the suppositional heuristic for conditionals (see above) to counterpossibles we will tend to treat them as hyperintensional, but the heuristic’s inconsistency also manifests itself with some counterpossibles. Thus, they are a poor basis on which to argue for hyperintensionalism.

I will focus on more metaphysically challenging alleged cases of hyperintensionality.

The first case goes back well before the supposed beginning of the hyperintensional revolution: Elliott Sober argued that logically equivalent predicates may pick out different properties (Sober 1982). His example is ‘triangular’ (being a regular figure with three angles) and ‘trilateral’ (being a regular figure with three sides). Necessarily, whatever is triangular is trilateral, and vice versa. Thus, the predicates ‘is triangular’ and ‘is trilateral’ are intensionally equivalent. We can nominalize them to refer to the properties they pick out, as ‘triangularity’ and ‘trilaterality’ respectively, but the results are not intensionally equivalent, because they refer to distinct properties, according to Sober. On that way of thinking, nominalization itself counts as an intensional operator.

Sober’s argument for the distinctness of triangularity and trilaterality is that the properties play different causal-explanatory roles. Imagine a machine which sorts various shapes and selects triangles by counting angles. That is physically a different procedure from selecting the same shapes by counting sides. Consider a selected shape and two statements about it:

(5)
This shape was selected because it is triangular.
(6)
This shape was selected because it is trilateral.

In the envisaged circumstances, it feels natural to assess (5) as true and (6) as false. On those assessments, the sentence operator ‘because’ in (5) and (6) is also hyperintensional, because their first constituent sentence is the same and their respective second constituent sentences are intensionally equivalent, yet (5) and (6) differ in truth value.

The hyperintensionality in the example is not supposed to come from anything implicitly meta-representational in nominalization or the word ‘because’, just from a difference in causal powers between two worldly properties. One might try to explain it by postulating that somehow triangularity has as a constituent property of being an angle where trilaterality instead has as a constituent property of being a side. Analogously, the proposition that this shape is triangular would differ from the proposition that this shape is trilateral in that where the former has triangularity as a constituent, the latter has trilaterality.

‘Because’ can be used to argue for many other cases of hyperintensionality (Schnieder 2011). To illustrate this, we can adapt a classic example from Kit Fine’s seminal paper ‘Essence and Modality’ (1994), often seen as the opening salvo in the hyperintensional revolution. It exploits the undisputed necessary equivalence between being a member of the singleton set {x}\{x\} and being x. Consider (7) and (8), uttered with reference to Socrates:

(7)
He is a member of {Socrates} because he is Socrates.
(8)
He is Socrates because he is a member of {Socrates}.

It is natural to judge (7) as true and (8) as false. Indeed, (7) sounds banal, while (8) sounds perverse—being a member of a set seems quite extraneous to being Socrates. Since the two constituent sentences ‘he is Socrates’ and ‘he is a member of {Socrates}’ are necessarily equivalent, if (7) is true and (8) false they together make another case of hyperintensionality. The contrast between (7) and (8) seems closely related to Fine’s argument that the property of being Socrates is essential to Socrates while the property of being a member of {Socrates} is not, even though the two properties are co-intensional. In the Aristotelian tradition to which Fine is sympathetic, essences play a key explanatory role.

A natural comment on (7) and (8) is that (7) displays the right order of explanation, while (8) displays the wrong order. The contrast between them hints at some sort of metaphysical priority ordering sensitive to hyperintensional differences in explanatoriness. Indeed, our judgements of (7) and (8) go hand in hand with our judgements of the corresponding explanations:

(7E)
He is Socrates. So: he is a member of {Socrates}.
(8E)
He is a member of {Socrates}. So: he is Socrates.

Here ‘so’ (like ‘therefore’) just flags the explanatory connection between the two sentences, without contributing to their on-topic content. In response to the question ‘Why is he a member of {Socrates}?’, (7E) is prima facie a good explanation. In response to the question ‘Why is he Socrates?’, (8E) is prima facie a bad explanation, because it brings in something irrelevant, a set.

Similarly, our judgements of (5) and (6) go hand in hand with our judgements of the corresponding explanations:

(5E)
It is triangular. So: it was selected.
(6E)
It is trilateral. So: it was selected.

In response to the question ‘Why was this selected?’, (5E) is prima facie a good explanation, while (6E) is prima facie a bad one, because it brings in something irrelevant, sides.

One benefit of the link between the ‘because’ statements and the corresponding explanations is that it helps explain why the former are factive: that is, ‘B\mathrm{B} because A\mathrm{A}’ is true only if both ‘A\mathrm{A}’ and ‘B\mathrm{B}’ are true. For if either ‘A\mathrm{A}’ or ‘B\mathrm{B}’ is untrue, the person who utters the explanatory discourse ‘A\mathrm{A}. So: B\mathrm{B}’ thereby makes an untrue statement. Thus, if we judge the statement ‘B\mathrm{B} because A\mathrm{A}’ by the explanation ‘A\mathrm{A}. So: B\mathrm{B}’, no wonder we judge the former untrue.

The correlation between assessments of ‘because’ statements and assessments of the corresponding explanations goes further. Consider (9) and (10) (where ‘furze’ and ‘gorse’ are two terms for the same natural kind):

(9)
Furze is as prickly as gorse because furze is gorse.
(10)
Furze is as prickly as gorse because furze is furze.

While (9) sounds fine, (10) sounds idiotic. The instinct is to judge (9) true, (10) false. Our reactions to (9) and (10) go hand in hand with our assessments of the corresponding explanations:

(9E)
Furze is gorse. So: furze is as prickly as gorse.
(10E)
Furze is furze. So: furze is as prickly as gorse.

In response to the question ‘Why is furze as prickly as gorse?’, (9E) is prima facie a good explanation—it provides exactly the needed information. In response to the same question, (10E) is prima facie a bad explanation, providing no relevant information; it sounds like a mere non sequitur. Yet (9E) and (10E) differ only verbally, in using synonyms for the same natural kind. The value of an explanation does not depend solely on the semantic contents of the explanandum and the explanans; unsurprisingly, it also depends on how those contents are presented. The value varies with how clear, perspicuous, and easy to follow the explanation is, which may in turn depend on comparatively superficial stylistic features, such as whether it always uses the same word for the same thing, and whether it orders the material in ways helpful to cognition, perhaps by recounting events in the order in which they happened, or by grouping information so as to make underlying patterns salient. A graph with lines in sharply contrasting colours is an explanatory improvement over one with lines in just discernibly different shades of grey.

Pre-theoretically, since we are tempted to judge (9) true and (10) false, just as we prefer (9E) to (10E), we are inclined to treat such presentational differences as relevant to the truth value of ‘because’ statements. One hypothesis is that we are right to do so, which makes ‘because’ implicitly metalinguistic in meaning. In that case, it cannot do the work in metaphysics which hyperintensionalists have wanted to use it for. They have been systematically confusing metaphysical and metalinguistic questions.

An alternative, perhaps more plausible hypothesis is that hyperintensionalists are correct in treating ‘because’ and similar terms as non-metalinguistic. In that case, (9) and (10) have the same truth value, because they differ only by the substitution of synonymous terms. But then the temptation to judge (9) true and (10) false shows that we are tempted to misjudge one of them. Cognitively, we are subject to interference from irrelevant factors.

A natural hypothesis explains these psychological effects: in assessing ‘because’ we typically rely on the heuristic: assess ‘A\mathrm{A} because B\mathrm{B}’ as true or false according to whether you assess ‘B\mathrm{B}’ as a satisfactory or unsatisfactory answer to the question ‘Why A\mathrm{A}?’. The background picture is that we have a metacognitive capacity to imagine how satisfying it would be to receive ‘B\mathrm{B}’ as an answer to the question ‘Why A\mathrm{A}?’, which we can leverage to assess the ‘because’ statement. The temptation to misjudge (9) or (10) in line with our assessment of (9E) and (10E) then shows that the heuristic is not fully reliable. Evidence for the hypothesis that we use such a heuristic is that it explains our propensity to error in ascribing different truth values to (9) and (10).

That hypothesis explains our assessments of the ‘because’ statements (5)–(8) too, since they align with our assessments of the corresponding explanations (5E)–(8E). But that undermines (5)/(6) and (7)/(8) as counterexamples to intensionalism. Not only do they rely on a less than fully reliable heuristic, they do so in cases where an intensionalist would expect the heuristic to be erroneous.

Consider (5)/(6). If triangularity and trilaterality are indeed the same property, as the intensionalist holds, it would still make for a better explanation to use ‘triangular’ rather than ‘trilateral’, since the association with angles is more explanatorily relevant than the association with sides. The allusion to angles points towards the relevant causally active mechanism of counting angles, whereas an allusion to sides would point distractingly towards a causally non-active mechanism of counting sides, even though the pointing is psychological rather than semantic. In that respect, (5E) is explanatorily helpful, (6E) explanatorily misleading (though not false, for the intensionalist). Given that we use the heuristic, it is no surprise that we judge (5) true, (6) false. Thus (5)/(6) does not refute intensionalism; the intensionalist can readily understand, and even predict, the anti-intensionalist appearances in terms of an intensional reality.

Similarly, consider (7)/(8). In terms of syntactic and semantic structure, and cognitive availability, (7E) moves in the familiar and helpful explanatory direction, from the simpler and easier to the more complex and more difficult. By contrast, (8E) moves in exactly the opposite direction, which is unfamiliar and unhelpful, from the more complex and more difficult to the simpler and easier. Given that we use the heuristic, it is no surprise that we judge (7) true, (8) false. Thus (7)/(8) does not refute intensionalism either; the intensionalist can again readily understand, and even predict, the anti-intensionalist appearances in terms of an intensional reality.

Likewise, the ability of some positive hyperintensional theory to explain why (5) and (6) or (7) and (8) differ in truth value does not support the theory by inference to the best explanation once we have evidence that the alleged difference in truth value is very likely a heuristic-generated illusion.

To check the robustness of these ideas, we can consider another type of example, of apparent hyperintensionality:

(11)
As a result of the illness, he was an invalid.
(12)
As a result of the illness, he was a self-identical invalid.

Assume that (11) is true. One might naturally object to (12) that his self-identity wasn’t a result of the illness; he was already self-identical before he became ill, and would have remained self-identical even had he not become ill. On such grounds, one might judge (12) false. But, necessarily, all and only invalids are self-identical invalids. Thus, if (11) is true and (12) false, (11)/(12) involves some kind of hyperintensionality.

Here is another example, a well-known truth about English history:

(13)
As a result of Mary Tudor’s death, Elizabeth Tudor was a queen.

As used here, ‘queen’ is synonymous with ‘female monarch’. Since nothing relevantly metalinguistic is going on in (13), we can safely substitute ‘female monarch’ for ‘queen’:

(14)
As a result of Mary Tudor’s death, Elizabeth Tudor was a female monarch.

But (14) is open to an objection just like that to (12): Elizabeth’s femaleness wasn’t a result of Mary’s death; she was already female before Mary died, and would have remained female even had Mary not died. Since (14) is true, as noted, something is wrong with the objection to (14); presumably, something similar is wrong with the objection to (12).

A natural hypothesis is that here too presentational features are illicitly influencing assessments of truth value. Roughly, we treat (11) as answering the question ‘What was a result of his illness?’ with ‘He was[/became] an invalid’, and (12) as answering the same question with ‘He was[/became] a self-identical invalid’. Conversationally, including ‘self-identical’ is pointless and misleading unless the self-identity was somehow a result of his illness too. Similarly, we treat (14) as answering the question ‘What was a result of Mary Tudor’s death?’ with ‘Elizabeth Tudor was[/became] a female monarch’. Conversationally, including ‘female’ is pointless and misleading unless Elizabeth’s femaleness was somehow a result of Mary’s death too. But those are just conversational implicatures, as the truth of (13) shows. When there is no syntactically separate item whose inclusion calls out for a rationale, the implicature drops away, and the corresponding explanation is better. Once again, in causal and explanatory contexts, presentational matters leach into assessments of truth value.

There are many more alleged counterexamples to intensionalism than I can discuss here, but I am aware of none clearly better than those already considered. If intensionalism is really false, finding counterexamples to it should not be so hard—and the search for them has been going on for over forty years.

Committed hyperintensionalists may assure us, for their favoured examples, that when they consciously screen out all but strictly metaphysical considerations they still obtain the same hyperintensional results. That has about as much credibility as an employer assuring us that they consciously screen out all forms of racial and gender bias before making an appointment. The heuristics on which we habitually rely are far from transparent to us. We have seen evidence that alleged counterexamples to intensionalism rely on heuristics of limited reliability in cases which may well fall beyond those limits.

We also saw that counterexamples to intensionalism would need to be especially firmly established, to enable hyperintensionalist theorizing to escape defend the obvious charge of overfitting, given its profligacy with degrees of freedom. There is no sign of ‘counterexamples’ to intensionalism meeting that high epistemic standard. The ‘hyperintensional revolution’ seems to be an artefact of a superficially plausible but deeply unsound methodology.

∗∗∗

Anyone who argues as I have just done faces an obvious question. Why not extend the critique of the ‘hyperintensional revolution’ to the ‘intensional revolution’ too? Why not go all the way back to extensionalism? After all, even the intensional revolution involved a significant increase in degrees of freedom, with the consequent danger of overfitting.

The similarities between the two movements amount to much less than the similarity of their names may suggest. The intensional revolution was not example driven to anything like the same extent as the hyperintensional revolution. Extensionalists did not imagine that modal operators were truth functional, until intensionalists surprised them with ingenious examples to show otherwise. There are only four monadic truth functions, and none of them makes any sense of the use of ‘possibly’ or ‘necessarily’. If any truth functional interpretation of the modal operators were correct, our heuristics for them would be useless—and our failure to use what would be much more reliable as well as simpler heuristics for them would be hard to explain.

The intensional revolution resulted from a technical breakthrough in modal logic combined with fruitful applications of the technical innovations to philosophically significant matters. The technical breakthrough was the identification of mathematically natural and tractable classes of formal model with respect to which already familiar systems of modal logic were sound and complete. Within twenty years, modal logic was a mathematically sophisticated, well-developed branch of logic, with many powerful theorems to its credit. On the non-technical side, the applications were normally articulated by interpreting the values of the new parameter in the models as ‘possible worlds’, in various senses. The most obvious applications were to metaphysics and to semantics, as part of linguistics as well as the philosophy of language, but its influence was felt in many other branches of philosophy too. Although intensionalism added a degree of freedom with the possible world parameter, in other ways it had a simplifying effect. For instance, intensionalism went naturally (though not inevitably) with the idea of direct reference, which simplified semantics compared to the descriptivist semantics it replaced. Although counterexamples played a significant role in the literature on applications of modal logic, typically they did so as illustrations of a rich positive alternative theory, not just as attempted proofs of a bare negative result. This setting made the intensional revolution less vulnerable to reliance on heuristics, by enabling theory-informed reasoning to act as a corrective to data-driven thinking.

By contrast, the hyperintensional revolution is not associated with any major technical breakthrough. There are some interesting results, but nothing remotely comparable to the success story of modal logic. The associated formal systems tend to be either too weak (in the case of impossible worlds semantics) or too ad hoc (in the case of truthmaker semantics) to be conducive to fruitful technical developments. In terms of applications, although theories of hyperintensionality have been very influential in metaphysics, their reach outside metaphysics has been much less than that of ‘possible worlds’ thinking. Although such considerations are not decisive, they contribute to the evidence that, in the long run, hyperintensionalism is not a fruitful research programme.

Hyperintensionalists could plead for more time. But intensionalists never had to do that. By the mid-1970s, the intensional revolution was more or less complete: although the intensional tradition did not stop growing and developing, it was no longer revolutionary; it was an established theoretical framework. In twice the time, the supposed hyperintensional revolution has achieved far less.

If overfitting is indeed a problem for hyperintensionalist metaphysics, it will only get worse over time, as more and more degrees of freedom are added in response to dodgy data, with continuing theoretical instability. That will at least have the advantage of making the problem more obvious, as did the continual addition of epicycles to Ptolemaic astronomy.

Could this emphasis on the problem of overfitting be accused of scientism? When philosophy is done in a scientific spirit, there is always a danger of self-righteously imitating the practices of natural science, without properly scrutinizing their supposed relevance to the philosophical question under discussion. When it comes to model building, however, the natural and social sciences have vastly more experience than philosophy has of that methodology, its pitfalls, and ways round them (for model building in philosophy, see Williamson 2017). Metaphysicians need the humility to learn, from all that experience, the dangers of overfitting.

Where overfitting is rife, theorizing can still seem systematic. One may be shown elaborate formal theories, and plausible data which they fit neatly. One may have to probe a little to find the evidence of overfitting. In the hyperintensionalist programme, such evidence is not hard to find. For systematic metaphysics, in the long run, the simpler, more tightly constrained framework of intensionalism is a better bet.1
