Cognitive homelessness

Timothy Williamson

The Journal of Philosophy 93 (1996): 554–573.

There is a constant temptation in philosophy to postulate a realm of phenomena in which nothing is hidden from us. René Descartes thought that one’s own mind is such a realm. Ludwig Wittgenstein1 enlarged the realm to everything that is of interest to philosophy. That they explained this special feature in very different ways hardly needs to be said; what is remarkable is their agreement on our possession of a cognitive home in which everything lies open to our view. Much of our thinking—for example, in the physical sciences—must operate outside this home, in alien circumstances. The claim is that not all our thinking could be like that.

To deny that something is hidden is not to assert that we are infallible about it. Mistakes are always possible. There is no limit to the conclusions into which fallacious reasoning can lure us. The point is that, in our cognitive home, such mistakes are always rectifiable. Similarly, we are not omniscient about our cognitive home. We may not know the answer to a question simply because the question has never occurred to us. Even if something is open to view, we may not have glanced in that direction. Again, the point is that such ignorance is always removable.

My aim is to argue that we are cognitively homeless. Although much is, in fact, accessible to our knowledge, nothing of interest is inherently accessible to it. It is first necessary to sharpen the issue, however, to make it more susceptible to argument.

I

It is convenient to frame the discussion in terms of conditions that obtain or fail to obtain in various contexts. Most conditions obtain in some contexts and not in others. For example, the condition that one is hungry obtains in the context of a particular person at a particular time, but a change of either person or time produces a new context in which the same condition may not obtain. The pronoun ‘one’ will be used for the subject of a context, to whom reference is made by use of the first person in that context, as in ‘the condition that one is hungry’. Although conditions are expressed by sentential clauses, they are not propositions as the latter are usually conceived, just because they are open with respect to person, place, and other contextual features. We often use clauses like that, as in ‘When it rains, it pours’. The domain of contexts will be taken to include counterfactual as well as actual circumstances. Since the circumstances on which the arguments below rely are physically and psychologically feasible, issues about the bounds of possibility are not pressing.

The next notion to be introduced is that of being in a position to know. To be in a position to know that P, it is neither necessary to know that P nor sufficient to be physically and psychologically capable of knowing that P. No obstacle must block one’s path to knowing that P. If one is in a position to know that P, and has done what one is in a position to do to decide whether P, then one does know that P. The fact is open to one’s view, unhidden, even if one does not yet see it. Thus, being in a position to know, like knowing and unlike being physically and psychologically capable of knowing, is factive: if one is in a position to know that P, then P. Although the notion of being in a position to know is obviously somewhat vague, it is clear enough for present purposes.

A special kind of condition can now be defined. If CC is the condition that PP, call CC luminous just in case (L) holds:2

(L)
For every context α\alpha, if in α\alpha PP, then in α\alpha one is in a position to know that PP.

Since being in a position to know is factive, the converse of (L) holds for any value of ‘PP’, so the conditional in (L) could just as well be a biconditional. The picture is that a luminous condition always shines brightly enough to make its presence visible. But (L) does not say that the condition that PP must obtain independently of our dispositions to judge that PP; for all (L) says, the condition might obtain in virtue of those dispositions. Now, a realm in which nothing is hidden is a realm in which all conditions are luminous. The question is: What conditions, if any, are in fact luminous?

Some examples will help. Pain is often conceived as a luminous condition, in the sense that, if one is in pain, then one is in a position to know that one is in pain.3 The definition of luminosity gives scope to finesse some of the more obvious objections to claims of this kind. Thus, people who lack the concept of pain (perhaps because their concepts carve up the space of possible sensations in an alternative way), and so never know that they are in pain, may still count as being in a position to know that they are in pain. Perhaps more primitive creatures are sometimes in pain without possessing any concepts at all; if they count as not even being in a position to know that they are in pain, a counterexample to luminosity might still be avoided by the ruling that the subject of a context must be a possessor of concepts. These rulings are not endorsed here, but they demonstrate the kinds of consideration to which the notion of luminosity can be made sensitive.

Two claims of luminosity are implicit in the following passage from Michael Dummett:4

It is an undeniable feature of the notion of meaning—obscure as that notion is—that meaning is transparent in the sense that, if someone attaches a meaning to each of two words, he must know whether these meanings are the same (ibid., p. 131).5

Thus, if two words have the same meaning in the context of a given subject (it is meaning for this subject which is in question), then that subject is in a position to know that they have the same meaning, and if the words have different meanings, then the subject is in a position to know that they have different meanings.6 The two claims of luminosity are genuinely distinct, for the premise that whenever PP one is in a position to know that PP does not entail the conclusion that whenever not-PP one is in a position to know that not-PP. If whenever one is awake one is in a position to know that one is awake, it does not follow that whenever one is not awake one is in a position to know that one is not awake.7

Other conditions for which luminosity is often claimed are those of the form ‘it appears to one that P’. When there really is an oasis ahead, one may not be in a position to know that there really is an oasis ahead, but, it is supposed, when there at least appears to be an oasis ahead, one must be in a position to know that there at least appears to be an oasis ahead.

I shall not dispute here that there are luminous conditions. An inconsistent condition is luminous because (L) holds vacuously; for it, the antecedent ‘in α P’ cannot obtain in any context. It is less obvious that a tautological condition is luminous, because it is less obvious that every context must have a subject who is in a position to possess the concepts that occur in the relevant tautology. I shall concede the point for the sake of argument, however, so that tautological conditions are luminous, too. The question thus becomes: Are there nontrivial luminous conditions? In the next section, I present a paradigmatic instance of a general form of argument against the luminosity of a given nontrivial condition.

II

For definiteness, I shall first present the argument for the particular case of the condition that one feels hot. To facilitate generalization, no special features of this case will be exploited. The argument proceeds by reductio ad absurdum on the relevant instance of (L), which claims that whenever one feels hot, one is in a position to know that one feels hot.

Suppose that the condition that one feels hot is luminous. Consider a morning on which one feels chilly at dawn, very slowly warms up, and feels much too hot by noon. One changes from not feeling hot to feeling hot, and from not being in a position to know that one feels hot to being in a position to know that one feels hot. If the condition that one feels hot is luminous, these changes are exactly simultaneous. Suppose that one’s feelings of heat and cold change so slowly during this process that one is not aware of any change in them over one second. Suppose, also, that throughout the process one thoroughly considers how hot or cold one feels. One’s confidence that one feels hot gradually increases. One’s initial answers to the question ‘Do you feel hot?’ are firmly negative; then hesitations and qualifications creep in, until one gives neutral answers, such as ‘It is hard to say’; then one begins to assent, with gradually decreasing hesitations and qualifications; one’s final answers are firmly positive.

Let t0,t1,…,tnt_0, t_1,\ldots,t_n be a series of times at one second intervals from dawn to noon. Let αi\alpha_i be the context at tit_i (0≤i≤n0\leq i\leq n). Consider a time tit_i between t0t_0 and tnt_n, and suppose that at tit_i one is in a position to know that one feels hot. By hypothesis, at tit_i one has done what one is in a position to do to decide whether one feels hot, by thoroughly considering the matter, so one does know that one feels hot. Thus, one is at least reasonably confident that one feels hot, for otherwise one would not know. Moreover, this confidence must be reliably based, for otherwise one would still not know that one feels hot. This is not to assume that the concept of knowledge can be analyzed in terms of the concept of reliability: it is just to assume that reliability is necessary for knowledge (which is hard to deny), but not that other necessary conditions can be framed (without use of the concept of knowledge) whose conjunction with reliability is a necessary and sufficient condition for knowledge. Nor does the invocation of reliability presuppose that the condition obtains independently of one’s dispositions to judge that it does; a constitutive connection between them is often supposed to underpin luminosity, but its effect is to make reliability less contingent, not to make unreliability consistent with being in a position to know. Now, at ti−1t_{i-1} one is almost equally confident that one feels hot, by the description of the case. So if one does not feel hot at ti−1t_{i-1}, one’s confidence at tit_i that one feels hot is not reliably based, for one’s almost equal confidence on a similar basis a second earlier that one felt hot was mistaken. In picturesque terms, that large proportion of one’s confidence at tit_i which one already had at ti−1t_{i-1} is misplaced. Even if one’s confidence at tit_i is just enough to count as belief, while one’s confidence at ti−1t_{i-1} fell just short of belief, what constitutes that belief at tit_i is largely misplaced confidence; the belief falls short of knowledge. One’s confidence at tit_i is reliably based in the way required for knowledge only if one feels hot at ti−1t_{i-1}. Since, as already argued, one’s confidence at tit_i is so reliably based, one does feel hot at ti−1t_{i-1}. In the terminology of contexts, this conditional has been upheld:

(1)
If in αi\alpha_i one is in a position to know that one feels hot, then in αi−1\alpha_{i-1} one feels hot.

Note that (1) is merely a description of a specific process; it does not purport to be a general principle about feeling hot. It is asserted for each ii from 1 to nn, but it says nothing about contexts other than α0,…,αn\alpha_0,\ldots,\alpha_n.

The argument can now be completed. The supposed luminosity of the condition that one feels hot yields this conditional:

(2)
If in αi\alpha_i one feels hot, then in αi\alpha_i one is in a position to know that one feels hot.

Now, suppose this:

(3)
In αi\alpha_i one feels hot.

By modus ponens, (2) and (3) yield this:

(4)
In αi\alpha_i one is in a position to know that one feels hot.

By modus ponens, (1) and (4) yield this:

(5)
In αi−1\alpha_{i-1} one feels hot.

The following is certainly true, for αn\alpha_n is the context at noon, when one feels very hot:

(6)
In αn\alpha_n one feels hot.

By repeating the argument from (4) to (5) n times, for descending values of i from n to 1, we reach the following from (6):

(7)
In α0\alpha_0 one feels hot.

But (7) is certainly false, for α0\alpha_0 is the context at dawn, when one feels chilly. Thus, not all instances of (1) and (2) are true. But it has just been argued that all instances of (1) are true. Thus, not all instances of (2) are true. Since (2) expresses the luminosity of the condition that one feels hot, that condition is not luminous. Feeling hot does not imply being in a position to know that one feels hot.

III

An obvious doubt arises about the argument of section II. The reasoning is very reminiscent of that in sorites paradoxes (How can i grains make a heap unless i−1i-1 do?). It may therefore be suspected of concealing a fallacy just like the concealed fallacy in sorites reasoning, whatever that is. May it not be illicitly exploiting the vagueness of ‘feels hot’ or ‘in a position to know’?

The doubt can be made more specific. If the conclusion of the argument is false, then either not all the premises are true or the reasoning is invalid. Given instances of (1) and the straightforwardly true (6) as premises, the argument derives instances of (2) from the supposed luminosity of the condition at issue, and then uses modus ponens to reach the straightforwardly false (7); by reductio ad absurdum, luminosity is rejected. On any reasonable view of vagueness, this reasoning shows that the luminosity claim is less than perfectly true, given that all instances of (1) are perfectly true. On some accounts, modus ponens fails to preserve less than perfect truth, but it should still preserve perfect truth. On other accounts, reductio ad absurdum is problematic because an assumption can have perfectly false consequences without itself being perfectly false, and therefore without having a perfectly true negation; nevertheless, it does still follow that the assumption is less than perfectly true. Moreover, it is arguable that vagueness requires no revision of classical logic at all.8 For my purposes here, it would suffice to argue that the luminosity claim is less than perfectly true, for then it will have perfectly false consequences, which should discourage its application to philosophy. Thus, the way for the defender of (perfect) luminosity to use the connection with sorites paradoxes is by arguing that not all instances of (1) are perfectly true, and using the vagueness of some relevant term to explain away their plausibility. Of course, the argument for (1) would remain to be addressed. Fortunately, however, the strategy can be tested more directly. For if (1) is, in effect, the major premise of a sorites paradox, then sharpening the relevantly vague expressions should give (1) a clearly false instance, just as sharpening the term ‘heap’ by stipulating a cut-off point gives the conditional ‘If ii grains make a heap, then i−1i-1 grains make a heap’ a clearly false instance. Does it?

The relevantly vague expressions in (1) are ‘feels hot’ and ‘in a position to know’. Sharpen ‘feels hot’ by using a physiological condition to resolve borderline cases. Assume that the subject of the process has no access to the technology needed to determine whether the physiological condition obtains, and so is not in a position to know whether it does. These stipulations in no way weaken the argument for (1). The considerations about reliability remain as cogent as before, for they were based on our limited powers of discrimination among our own sensations, not on the vagueness of ‘feels hot’. It might be objected that the sharpening violates the intended meaning of ‘feels hot’. But that would not undermine the contrast between (1) and the major premise of a sorites paradox. For any complete sharpening of ‘heap’ yields a clearly false instance of the principle ‘If ii grains make a heap, then i−1i-1 grains make a heap’, even if it violates the intended meaning of ‘heap’ by, for example, falsifying the converse upward principle ‘If i−1i-1 grains make a heap, then ii grains make a heap’. By definition, the sharpened term applies wherever the unsharpened term clearly applied and fails to apply wherever the unsharpened term clearly failed to apply; thus, on any sharpening, ‘10,000 (optimally arranged) grains make a heap’ is true and ‘0 grains make a heap’ false, so the major premise has a false instance. Even the truth of (1) on a sharpening of its vague terms that violates their intended meaning is enough to differentiate (1) from the major premise of a sorites paradox.

The vague expression ‘in a position to know’ remains. Sharpen it by tightening up its conditions of application: it is not to apply in borderline cases of being in a position to know (in the old sense). If anything, this strengthens the argument for (1), by building more into its antecedent. It does not help one to know whether one feels hot; indeed, one need not even be aware of the stipulation about ‘in a position to know’, for it is made by the theorist, not by the subject. The stipulations will not make ‘feels hot’ and ‘in a position to know’ perfectly precise; no feasible sharpening could do that. Fortunately, perfect precision is not necessary. It is sufficient to sharpen those expressions to a point at which the finitely many borderline cases that actually arise in the argument are resolved. Such sharpening has the opposite effect to that predicted by the assimilation of the argument against luminosity to sorites reasoning; (1) becomes more not less plausible. The argument is not just another sorites paradox.

Nevertheless, the argument against (2) might be thought to commit a subtler fallacy of vagueness. A defender of (2) might take the vagueness of its constituent terms to be essential to its truth, and explain the plausibility of (1) by assigning it a status short of perfect truth, while conceding that all instances of (1) are true on some sharpenings, such as those considered above. All sharpenings that falsify an instance of (2) might be held to violate the intended meanings of the vague terms, on the grounds that those meanings make (2) analytic. On such a view, the unsharpened (1) would be almost but not quite perfectly true in some instances, because its consequent would be almost but not quite as true as its antecedent. The reliability conditions adduced in favor of (1) would be treated as almost but not quite perfectly correct. No justification has been provided for not treating them as perfectly correct, but let that pass; for the concession is in any case inadequate. The view must reject the following variation on (1):

(1P)
If it is perfectly true that in αi\alpha_i one is in a position to know that one feels hot, then it is perfectly true that in αi−1\alpha_{i-1} one feels hot.

For if (2) is perfectly true, then the perfect truth of its antecedent implies the perfect truth of its consequent:

(2P)
If it is perfectly true that in αi\alpha_i one feels hot, then it is perfectly true that in αi\alpha_i one is in a position to know that one feels hot.

(1P) and (2P) give an argument from the perfect truth of (3) to the perfect truth of (5), and therefore from the perfect truth of (6) (which is uncontested) to the perfect truth of (7); but the falsity of (7) is uncontested. The objector will treat (1P) like (1), claiming that for some ii, it is perfectly true that in αi\alpha_i one is in a position to know that one feels hot, but slightly less than perfectly true that in αi−1\alpha_{i-1} one feels hot. But can there be such an ii? If it is less than perfectly true that in αi−1\alpha_{i-1} one feels hot, then there is a strict standard by which it is false in αi−1\alpha_{i-1} that one feels hot; so by that standard, in αi−1\alpha_{i-1} one is fairly confident of what is false, that one feels hot (by the argument in section II), in which case it is less than perfectly true that in αi\alpha_i one is in a position to know that one feels hot, if the reliability considerations are to be assigned any positive weight at all. To put the argument more directly, if it is perfectly true that in αi\alpha_i one is in a position to know that one feels hot, then it is perfectly true that the reliability requirement is met, and therefore perfectly true that in αi−1\alpha_{i-1} one feels hot. Thus, the objection to (1P) fails, and (1P) suffices for an argument against the perfect truth of (2). Invoking degrees of truth will not protect luminosity claims.9

The point is reinforced by the observation that, once the luminosity assumption is dropped, (7) does not follow in classical logic from (6) and all instances of (1). To see this, pick jj and kk such that n≥k>j>0n\geq k>j>0; for each ii, evaluate ‘one feels hot’ as true in αi\alpha_i if and only if i≥ji\geq j, and otherwise as false; evaluate ‘one is in a position to know that one feels hot’ as true in αi\alpha_i if and only if i≥ki\geq k, and otherwise as false. On this evaluation, each instance of (1) is true, for if the antecedent is true, then i≥k>ji\geq k>j, so i−1≥ji-1\geq j, so the consequent is true. (6) is true because n≥jn\geq j. (7) is false because j>0j>0. One can elaborate this evaluation in the manner of the standard semantics for modal logic by treating contexts like possible worlds and ‘one is in a position to know that…’ like ‘it is necessary that…’. The foregoing evaluation results if one defines αh\alpha_h to be accessible from αi\alpha_i just in case |h−i|≤k−j|h-i|\leq k-j, evaluates ‘one is in a position to know that PP’ as true at a context αi\alpha_i if and only if ‘PP’ is true at all contexts accessible from αi\alpha_i, and evaluates ‘one feels hot’ as before. Since a classical evaluation makes (6) and all instances of (1) true and (7) false, the latter does not follow from the former in classical logic. Contrast the sorites paradox: ‘0 grains make a heap’ does follow in classical logic from ‘nn grains make a heap’ and all instances of ‘If ii grains make a heap, then i−1i-1 grains make a heap’. Once luminosity is denied, (1) generates no paradox.

It is consistent with all this to postulate a more general phenomenon of which both the vagueness of ‘heap’ and the nonluminosity of the condition that one feels hot are instances. Inexact knowledge may be such a phenomenon. It is characterized by margin for error principles like (1), according to which, if in some context one is in a position to know that PP, then in all sufficiently similar contexts it is true that PP, whether or not one is in a position to know it, since otherwise one’s judgments would lack the margin for error that reliability requires, given one’s limited powers of discrimination; how much similarity suffices, and in what respects, depends on the details of the case.10 If vagueness is characterized by margin for error principles, however, their source will be of a peculiarly conceptual kind.11 On this view, the epistemological principles underlying (1) are important for vagueness, too, but it does not follow that all their manifestations involve vagueness. The argument against luminosity does not depend on any theory of vagueness.

IV

How far does the argument against luminosity generalize? It assumes nothing specific about the condition of feeling hot. It extends to the examples of supposedly luminous conditions mentioned in section I. The onset of pain can be gradual, for example, as odd sensations merge into painful ones. An argument against the luminosity of the condition that one is in pain can be modeled on the argument against the luminosity of the condition that one feels hot, without any structural revisions. It is not perfectly true that in every context in which one is in pain, one is in a position to know that one is in pain. Being in pain does not imply being in a position to know that one is in pain.

Similarly, two words can gradually become synonymous, as a difference in application withers into a stylistic preference which slowly disappears altogether. The structure of the argument against luminosity is just as before. Assigning two words the same meaning does not imply being in a position to know that one assigns them the same meaning. Equally, assigning two words different meanings does not imply being in a position to know that one assigns them different meanings.

The argument also applies to conditions of the form ‘it appears to one that PP’. A context in which it does not appear to one that PP can gradually change into a context in which it does appear to one that PP. Having it appear to one that PP does not imply being in a position to know that it appears to one that PP.12

Further applications of the argument involve conditions on what one is in a position to know. Since a context in which one is not in a position to know that PP can gradually change into a context in which one is in a position to know that PP, the argument shows that the condition that one is in a position to know that PP is nonluminous (for most values of ‘PP’).13 Being in a position to know that PP does not imply being in a position to know that one is in a position to know that PP. Thus, the operator ‘one is in a position to know that…’ does not satisfy the principle □p→□□p□ p\rightarrow□□ p of the modal logic S4. Indeed, actually knowing that PP does not imply being in a position to know that one knows that PP. Equally, not being in a position to know that PP does not imply being in a position to know that one is not in a position to know that PP. Thus, the operator ‘one is in a position to know that…’ does not satisfy the principle ¬□p→□¬□p\neg□ p\rightarrow□\neg□ p of the stronger modal logic S5. Indeed, not knowing that PP does not imply being in a position to know that one does not know that PP. These results can be extended. For example, there is no natural number nn such that nn iterations of ‘one is in a position to know that…’ imply n+1n+1 iterations of it. The general absence of nontrivial luminous conditions can be represented in modal logic by the rule that A→□AA\rightarrow□ A is a theorem only if either AA is a theorem or ¬A\neg A is.14

What are the general features of a condition on which the argument against luminosity depends? As it stands, it requires the condition to obtain in some contexts and not in others. Thus, it is ineffective against conditions that hold in all contexts or in none. Given a sufficiently restrictive understanding of what a context is, those might include some Cartesian conditions, such as that one exists and even that one thinks. They do not include the condition that one is thinking about one’s existence, however, since one is thinking about one’s existence in some contexts and not in others on any reasonable understanding of what a context is.

A condition that obtains in no context is automatically luminous. A condition that obtains in every context is not automatically luminous, for it is not automatic that in every context one is in a position to know that it obtains: perhaps it is an a posteriori necessity, or a mathematical theorem as yet unproved. But if one is not in every context in a position to know that the condition obtains, although the condition does obtain in every context, then it follows at once that the condition is not luminous. What is automatically luminous is a condition such that in every context one is in a position to know that it obtains.

The argument also requires the possibility of a change from a context in which the condition does not obtain to a context in which it does. Thus, the argument would not be effective against an eternal condition that always obtains if it ever obtains: for example, the condition that one felt hot at noon on midsummer’s day 1996. But an eternal condition often permits a change from a context in which one is not in a position to know that it obtains to a context in which one is in a position to know that it obtains; this certainly applies to the example just given. Such a condition cannot be luminous, for it obtains in the later context in which one is in a position to know that it obtains (because being in a position to know is factive), so it obtains in the earlier context in which one is not in a position to know that it obtains (because the condition is eternal). Thus, an eternal condition is luminous only if it is impossible to change from not being in a position to know that it obtains to being in such a position. No argument has been given against the luminosity of a condition such that, if one is ever in a position to know that it obtains, then one is always in a position to know that it obtains, even though some people are in a position to know that it obtains while others are not. There are candidates for such conditions. For example, if a subject S is always in a position to know that she is S (which is not to say that she must know her own name), then anyone who is ever in a position to know that the condition that one is S obtains is always in a position to know that it obtains, because the only such person is S herself. Perhaps the argument could be extended to show that not even this condition is luminous, by consideration of a science-fictional process of gradual change in which someone else becomes (is replaced by) S. But no such extension will be attempted here. Such examples do not seriously threaten the idea that only trivial conditions are luminous.

The argument depends on the gradualness of the change from contexts in which the condition does not obtain to contexts in which it does. The relevant kind of gradualness is epistemic, however. Even if there is a jump in conditions, the argument still applies, provided that there is no corresponding jump in our awareness of them. Such a jump in our awareness is never guaranteed to occur. Thus, the gradualness requirement does not obviously exclude any further conditions. We might therefore conjecture that no condition such that one can pass from not being in a position to know that it obtains to being in a position to know that it obtains is luminous.

A difficulty for the conjecture is raised by the notion of ancestrally knowing. One ancestrally knows that PP just in case PP, and one knows that PP, and one knows that one knows that PP, and so on ad infinitum. The condition that one ancestrally knows that PP might seem to be luminous; for consider the following inference, with infinitely many premises:

PP
One knows that PP.
One knows that one knows that PP.
⋮\vdots
One ancestrally knows that PP.

The inference is deductively valid, by definition of ancestral knowledge. Now, anyone who ancestrally knows that PP knows each premise of the argument, and so (it seems) is in a position to know the conclusion, for one should be in a position to know, even if one does not actually know, that which follows deductively from what one knows. Hence the condition that one ancestrally knows that PP is luminous. Nevertheless, for most values of ‘PP’, there seems to be no metaphysical impossibility in a change from not ancestrally knowing that PP to ancestrally knowing that PP, and so from not being in a position to know that one ancestrally knows that PP to being in a position to know that one ancestrally knows that PP. The condition that one ancestrally knows that PP is therefore a prima facie counterexample to the conjecture.

The counterexample might be challenged on the grounds that we are not in a position to make infinitary inferences. Indeed, even when the inference is finitary, it is not obvious that one is in a position to know that which follows deductively from what one knows. Only in a rather attenuated sense are we in a position to know all the consequences of the axioms of Peano arithmetic. This response is not wholly satisfying, however, for the original argument against luminosity made no appeal to limits on powers of inference. If the condition that one ancestrally knows that PP is luminous in the attenuated sense, why does the original argument fail to generalize to this case?

Ancestral knowing may fail the gradualness requirement. Although it is possible to change from not ancestrally knowing that PP to ancestrally knowing that PP, the change may necessarily be sudden. After all, it is the change from finitely many iterations of knowledge to infinitely many. How could it be gradual? Thus, ancestral knowing may provide a counterexample at least to the conjecture that no condition such that one can pass from not being in a position to know that it obtains to being in a position to know that it obtains is luminous in the attenuated sense.

If ancestral knowing does fail the gradualness requirement, it will be a hard state to enter. How is one to make the instantaneous jump from the finite to the infinite? The kind of common knowledge that we are supposed to have of conventions is usually defined in a way that requires us to know ancestrally; for example, if John knows that Jane knows that John knows that Jane knows that John knows that PP, then John knows that John knows that John knows that PP (at least in the attenuated sense). Common knowledge would therefore be a convenient idealization, like a frictionless plane.15 Thus, even if the conjecture is false, all luminous conditions may still be trivial in practice, for the condition that one ancestrally knows that PP seems to be empty in practice. Similar remarks apply to being ancestrally in a position to know, which stands to being in a position to know as ancestrally knowing stands to knowing.

Luminous conditions are curiosities. Far from forming a cognitive home, they are remote from our ordinary interests. The conditions with which we engage in our everyday life are, from the start, nonluminous. Their obtaining does not wait for us to be in a position to know that they obtain.

V

To be physically and psychologically capable of knowing that PP is not sufficient, even given that PP, for being in a position to know that PP; one may be in the wrong place. Thus, it does not follow from the nonluminosity of a condition that there are contexts in which, though it obtains, one is not physically and psychologically capable of knowing that it obtains. Nevertheless, it is natural to ask, if one is not in a position to know that one feels hot when one does feel hot, how is one to know it at all? Of course, in many other contexts one does know that one feels hot.16 The question is this: If in context α\alpha one feels hot, perhaps without being in a position to know that one feels hot, must or can there be another context β\beta in which one knows that in α\alpha one feels hot? Analogous questions can be asked about other nonluminous conditions. The argument for nonluminosity leaves them open. It is consistent with, but does not entail, the possibility of a physiological technique by which one could subsequently discover that one had been feeling hot in α\alpha.

The hypothetical technique faces difficulties. Suppose that feelings of hot and cold are found generally to be correlated with a measurable physiological variable VV. We must discover which values of VV are associated with the condition that one feels hot. They include the values associated with the condition that one is in a position to know that one feels hot. But if they included only those values, the condition that one feels hot would be luminous, which it is not. We are not in a position to know which further values of VV are associated with that condition. Our problem is that we cannot calibrate the physiological measurement of feeling hot. Even if measurements of VV were perfectly precise—which they will not be—they would not answer the original question. Attempts to measure other ordinary conditions face similar problems.

It might still be held to be metaphysically possible to find out whether one feels hot by the testimony of a literal or metaphorical deus ex machina. But it certainly cannot be assumed without argument that, if an ordinary condition obtains in a context α\alpha, then in some possible context it is known that the condition obtains in α\alpha.

VI

The failure of luminosity impinges on Dummett’s arguments for an antirealist theory of meaning. Of course, Dummett’s antirealist does not make the extreme claim that every condition is luminous. All parties can accept that stone-age people lived in contexts in which the moon caused the tides, although they were not in a position to know that the moon caused the tides. The connection between luminosity and antirealism is a subtler one.

Dummett objects to the realist’s truth-conditional theory of meaning that it violates a necessary connection between meaning and use. To understand a sentence is to know what it means; if, as Dummett’s realist holds, meanings are truth conditions, then speakers of a language know the truth conditions of its sentences.17 Knowing the truth condition of a sentence ss cannot consist merely in being disposed to say something of the form ‘ss is true if and only if PP’; one must also understand the biconditional, and an infinite regress looms. If one could always recognize whether the truth condition obtained, then knowledge of the truth condition of ss might consist in a willingness to assert ss just when the truth condition obtains (it is assumed that dishonesty, shyness, and other complications can somehow be filtered out). But the realist insists that the truth conditions of some sentences obtain even though no speaker of the language can recognize that they obtain. Dummett argues that the realist has no substantial explanation of what knowing such truth conditions consists in. The proposed remedy is that the meaning of a sentence should be given by the condition for it to be warrantedly assertible, rather than by the condition for it to be true. Thus, to understand a sentence is to know its assertibility condition, and this knowledge can consist in a willingness to assert the sentence just when the assertibility condition obtains.18 The remedy would fail if the objection to truth-conditional theories of meaning applied equally to assertibility-conditional theories of meaning; thus, the argument requires that when an assertibility condition obtains, competent speakers of the language can recognize that it obtains. Dummett acknowledges this requirement: “The conditions under which a sentence is recognized as true or false […] have, by the nature of the case, to be conditions which we can recognize as obtaining when they obtain.”19 That is, when a recognition condition obtains, we can recognize that the recognition condition obtains. Dummett evidently intends the recognition condition for the truth of a sentence to be its assertibility condition, which yields the thesis that when an assertibility condition obtains, we can recognize that it obtains. But recognizing is coming to know, and Dummett’s ‘can’ may be glossed as ‘is in a position to’. Thus, Dummett requires assertibility conditions to be luminous.

The argument against luminosity in section II generalizes to assertibility conditions. For example, contexts in which it is not assertible that it is raining change gradually into contexts in which it is assertible that it is raining. By the argument, having it assertible that it is raining does not imply being in a position to know that it is assertible that it is raining. Even in the mathematical case, in which Dummett uses the proof-based intuitionistic semantics as a paradigm of an assertibility-conditional theory of meaning, proofs are acquired gradually; it takes time to understand and check them.20 By the argument, having a proof of a mathematical assertion does not imply being in a position to know that one has a proof of it. Thus, assertibility conditions have the very feature that is supposed to lay truth conditions open to Dummett’s attack.21

It might be replied that one’s antirealist understanding of a sentence can consist in the fact that one is willing to assert it when and only when its assertibility condition obtains, even if one does not know that it obtains. This reply concedes that assertibility conditions fail Dummett’s luminosity constraint; but then something is wrong with his argument for assertibility-conditional theories of meaning, which treats that constraint as binding.

A different reply is that, if Dummett intends recognizability to be assertibility, then what he requires is only that when it is assertible that PP, it is assertible that it is assertible that PP (the S4 principle for assertibility).22 If false assertions are sometimes warranted by misleading evidence, then it might be assertible that it is assertible that PP even when one is not in a position to know that it is assertible that PP; thus, Dummett would not require assertibility conditions to be luminous. This reply is open to objection. The argument that only trivial conditions are luminous can be generalized to an argument that only trivial conditions satisfy the requirement that whenever they obtain, it is assertible that they obtain: (1) is replaced by a conditional of the form ‘If in αi\alpha_i it is assertible that condition CC obtains, then in αi−1\alpha_{i-1} CC obtains’. Of course, assertibility obeys a margin-for-error principle universally only if it is factive, for since any context α\alpha will be similar enough in relevant respects to itself, ‘In α\alpha it is assertible that CC obtains’ would imply ‘In α\alpha CC obtains’, given such a principle. Nevertheless, the analogue of (1) holds in particular examples, when the evidence may be inadequate but is not positively misleading, which is all that the argument requires.23 Moreover, appearances notwithstanding, having a warrant to assert that PP arguably is being in a position to know that PP. On this view, assertibility is factive but defeasible in the sense that it can be lost when further (misleading) evidence is gained, just as knowledge can be.24

The dialectical situation is further complicated by Dummett’s presentation of his argument as a challenge to the realist, to explain what knowledge of realist truth conditions consists in. He does not claim to prove that the realist cannot meet the challenge, although he certainly denies that it has been met so far. Nor does he rule out the possibility that it can be met in some areas and not in others. But Dummett does assume that the antirealist can easily meet the corresponding challenge, to explain what knowledge of assertibility conditions consists in. If the argument of this paper is correct, that assumption is false; the antirealist faces the same sort of difficulty as the realist does. The contrast between truth conditions and assertibility conditions may be off the point.

Both truth-conditional and assertibility-conditional theories of meaning find it hard to meet Dummett’s challenge because both truth conditions and assertibility conditions are nonluminous. They share this feature with every other kind of nontrivial condition that might be offered as the meaning of a sentence. Since trivial conditions are not serious candidates for the meanings of most sentences, a serious X-conditional theory of meaning will find it hard to meet Dummett’s challenge, for any X. If any systematic (contrast: skeptical) theory of meaning can be cast as X-conditional for some X, then any systematic theory of meaning will find it hard to meet Dummett’s challenge. If ‘hard’ turns out to be ‘impossible’, then failure to meet the challenge eliminates truth-conditional theories of meaning only if it eliminates all systematic theories of meaning. The challenge may be suspected of depending on excessive demands on a theory of meaning; in particular, the demand for an explanation of what knowledge of meaning ‘consists in’ seems to embody a dubious reductionism. That suspicion will not be investigated here.

On an antirealist picture, thought initially engages with conditions whose esse is their percipi; if it later finds its laborious way to conditions of greater depth, it must do so from the starting point of that cognitive home. Assertibility conditions are pictured as forming a linguistic version of the cognitive home. They do not. Thought engages with conditions whose esse is distinct from their percipi as soon as it engages with any conditions at all; even perception does. Trivialities aside, there is nothing else with which to engage. We have no cognitive home.
