Verificationism and non-distributive knowledge

Timothy Williamson

Australasian Journal of Philosophy 71 (1) (1993): 78–86.

There are unknown truths, but are there any unknowable ones? A negative answer may be called verificationism, for want of a better term. It is the direct outcome of some epistemic or pragmatist conceptions of truth, and the indirect outcome of some epistemic or pragmatist conceptions of meaning. All these conceptions are threatened by an argument against verificationism. The argument in question purports to show that if there are unknown truths (as there surely are), there are unknowable ones. Granted classical logic, the argument appears to stand or fall with a premise to the effect that knowledge distributes over conjunction; if one knows the truth of a conjunction, one knows the truth of its conjuncts. Verificationists may seek to escape the argument by denying the premise. One aim of this paper is to close that loophole. The plausibility of the premise is defended but, for safety’s sake, the argument is reconstructed without it.

I

The claim that every truth is knowable may be schematically formalized as:

(1)

p→◊Kpp \rightarrow \lozenge \mathrm{K}p.

A more precise claim is that, for any true proposition which humans can conceive, it is possible that the truth of that proposition was, is or will be known by some human. Thus ‘pp’ is a propositional variable ranging over propositions which humans can conceive, ‘◊\lozenge’ is to be read as ‘it is possible that’ (in the sense ‘it is or could have been the case that’) and ‘K’ as ‘it was, is or will be known by some human that’. The verificationist asserts (1) for all values of ‘pp’.1

It is clear that not all truths conceivable by humans will ever be known by them. One does not want the principle:

(2)

p→Kpp \rightarrow \mathrm{K}p.

For example, if pp is the proposition that the number of tennis balls in NN’s garden on 7th November, 1991 is even, and qq the proposition that it is odd, both propositions have been conceived by a human, but either pp is a truth never known by any human or qq is; one or the other is a counter-example to (2).

The relevant assumptions about knowledge can now be formulated. Knowledge is veridical; neither the actual world nor any possible one contains untrue knowledge. Thus the converse of (2) is necessary:

(3)

Kp→p\mathrm{K}p \rightarrow p.

It is important here that the ‘was, is or will be’ in the definiens of ‘K’ does not act as a tense operator on the content clause following ‘known’, but only on ‘known’ itself. Thus K(it is raining)\mathrm{K}(\text{it is raining}) is evaluated as true with respect to a time tt if there is knowledge at some time t#t\# of rain at tt; knowledge at t#t\# of rain at t#t\# is neither necessary nor sufficient. This is in fact a natural reading of the English ‘It was, is or will be known that it is raining’. On the other reading, Kp\mathrm{K}p would entail ‘It was, is or will be the case that pp’, but not pp itself. The claim that knowledge distributes over conjunction can also be reflected in the language:

(4)

K(p&q)→(Kp&Kq)\mathrm{K}(p \mathbin{\&} q) \rightarrow (\mathrm{K}p \mathbin{\&} \mathrm{K}q).

(4) is supposed to be non-contingently true.

The anti-verificationist argument is intended as a reductio ad absurdum of (1) to (2), by appeal to the necessity of (3) and (4). The first stage uses (3) and (4) to establish:

(5)

∼K(p&∼Kp)\mathord{\sim} \mathrm{K}(p \mathbin{\&} \mathord{\sim} \mathrm{K}p).

For, by substitution, (3) yields K(p&∼Kp)→∼Kp\mathrm{K}(p \mathbin{\&} \mathord{\sim} \mathrm{K}p) \rightarrow \mathord{\sim} \mathrm{K}p while (4) yields K(p&∼Kp)→Kp\mathrm{K}(p \mathbin{\&} \mathord{\sim} \mathrm{K}p) \rightarrow \mathrm{K}p. Since (3) and (4) are assumed to be necessary, K(p&∼Kp)\mathrm{K}(p \mathbin{\&} \mathord{\sim} \mathrm{K}p) is impossible, not just false:

(6)

∼◊K(p&∼Kp)\mathord{\sim}\lozenge \mathrm{K}(p \mathbin{\&} \mathord{\sim} \mathrm{K}p).

Now a special case of (1) is (p&∼Kp)→◊K(p&∼Kp)(p \mathbin{\&} \mathord{\sim} \mathrm{K}p) \rightarrow \lozenge \mathrm{K}(p \mathbin{\&} \mathord{\sim} \mathrm{K}p); thus, by (6), (1) yields ∼(p&∼Kp)\mathord{\sim}(p \mathbin{\&} \mathord{\sim} \mathrm{K}p), which is equivalent to (2) in classical logic. QED.2

If knowledge does not distribute over conjunction, (4) is not necessarily true and the argument against (1) breaks down.3 Indeed, there are systems based on the classical modal logic KT (also known as T, and as M) in which (1) and (3) are theorems, but (2) is not derivable. Can the verificationist plausibly deny that knowledge necessarily distributes over conjunction?

II

Many propositional attitudes distributes over conjunction. In conceiving that pp and qq, for example, one conceives that pp and conceives that qq; in saying that pp and qq, one says that pp and says that qq. Not all attitudes distribute. I disbelieve the conjunction that Edinburgh is in Scotland and Scotland is in Asia because I disbelieve the latter conjunct, but I do not disbelieve the former. However, that example leaves open the possibility that all positive propositional attitudes distribute over conjunction, in a sense of ‘positive’ that would need to be made precise.4

If a positive propositional attitude is closed under at least some forms of logical consequence, it is likely to be closed under a very obvious one such as &\mathbin{\&}-Elimination. To investigate whether it distributes over conjunction would then be to test the null hypothesis that it satisfies no form of deductive closure at all. The null hypothesis in turn exemplifies a maximally liberal view of the extent to which rationality constrains the attribution of propositional attitudes.5

This paper concerns the special case of knowledge. We plainly do not know all the logical consequences of what we know; that is the point of mathematics. We do not even know all the obvious logical consequences of what we know, for what we know is linked to some of what we do not by chains of propositions each member of which is an obvious logical consequence of its predecessors. Thus a verificationist can deny that knowledge distributes over conjunction without denying that a conjunction obviously entails its conjuncts.

Two quite different kinds of counter-example might be offered against the claim that knowledge distributes over conjunction. For there are several logically independent necessary conditions for knowledge, and the nature of the putative counter-example will depend on which of them is supposed not to be transmitted from conjunction to conjuncts. Truth, of course, is one necessary condition for knowledge that cannot fail to be transmitted. It will simplify the classification to assume that belief is a necessary condition for knowledge; even if that principle is not quite correct when taken in its everyday sense, knowledge presumably does entail some sort of acceptance, for which the word ‘belief’ may be taken to stand in what follows.

Suppose that SS knows that pp and qq, but SS does not know that pp. Thus SS believes that pp and qq. Either SS believes that pp, or SS fails to believe that pp. If SS fails to believe that pp, that by itself accounts for SS’s failure to know that pp. Call such a case irrational; it would also be a counter-example to the principle that belief distributes over conjunction. If SS does believe that pp, SS’s belief is true; SS’s failure to know that pp would need to be accounted for by the failure of some necessary condition for knowledge over and above that of true belief. Call such a case rational; with respect to the relevant propositions it would not violate the distribution of belief over conjunction.

The distribution of belief over conjunction seems to be neither necessary nor sufficient for the distribution of knowledge over conjunction. It seems to be unnecessary, because belief in the conjunct might follow from knowledge of the conjunction, although not from mere belief in it. Thus knowledge might distribute while belief failed to do so (perhaps failure to note certain immediate consequences is an obstacle to knowledge but not to belief). Conversely, the distribution of belief seems to be insufficient for the distribution of knowledge, since it rules out irrational counter-examples to the latter but not rational ones. Are there rational or irrational counter-examples to the claim that knowledge distributes over conjunction?

Robert Nozick’s theory of knowledge implies the possibility of rational counter-examples to the claim, as Nozick himself points out; it does not threaten the distribution of belief over conjunction in any way. According to Nozick, knowledge is a belief which tracks the truth. More precisely, SS knows that pp if and only if <1> it is true that pp, <2> SS believes that pp, <3> if it were false that pp then SS would not believe that pp and <4> if it were true that pp then SS would believe that pp. On this analysis, belief in a conjunction may track the truth while belief in a conjunct does not because ‘We can satisfy condition 3 for a conjunction by satisfying it for its most vulnerable conjunct, the one that would be false if the conjunction were false; it does not follow that we satisfy condition 3 for the other conjunct as well’.6,7 Nozick sometimes knows that he is in Emerson Hall and not floating in an Earth-simulator on Alpha Centauri, for if the conjunction had been false it would have been because he was somewhere mundane other than Emerson Hall, not because he was floating in a tank on Alpha Centauri, and he would not have believed the conjunction. However, at those times he does not know that he is not floating in an Earth-simulator on Alpha Centauri, for if that conjunct had been false he would still have believed it. Less dramatic illustrations of the same point are also possible. A malicious man could determinedly adopt the following policy: if his wife has been moving and talking in her sleep, tell her the next morning that she has been, but in any other case tell her that she has been moving. If she believes what he tells her, then on an unfudged version of Nozick’s analysis she may know that she has been moving and talking in her sleep without knowing that she has been moving in her sleep.

What of irrational counter-examples? It is easy to find cases in which a man would assent to a conjunction if queried when he would have dissented from (and not assented to) one conjunct if queried on that alone. For example, a man might answer ‘Yes’ if asked ‘Is it true that a city other than Rome was once the capital of Italy and Turin was such a city?’ when he would have answered ‘No’ if asked ‘Is it true that a city other than Rome was once the capital of Italy?’. He might have forgotten that Turin was the capital of Italy from 1861 to 1864, and need mention of it to jog his memory. If a disposition to heartfelt assent on being asked whether pp is sufficient for belief that pp, and a disposition to heartfelt dissent incompatible with it, then (if neither question is in fact being asked) our man believes that a city other than Rome was once the capital of Italy and Turin was such a city without believing that a city other than Rome was once the capital of Italy. Moreover, he knows the conjunction, having learnt it at school from a reliable teacher in the usual way. Yet he does not know the conjunct, for he does not believe it.8

Neither kind of case is terribly convincing. The rational cases might well be taken as a reductio ad absurdum of Nozick’s theory of knowledge, the irrational ones as a reductio of too close a link between belief and speech dispositions.

III

Can it be argued that knowledge does distribute over conjunction? It would be insufficient to argue that a subject will always make the relevant inference, for making an inference takes time, so that the subject might violate the claim before the inference is completed. If knowledge of the conjuncts results from knowledge of the conjunction, and the cause is not simultaneous with the effect, then there would be a period during which the subject knew the conjunction without knowing its conjuncts; that period would constitute a counter-example. Moreover, there is no form of inference which people can be relied on to carry out; distraction or sudden death is always liable to intervene. The required premise is precisely not that deductive inference is a way of extending knowledge. Rather, what would need to be shown is that knowledge of a conjunction is already knowledge of its conjuncts.

It might be suggested that one can know a conjunction only by inferring it from its conjuncts, and that the output of the inference is knowledge only if the inputs are, so that in order to know a conjunction one must already know its conjuncts. However, although inference from the premises pp and qq may in some sense be the canonical way of coming to know the conjunction p&qp \mathbin{\&} q, there are others: from testimony to the conjunction, or by inference from the premises (p&q)∨r(p \mathbin{\&} q) \vee r and ∼r\mathord{\sim} r, and so on.

Nevertheless, &\mathbin{\&}-Elimination has a rather special status. It may be brought out by a comparison with the ∨\vee-Introduction inference from a disjunct pp to the disjunction p∨qp \vee q. Although the validity of the inference is fundamental to the meaning of inclusive disjunction, a perfect logician who knows that pp may lack the concepts to grasp (understand) the other disjunct qq. Since knowledge of a proposition requires grasp of it, and grasp of a complex proposition requires grasp of its components, the subject is in no position to grasp p∨qp \vee q, and therefore does not know the disjunction. In contrast, those who know a conjunction grasp its conjuncts, for they grasp the conjunction.9 Moreover, they grasp the sense of its composition: they grasp the conjuncts as consequences of the conjunction. If they know the conjunction they grasp it, and in doing so grasp its conjuncts as its consequences. There is no obstacle here to the idea that knowledge of a conjunction constitutes knowledge of the conjuncts, just as, in mathematics, a proof of a conjunction counts as a proof of its conjuncts. If p&qp \mathbin{\&} q is proved then pp is proved, not just provable.

Someone might know a conjunction but believe one of its conjuncts for independent and bad reasons. The latter belief would not constitute knowledge. Nevertheless, the case is not a counter-example to what has just been said. For the person may be considered as having two beliefs in the conjunct, one constituted by their knowledge of (and therefore belief in) the conjunction, the other dependent on the bad reasons. The former counts as knowledge; the latter does not.

Unfortunately, no analysis of knowledge is well enough confirmed to act as an effective test of the claim that knowledge distributes over conjunction. In particular, any deduction of such a fine-grained principle is likely to be sensitive to small modifications of the analysis. The argument for distribution is suggestive, but no more. It is therefore prudent to seek ways of modifying the anti-verificationist argument so as to avoid commitment to the distribution of knowledge.

IV

The anti-verificationist argument is a reductio of (1) to (2), given the necessity of (3) (knowledge implies truth) and (4) (distribution). There is no formal argument from (1) to (2) given only the necessity of (3). However, there are two obvious strategies for reconstructing an anti-verificationist argument without appeal to (4). (a) One might argue that verificationists are committed to something stronger than (1), and deduce the absurd (2) from it. (b) One might deduce something weaker than (2) from (1), but argue that it is still absurd. Both strategies look promising.

(a) Consider the principle that if a conjunction holds, it is possible that each conjunct is known to hold:

(1a)

(p1&…&pn)→◊(Kp1&…&Kpn)(p_1 \mathbin{\&} \ldots \mathbin{\&} p_n) \rightarrow \lozenge(\mathrm{K}p_1 \mathbin{\&} \ldots \mathbin{\&} \mathrm{K}p_n).

It is hard to see what reason someone could have to assert (1) that would not also be a reason to assert (1a). For example, verificationists who use meaning-theoretic considerations to motivate principles such as (1) often rely on some concept of canonical verification, where verification yields knowledge and canonically verifying a conjunction involves verifying its conjuncts. Someone who endorses (1) on the grounds that any truth can be canonically verified is equally committed to (1a). But an instance of (1a) is (p&∼Kp)→◊(Kp&K∼Kp)(p \mathbin{\&} \mathord{\sim} \mathrm{K}p) \rightarrow \lozenge(\mathrm{K}p \mathbin{\&} \mathrm{K}\mathord{\sim} \mathrm{K}p). Given the necessity of (3), Kp&K∼Kp\mathrm{K}p \mathbin{\&} \mathrm{K}\mathord{\sim} \mathrm{K}p is impossible, and the argument proceeds as before. (1a) is reduced to the absurd (2); without (1a), (1) is unmotivated.

(b) Say that it is completely unknown that pp if no one ever knows a conjunction of which it is a conjunct that pp. No one can know that: pp and it is completely unknown that pp. For if someone did, it would not be completely unknown that pp. This reasoning relies on the necessity of (3) but not on (4). Thus (1) implies that there are no completely unknown truths. But that is absurd, for no one will ever know a conjunction of which it is a conjunct that the number of tennis balls in NN’s garden on 7th November, 1991 is even and no one will ever know a conjunction of which it is a conjunct that the number is odd; since one of those propositions about the number of balls is true, one of them is a completely unknown truth.

Formal versions of (a) and (b) are given in the appendix. Even if knowledge does not distribute over conjunction, the anti-verificationist argument can be reconstructed in at least two ways. The verificationist cannot escape by denying distribution.

V

Other operators can be substituted for K in the reductio of (1) to (2). Any veridical attitude that distributes over conjunction will do, although it must be checked that the analogue of (2) is genuinely implausible. Consider, for example, T-conception. One T-conceives that pp just in case pp and one conceives (i.e., grasps the proposition) that pp. T-conception is veridical by definition; it necessarily distributes over conjunction because both truth and conception do (if one conceives a conjunction, one conceives its conjuncts). The argument then shows that if all truths are T-conceivable, all truths are T-conceived. More specifically, if every truth conceivable by a human is T-conceivable at some time by a human, then every truth conceivable by a human is T-conceived at some time by a human. But surely not every truth conceivable by a human will ever be T-conceived by a human, just because not every truth conceivable by a human will ever be conceived by a human. Of course, I (being human) cannot give a counter-example; as soon as I gave it, it would be conceived, and therefore not a counter-example. I cannot even do what I could for (2): give a list of two or more propositions which I know to contain a counter-example. For no proposition I conceive is a counter-example to the principle that every truth conceivable by a human is at some time T-conceived by a human. Nevertheless, there are counter-examples, and I can gesture towards areas where some of them lie. For example, there are truths conceivable but never conceived by a human about the state of NN’s garden on 7th November, 1991; we all have better things to think about. By contraposition, there are truths that can be conceived by a human, but can never be true and conceived by one.

Appendix

There are two obvious ways of trying to approximate an operator O which lacks a certain feature FF by an operator with FF. One is to seek the weakest operator O+ stronger than O which has FF; the other is to seek the strongest operator O- weaker than O which has FF. Of course, there is no general guarantee that either O+ or O- exists. However, when FF is the feature of distributing over conjunction, both K+ and K- can now be defined in terms of K, granted a notion of propositions with conjunctive structure.

In what follows, every proposition is treated as a conjunct of itself, and every conjunct of a conjunct of a proposition as a conjunct of it. Put:

K+p=p = df For every conjunct qq of pp, Kq\mathrm{K}q.

K-p=p = df For some qq of which pp is a conjunct, Kq\mathrm{K}q.

K+pp entails Kp\mathrm{K}p, for pp is a conjunct of itself. K+ also distributes over conjunction, since every conjunct of a conjunct is a conjunct of the conjunction. If (4) is not necessary, K+ is stronger than K. However, K+ is the weakest operator stronger than K to distribute over conjunction; i.e., K+ is entailed by any operator O which entails K and distributes over conjunction. For suppose Op\mathrm{O}p; then Oq\mathrm{O}q for every conjunct qq of pp, by the distributivity of O; since O entails K, Kq\mathrm{K}q for every conjunct of pp; i.e., K+pp. Finally, K+ is veridical, since it entails the veridical K; its veridicality is necessary, for that of K is.

If K+ is substituted for K in the anti-verificationist argument, the result is a valid argument from the schema p→◊Kp \rightarrow \lozenge \mathrm{K}+pp to the schema p→Kp \rightarrow \mathrm{K}+pp. Since the latter entails (2), it must be rejected. Thus p→◊Kp \rightarrow \lozenge \mathrm{K}+pp must be rejected too.

Now consider K-. Kp\mathrm{K}p entails K-pp, for pp is a conjunct of itself. K- necessarily distributes over conjunction, just as K+ does. If (4) is not necessary, K- is weaker than K. However, K- is the strongest operator weaker than K to distribute over conjunction; i.e., K- entails any operator O which is entailed by K and distributes over conjunction. For suppose K-pp; i.e., Kq\mathrm{K}q for some qq of which pp is a conjunct; since K entails O, Oq\mathrm{O}q; since pp is a conjunct of qq and O distributes, Op\mathrm{O}p. Finally, K- is veridical, since if pp is a conjunct of qq, qq entails pp, so Kq\mathrm{K}q also does by the veridicality of K; its veridicality is necessary, for that of K is.

If K- is substituted for K in the anti-verificationist argument, the result is a valid argument from the schema p→◊Kp \rightarrow \lozenge \mathrm{K}-pp to the schema p→Kp \rightarrow \mathrm{K}-pp. Since (1) entails the former, it entails the latter. The latter is absurd, so (1) should be rejected.

The question is whether K+ and K- are well-defined. When pp has only finitely many conjuncts, K+pp can be defined without second-order quantification; the anti-verificationist argument does not require any proposition to have infinitely many conjuncts. In contrast, any proposition is a conjunct of infinitely many conjunctions, so the second-order quantification in the definition of K- is ineliminable. For some propositions pp, a problem of circularity may arise for K-pp. However, if K-pp is not always well-defined, it does not follow that it never is. It seems quite easy to grasp in the cases relevant to the anti-verificationist argument. Not all impredicativity is vicious.

Thus a simple-minded application of Nozick’s account seems to deliver K(p&∼Kp)\mathrm{K}(p \mathbin{\&} \mathord{\sim} \mathrm{K}p), and therefore ∼Kp\mathord{\sim} \mathrm{K}p. It may be felt that this result does more harm to Nozick than good to the verificationist. ↩︎

One might also consider a rather dim community whose members take several seconds to see the entailment from a given conjunction to one of its conjuncts; but do they know the conjunction before they know that it entails its conjuncts? Another objection, based on the idea that knowledge is implicitly typed according to a hierarchy of levels, is discussed at pp. 311-312 of ‘Two Incomplete Anti-Realist Modal Epistemic Logics’, op. cit. ↩︎

Fred Dretske’s endorsement, at p. 1009 of ‘Epistemic Operators’, The Journal of Philosophy 67 (1970) pp. 1007-1023, of the claim that knowledge of a conjunction entails knowledge of the conjuncts is compromised by his bracketing of it with the claim that knowledge of a disjunct entails knowledge of the disjunction. ↩︎
