# Bivalence and subjunctive conditionals

Timothy Williamson

Synthese 75 (1988): 405–421.

ABSTRACT. Writers such as Stalnaker and Dummett have argued that specific features of subjunctive conditional statements undermine the principle of bivalence. This paper is concerned with rebutting such claims. 1. It is shown how subjective conditionals pose a prima facie threat to bivalence, and how this threat can be dissolved by a distinction between the results of negating a subjective conditional and of negating its consequent. To make this distinction is to side with Lewis against Stalnaker in a dispute about possible worlds semantics for such conditionals, and reasons are given for doing so. 2. These arguments are extended to answer Dummett’s claim that behaviourist and phenomenalist analyses in terms of subjunctive conditions violate bivalence. This answer is shown to be compatible with the principle that hypothetical statements are true only in virtue of categorical facts.

Robert Stalnaker has argued that subjunctive conditional statements violate the principle of bivalence, which says that, of necessity, every well-defined statement is either true or false. In a different way, Michael Dummett has argued that certain statements, analyzed in terms of subjunctive conditionals, violate bivalence. This paper aims to show how, if we are careful to negate subjunctive conditionals correctly, we can answer both arguments.

# 1.

Had there been nothing but a gold or silver sphere, would it have been gold? Apparently, to answer “Yes” is to say that had there been nothing but a gold or silver sphere, it would have been gold; to answer “No” is to say that had there been nothing but a gold or silver sphere, it would not have been gold (and therefore would have been silver). Not only have we no reason to say either of these things, but it is hard to take seriously the supposition that one or other of them is, unbeknownst to us, true. Can subjunctive conditionals then fail to be either true or false, thereby falsifying the principle of bivalence?

There is an obvious way of dealing with this threat, as follows. The answer “Yes” to the original question is obviously unacceptable. To answer “no” is to deny that, had there been nothing but a gold or silver sphere, it would have been gold. That is to assert that it is not the case that, had there been nothing but a gold or silver sphere, it would have been gold. The argument does not require that there be a more idiomatic reformulation of this rather cumbersome assertion, but if one is required, a good candidate would be the assertion that, had there been nothing but a gold or silver sphere, it might not have been gold. However, none of this is to assert that, had there been nothing but a gold or silver sphere, it would not have been gold. For there is no reason why negating a subjunctive conditional should be equivalent to negating its consequent, although it is plausible that it is equivalent to changing “would” to “might” and then negating the consequent. We can also, of course, assert the triviality that, had there been nothing but a gold or silver sphere, it would have been either gold or not gold – for there is no reason why this conditional with a disjunctive consequent should entail the disjunction of unattractive conditionals, that either had there been nothing but a gold or silver sphere, it would have been gold or had there been nothing but a gold or silver sphere, it would not have been gold. Of these assertions, only the one to which the answer “No” does not commit us (had there been nothing but a gold or silver sphere, it would have been gold) is unacceptable. Hence we can reasonably answer “No”. But if it is not the case that had there been nothing but a gold or silver sphere it would have been gold, then the statement “Had there been nothing but a gold or silver sphere, it would have been gold” is false; since it is false, it is bivalent. In other words, the apparent threat to the principle of bivalence is diagnosed as resulting from a confusion between the scopes of negation and subjunctive conditional operator.

Such a treatment is suggested by the work of David Lewis on counterfactuals.1 Stalnaker has championed a rival view on which the above treatment would fail. Before we examine the bearing of their debate on the issue of bivalence, however, it is worth formalizing the original dilemma; for in doing so we shall uncover an unexpectedly elaborate network of assumptions about subjunctive conditionals that are of use in subsequent discussion.

Let us write the subjunctive conditional with antecedent $P$ and consequent $Q$ as $P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q$. ‘$\mathord{-}$’, ‘$\vee$’ and ‘&’ are negation, disjunction and conjunction respectively. $G$ is the statement that there is nothing but a gold sphere, $S$ the statement that there is nothing but a silver sphere. Thus the queried statement is $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to} G$. What needs to be explained first is why we find each of $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to} G$ and $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}G$ a silly supposition. Clearly, it is crucial that we regard the case as being symmetrical between $G$ and $S$; not only do we know of no reason why the solitary sphere should be gold rather than silver or vice versa, we believe that there is no reason to be known. In other words, we take the actual world to be neutral between $G$ and $S$. This assumption can be formulated as follows. Let $A$ be any statement made up entirely of $G$, $S$ and the above logical operators, and let $A(G//S)$ be the result of interchanging $G$ and $S$ throughout $A$ (for example, if $A$ is $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to} G$, then $A(G//S)$ is $(S\vee G)\mathbin{□\!\to} S$). Then we can endorse the following schema:

(a)
$A$ is true (in the actual world) if and only if $A(G//S)$ is true (in the actual world).

This symmetry does not yield a dilemma unless $G$ and $S$ are incompatible, for otherwise the supposition that the solitary sphere would be gold would not entail that it would not be silver, and thus would not produce an asymmetric preference for gold over silver. Thus we assume:

(b)
$G\mathbin{\&}S$ is not possible.

Finally, the antecedent of the supposition – that there is nothing but a gold or silver sphere – must be possible, otherwise its impossibility would be a sufficient explanation of our unhappiness with both $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to} G$ and $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}G$. So we assume:

(c)
$G\vee S$ is possible.

If the reader is not convinced of (a)–(c), there are several classic and less far-fetched examples which can plausibly be taken to have the same structure, to which the argument below can be applied. For example, Goodman uses “Jones is in South Carolina” and “Jones is in North Carolina” in place of $G$ and $S$.2 Similarly, Lewis considers “If Bizet and Verdi were compatriots, Bizet would be Italian”; thus we could take $G$ as “Bizet and Verdi are both Italian” and $S$ as “Bizet and Verdi are both French”.3

In order to construct a plausible argument from (a)–(c) to the conclusion that neither $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to} G$ nor $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}G$ is true, the following elementary assumptions about the logic of subjunctive conditionals are required. We state the schemata informally in order to avoid irrelevant commitment to particular formal systems.

(I)
If $Q_1,\ldots,Q_n$ logically entail $R$, then $P \mathbin{□\!\to} Q_1,\ldots,P \mathbin{□\!\to} Q_n$ logically entail $P \mathbin{□\!\to} R$.
(II)
If $P$ and $Q$ are logically equivalent, then $P \mathbin{□\!\to} R$ and $Q \mathbin{□\!\to} R$ are logically equivalent.
(III)
$P \mathbin{□\!\to} P$ is logically true.
(IV)
If $P \mathbin{□\!\to} Q$ is true and $P$ is possible, then $Q$ is possible.

(I)–(IV) all seem to be intuitively persuasive, and the corresponding formal inference rules are certainly validated by various standard semantics for counterfactuals and modal operators.4 (I)–(III) could all be restricted to the case when the antecedent $P$ is possible, but there is no obvious reason to do so.

We can now argue that $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to} G$ is not true, as follows. Suppose, for a contradiction, that $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to} G$ is true. Then, by (a), $(S\vee G)\mathbin{□\!\to} S$ is also true. Hence, by (II), $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to} S$ is true. Thus, by (I), $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to}(G\mathbin{\&}S)$ is true. (IV) then requires that if $G\vee S$ is possible, so is $G\mathbin{\&}S$, which contradicts (b) and (c). Then $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to} G$ is not true. By symmetrical reasoning, $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to} S$ is not true. Furthermore, suppose for a contradiction that $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}G$ is true. By (III), $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to}(G\vee S)$ is true. By (I), $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to} S$ is therefore true (for $\mathord{-}G$ and $G\vee S$ entail $S$); but we have already seen that is is not. Thus $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}G$ is not true either. By symmetrical reasoning, $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}S$ is not true. Note that all of (a)–(c) and (I)–(IV) were used in this reasoning.

By the previous paragraph, none of the statements $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to} G$, $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to} S$, $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}G$ and $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}S$ is true. Thus, for the defender of bivalence, all four are false. For them, the following assertions are therefore true: $\mathord{-}((G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to} G)$, $\mathord{-}((G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to} S)$, $\mathord{-}((G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}G)$ and $\mathord{-}((G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}S)$. This is precisely to formalize the treatment of the case informally proposed at the beginning. The scope of the negations is obviously crucial, since if $\mathord{-}((G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to} G)$ entailed $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}G$, the treatment would involve a contradiction. Note also that $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to}(G\vee\mathord{-}G)$ and $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to}(S\vee\mathord{-}S)$ can be unproblematically asserted; indeed, they can be shown to be true on the basis of (I) and (III) given classical logic.

These considerations can also be modeled within Lewis’s possible worlds semantics for counterfactuals.5 Roughly speaking, when $P$ is possible Lewis defines $P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q$ to be true at a world $w$ if and only if $P\mathbin{\&}Q$ is true at a world $w^\prime$ such that $P\mathbin{\&}\mathord{-}Q$ is not true at any world which is as similar to $w$ as $w^\prime$ is. It follows that $\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)$ and $P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q$ are by no means equivalent. If the most similar worlds to $w$ at which $P$ is true include some at which $Q$ is true and some at which it is not, or if for every world at which $P\mathbin{\&}Q$ is true there is a world more similar to $W$ at which $P\mathbin{\&}\mathord{-}Q$ is true and vice versa, then $\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)$ will be true and $P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q$ false at $w$; either of these cases could be used to model the symmetry of $G$ and $S$ (taking $P$ as $G\vee S$ and $Q$ as $G$). Lewis treats the remaining case of a counterfactual with an impossible antecedent as vacuously true, so in this case too $\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)$ and $P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q$ are not equivalent: the former is false and the latter true. Note also that any counterfactual of the form $P\mathbin{□\!\to}(Q\vee\mathord{-}Q)$ will be trivially true. Finally, it is not hard to check that Lewis’s semantics validates all of (I)–(IV), when they are formulated in the obvious way.

So far, everything has run smoothly for the defender of bivalence. However, Stalnaker has criticized Lewis’s system on precisely the point which is being used to defend bivalence, the distinction between $\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)$ and $P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q$; Stalnaker argues that “the normal way to contradict a counterfactual is to contradict the consequent, keeping the same antecedent”.6 His argument turns not on any technical feature of Lewis’s system but on the sheer appearance of contradiction in saying things like “Had there been nothing but a gold or silver sphere, it would have been either gold or not gold, but it is not the case that, had there been nothing but a gold or silver sphere, it would have been gold, nor is it the case that, had there been nothing but a gold or silver sphere, it would not have been gold”. Lewis himself admits that there is an appearance of contradiction.7

Stalnaker’s possible worlds semantics for counterfactuals virtually obliterates the distinction between $\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)$ and $P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q$; it is the main point on which he differs from Lewis, and – unfortunately for the defender of bivalence – it is one on which Stalnaker seems to be closer to our ordinary use of language. Technically, Stalnaker makes $P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q$ true at a world $w$ if and only if $Q$ is true at $f(P,w)$, which can be thought of as the most similar world to $w$ at which $P$ is true.8 $\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)$ and $P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q$ are equivalent because each is true at $w$ if and only if $Q$ is false at $f(P,w)$. There is just one exception – if $P$ is true at no world, Stalnaker takes $f(P,w)$ as an “absurd world” at which every statement is true; thus $(Q\mathbin{\&}\mathord{-}Q)\mathbin{□\!\to} Q$ and $(Q\mathbin{\&}\mathord{-}Q)\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q$ are both true, and therefore are not contradictories.9 We could, however, just as easily stipulate that if $P$ is impossible, $f(P,w)$ is $w$ itself. We have no firm intuitions about counterfactuals with impossible antecedents for this to violate, and it would make $\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)$ and $P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q$ equivalent in all cases.

Not only would Stalnaker’s semantics render self-contradictory the way of defending bivalence which we have suggested: it poses a direct threat to bivalence itself. This comes about as follows. It is not very plausible to assume that for every statement $P$ and possible world $w$ there is a unique world which is the most similar to $w$ at which $P$ is true; for example, the assumption seems to fail when $P$ is $G\vee S$ and $w$ is the actual world: if there is a world which is the most similar to the actual world of those in which there is nothing but a gold or silver sphere, is the sphere in that world gold or silver? Hence the function $f(P,w)$ does not seem to be well defined. This may look like a reason for preferring Lewis’s system – which does not require uniqueness – to Stalnaker’s.10 However, then $P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q$ would cease to be the contradictory of $P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q$. So Stalnaker, while agreeing that the structure of possible worlds does not single out a unique function $f(P,w)$, argues that we should treat this as a case of vagueness, i.e., it is indeterminate which function “$f(P,w)$” should be since there are many determinate functions which do the job equally well. Each such function gives an admissible bivalent valuation of counterfactual statements. Since it would be arbitrary to choose one of these valuations rather than another, Stalnaker combines them by Bas van Fraassen’s method of supervaluations: $P$ will be true at $w$ on the supervaluation if and only if it is true at $w$ on each admissible bivalent valuation; $P$ will be false at $w$ on the supervaluation if and only if it is false at $w$ on each admissible bivalent valuation; $P$ will be neither true nor false at $w$ on the supervaluation otherwise. Thus bivalence will not hold in general for counterfactuals.11 Consider again our example. Let $w$ be the actual world. Then, to avoid an implausible asymmetry, Stalnaker will allow that one admissible valuation will use a function $f$ such that $f(G\vee S,w)$ is a world at which $G$ is true, while another admissible valuation will use a function $f^\prime$ such that $f^\prime(G\vee S,w)$ is a world at which $S$ is true.12 On the former valuation, $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to} G$ will be true at $w$; on the latter, it will be false at $w$ (given (b)). Hence, on the final supervaluation, $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to} G$ will be neither true nor false at $w$, the actual world. Similarly, the supervaluation will make $(G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}G$ neither true nor false. In general, $P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q$ counts as neither true nor false when and only when $P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q$ does, for the biconditional $\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)\equiv(P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q)$ remains logically valid (the impact of supervaluation is on the semantics, not the logic, of counterfactuals). Similarly, $((G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to} G)\vee((G\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}G)$ is true on the supervaluation, even though neither disjunct is.

Thus if one allows $\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)$ and $P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q$ to be equivalent, a very natural line of thought leads to the abandonment of bivalence for such statements. Conversely, the defender of bivalence should not allow them to be equivalent, and thus must deal with Stalnaker’s arguments that they are. Lewis’s own reply to Stalnaker is that a natural and elegant analysis of conditionals of the form “If it had been that $P$, it might have been that $Q$” is as $\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q)$, but that if $P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q$ were equivalent to $\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)$, this analysis would make the “might” conditional equivalent to the corresponding “would” conditional, which would surely be wrong. Thus Lewis challenges Stalnaker to produce an analysis of “might” conditionals.13 Stalnaker, however, has produced such an analysis, which there is not space to evaluate here.14 Rather, I shall make some critical remarks about Stalnaker’s arguments for the equivalence of $\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)$ and $P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q$, and then give some arguments for their non-equivalence.

Stalnaker has certainly adduced evidence that we tend to treat the denial of $P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q$ as though it were equivalent to the assertion of $P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q$, but he does not show that this is not a fallacy that we tend to commit, rather than something constitutive of the truth conditions of our thoughts and sentences.15 For the upholder of bivalence can explain why we should tend to confuse $\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)$ with $P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q$. It is not just that human beings have a well-documented general tendency to make scope confusions; in this particular case something more interesting can be said. The natural English negation (“not”) attaches to a verb; since the main connective of a conditional in English (“if … then –”) does not contain a verb, we naturally attach the “not” to the main verb of the consequent, thereby making the confusion to be explained. The point of using the sentential operator “It is not the case that …” to negate conditionals is precisely that the main verb “is” falls outside the scope of “if … then –”, but we seek to avoid such constructions because they are so cumbersome.16 Thus Stalnaker’s appeal to linguistic intuition cannot prevent his opponent from distinguishing between $\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)$ and $P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q$.

However, the defender of bivalence would like to have some independent arguments for making this distinction. I shall suggest two. First, consider the following principle:

(V)
$P\mathbin{□\!\to}(Q\vee R)$ logically entails $(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)\vee(P\mathbin{□\!\to} R)$.17

We can show that anyone who thinks that $\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)$ is always equivalent to $P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q$ is committed to (V), given classical logic and the uncontroversail principle (I) above, as follows. Assume $\mathord{-}((P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)\vee(P\mathbin{□\!\to} R))$. This is equivalent to $\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)\mathbin{\&}\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to} R)$, which by hypothesis is equivalent to $(P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q)\mathbin{\&}(P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}R)$. Since $\mathord{-}Q$ and $\mathord{-}R$ entail $\mathord{-}(Q\vee R)$, $(P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q)\mathbin{\&}(P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}R)$ entails $P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}(Q\vee R)$ by (I). The latter is equivalent to $\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to}(Q\vee R))$ by hypothesis. Contrapositively, $P\mathbin{□\!\to}(Q\vee R)$ entails $(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)\vee(P\mathbin{□\!\to} R)$. As we should expect, Stalnaker’s system (but not Lewis’s) validates (V). However, (V) is not a very plausible principle. For example, it takes us from the trivial premise that, had there been nothing but a gold or silver sphere, there would have been nothing but a gold or silver sphere (which follows from (III)) to the implausible conclusion that either, had there been nothing but a gold or silver sphere, it would have been gold or, had there been nothing but a gold or silver sphere, it would have been silver. Stalnaker will say that the disjunction can be true even though neither disjunct is, but that reply is inadequate, for the disjunction itself is implausible. Moreover, the sense that (V) is implausible certainly does not depend on a prior commitment to bivalence, for Michael Dummett, who is no friend of bivalence, has objected to (V) – his example is that “If Fidel Castro were to meet President Carter he would either insult him or speak politely to him” does not entail “Either if Fidel Castro were to meet President Carter he would insult him or if Fidel Castro were to meet President Carter he would speak politely to him”.18

A second argument against the equivalence of $\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)$ and $P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q$ is as follows. It is not incoherent to deny determinism; after all, we do not know it to be true, and perhaps have scientific evidence that it is false. Yet the most natural articulation of an indeterministic account of the world would become self-contradictory were $\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)$ deemed equivalent to $P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q$. For example, suppose that a certain apparatus was not switched on at noon. Surely a respectable scientific theory can entail that nothing about the apparatus and its surroundings at noon determined whether, had it been switched on, it would have emitted a particle. The only natural way to express this entailment in terms of subjunctive conditionals is as the negation of the disjunction of $P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q$ and $P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q$, where $P$ is “The apparatus is switched on at noon” and $Q$ is “The apparatus emits a particle”. If $P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q$ were equivalent to $\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)$, this would be a contradiction; given that it is not a contradiction, $P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q$ is not equivalent to $\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)$.19 Note that it would be unsatisfying to substitute for $\mathord{-}((P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)\vee(P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q))$ some metalinguistic comment on the semantic status of $(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)$ and $(P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q)$ (for example, that neither is true on the supervaluation), for in an important sense the scientific theory is talking about the apparatus itself, not about our talk about it. Nor could the denial of $(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)\vee(P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q)$ be replaced by the mere refusal to assert it, for our aim is to express what the theory does say, not what it does not.

Thus there are good reasons to reject Stalnaker’s semantics for counterfactuals, so his argument against bivalence for them fails. Now observe that the second of the above arguments (unlike the first) remains valid when the background logic is taken to be intuitionistic. For the crucial point was that it should be consistent to assert $\mathord{-}((P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)\vee(P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q))$, which the equivalence of $\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)$ and $P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q$ would rule out. Intuitionistically, if $\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)$ is equivalent to $P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q$ then $\mathord{-}((P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)\vee(P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q))$ is equivalent to $\mathord{-}((P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)\vee\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q))$, which is intuitionistically inconsistent, for any statement of the form $\mathord{-}(R\vee\mathord{-}R)$ is as inconsistent intuitionistically as it is classically. Thus an upholder of bivalence who uses the distinction between $\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)$ and $P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q$ to defend themselves against an intuitionist opponent cannot simply on those grounds be accused of begging the question against the intuitionist. This point is of some importance to the second half of the paper, in which we consider a line of argument against bivalence, again turning on subjunctive conditionals, which Michael Dummett has proposed.

# 2.

Another well-known line of argument against the principle of bivalence has been advanced by Michael Dummett, in which subjunctive conditionals again figure largely.20 It is, of course, only one front in a complex assault on the realist doctrine – that the truth of sentences of our language can transcend our ability to recognize them as true – on which Dummett takes classical logic and semantics to rely.21 However, I shall not say much about this background. For there are local ways of resisting this element of Dummett’s attack on bivalence which do not raise every global issue. In particular, it has just been seen that to distinguish between $\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)$ and $P\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}Q$ is not tantamount to assuming realism; indeed, Dummett himself does not deny that such a distinction can be drawn.22 Hence, in what follows, that result of the previous section may legitimately be applied.

Dummett’s primary concern is with statements which someone wishes to analyze reductively in terms of subjunctive conditionals. For example, a phenomenalist might begin by analysing the material-object statement “$a$ is $\phi$” as “If $a$ had been in circumstances $C$, $a$ would have appeared $\phi$ly”. Similarly, a behaviourist might begin by analyzing the psychological statement “$a$ is $\phi$” as “If $a$ had been in circumstances $C$, $a$ would have acted $\phi$ly”. I shall not ask whether such analyses are in fact correct, but only whether they have the consequences which Dummett claims them to have; in each case, the antecedent and consequent certainly require further analysis, but that should not affect the overall conditional form.

To reconstruct Dummett’s argument, consider a class of statements which are to be analyzed as subjunctive conditionals: if $R$ is in the class, its analysis will be $A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} C(R)$. If the class is closed under negation, $\mathord{-}R$ will be analyzed as $A(\mathord{-}R) \mathbin{□\!\to} C(\mathord{-}R)$. Since we understand $\mathord{-}R$ by understanding $R$ and negation, $A(\mathord{-}R) \mathbin{□\!\to} C(\mathord{-}R)$ must somehow be composed from the material in $A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} C(R)$ and negation, and the only promising solution is to take $A(R)$ as $A(\mathord{-}R)$ and $\mathord{-}C(R)$ as $C(\mathord{-}R)$; thus $\mathord{-}R$ is analyzed as $A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} \mathord{-}C(R)$. To call $R$ false is, we may for present purposes assume, to call $\mathord{-}R$ true. Thus $R$ is bivalent if and only if either $A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} C(R)$ is true or $A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} \mathord{-}C(R)$ is true. Dummett argues that we often have no reason to accept the latter disjunction, and thus no reason to suppose that $R$ is bivalent. In particular, he suggests that an hypothetical statement can be true only if categorical facts make it true, and that it is implausible that there should always be either categorical facts to make $A(R)\mathbin{□\!\to} C(R)$ true or categorical facts to make $A(R)\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}C(R)$ true. Most poignantly, the very facts (such as Jones’s bravery or cowardice) which seem to have the best chance of deciding between $A(R)\mathbin{□\!\to} C(R)$ (“Had Jones been in danger, he would have acted bravely”) and $A(R)\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}C(R)$ (“Had Jones been in danger, he would not have acted bravely”), are those like $R$ (“Jones is brave”), and are therefore excluded by the analysis itself as being, despite appearances, not really categorical. I shall not develop Dummett’s argument on this last point, since I do not propose to criticize it and I have already accepted its conclusion – that it is not always the case that either $A(R)\mathbin{□\!\to} C(R)$ or $A(R)\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}C(R)$ is true. If that conclusion is false, subjunctive conditionals a fortiori do not undermine bivalence.

Given our previous discussion, the obvious reply to Dummett’s argument is as follows. The negation of $A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} C(R)$ is by definition $\mathord{-}(A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} C(R))$, and no reason has been given to suppose this equivalent to $A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} \mathord{-}C(R)$; we have already seen how they could have been confused with each other in spite of not being equivalent. This confusion led us mistakenly to suppose that the bivalence of $R$ required either $A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} C(R)$ or $A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} \mathord{-}C(R)$ to be true (Dummett calls this “strong bivalence”), which is indeed implausible; that bivalence loses its air of implausibility once we see that it requires only either $A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} C(R)$ or $\mathord{-}(A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} C(R)$ to be true (“weak bivalence”).23 Cases in which it is genuinely indeterminate what would have happened can now all be described consistently with the principle of bivalence after the following pattern. It is not the case that had Jones been in danger he would have acted bravely; it is not the case that had Jones been in danger, he would not have acted bravely – trivially, of course, had Jones been in danger, he would have either acted bravely or not acted bravely. In symbols $\mathord{-}(A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} C(R))$, $\mathord{-}(A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} \mathord{-}C(R))$ and $A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} (C(R) \vee \mathord{-}C(R))$ are true. $A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} C(R)$ and $A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} \mathord{-}C(R)$ are bivalent, because they are false. Thus the principle of bivalence is not threatened for the subjunctive conditionals themselves. $R$, however, has been mishandled. For the negation of the analysis of $R$ ($\mathord{-}(A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} C(R))$) is not equivalent to the analysis of the negation of $R$ ($A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} \mathord{-}C(R)$): the scheme of analysis is internally inconsistent and must be revised. More generally, the class of statements to be analyzed as individual subjunctive conditionals (not just as truth functions of subjunctive conditionals) must not be closed under negation (I assume that if any subjunctive conditional were the negation of $A(R)\mathbin{□\!\to} C(R)$, it would be $A(R)\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}C(R)$).

Thus reductionists have two alternatives. (i) They can take the apparent negation of $R$ to be its real negation. They must then analyze the apparent negation of $R$ as the negation of the analysis of $R$. If they analyze $R$ as a subjunctive conditional, they must analyze $\mathord{-}R$ not as a subjunctive conditional but as the negation of one. Since they have been given no reason to deny that either $A(R)\mathbin{□\!\to} C(R)$ or $\mathord{-}(A(R)\mathbin{□\!\to} C(R))$ is true, they can assume $R$ to be bivalent. (ii) They can take the apparent negation of $R$ ($nR$) not to be its real negation. Hence they can still analyze $R$ as $A(R)\mathbin{□\!\to} C(R)$ and $nR$ as $A(R)\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}C(R)$, but since the falsity of $R$ will not require the truth of $nR$, they can assume $R$ to be bivalent.

How does Dummett reply to this objection? He sometimes suggests that we might be able to recognize the falsity of $R$ as directly depending on the truth of $A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} \mathord{-}C(R)$ without considering what the negation of $R$ is.24 Now we can grasp that a statement might be false independently of whether it has a true negation, in a case in which it has no negation, or at least not one which there are the means to express, i.e., if the statement was made in some limited system of signs. But we have no reason to deny that $A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} C(R)$, the analysis of $R$, does have a negation, $\mathord{-}(A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} C(R))$, which we would not be justified in calling the negation of $R$ unless (at least) $\mathord{-}(A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} C(R))$ is true if and only if $A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} C(R)$, therefore $R$, is false. Of course, our identification of a statement as the negation of $R$ may depend on our prior grasp of what it takes for $R$ to be false, and if we had an overwhelming intuition that $R$ is false if and only if $A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} \mathord{-}C(R)$ is true, we should have to take $\mathord{-}(A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} C(R))$ as equivalent to $A(R) \mathbin{□\!\to} \mathord{-}C(R)$. I ignore this line of argument having no such intuition myself.

Dummett has considered positions for reductionists which resemble (i) and (ii), but his objections to them are unsatisfactory. He rejects (i) on the grounds that “it is plain that” in the cases with which they are concerned, reductionists will analyze all statements, positive and negative, in a given class into statements of the form $P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q$; but plainness, like or unlike beauty, is in the eye of the beholder.25 Dummett rejects (ii) on the grounds that it is integral to a realist account of the class of statements which includes $R$ that it should be closed under negation; but this requires an account of realism in which its acceptance for a given class of statements requires the application of a highly specific style of truth-conditional semantics to them; therefore the rejection of realism in this sense does not entail the rejection of bivalence for the given class.26

Whether or not reductionists accept the principle of bivalence, they are unlikely to feel bound by Dummett’s twin demands – that the given statements should all be analyzed into the form $P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q$, and that as a class they should be closed under negation. For they can argue as follows. The second demand has no obvious rationale except that the class should be closed under standard truth-functional operations; hence it should also be closed under disjunction – if the second demand is legitimate. Thus if $R$ and $S$ are in the class, so is $R\vee S$, and their analyses, if the first demand is legitimate, will be $A(R)\mathbin{□\!\to} C(R)$, $A(S)\mathbin{□\!\to} C(S)$ and $A(R\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to} C(R\vee S)$ respectively. Since we understand $R\vee S$ by understanding $R$, $S$, and disjunction, then $A(R\vee S)\mathbin{□\!\to} C(R\vee S)$ must somehow be composed from the material in $A(R)\mathbin{□\!\to} C(R)$, $A(S)\mathbin{□\!\to} C(S)$ and disjunction. We can assume $A(R)$ and $A(S)$ to be identical since this can only simplify the problem which I am about to argue to be insoluble. In particular, $A(R)$ and $A(S)$ have already been assumed to coincide when $S$ is $\mathord{-}R$. The only promising solution is to take $A(R)$ ($=A(S)$) as $A(R\vee S)$ and $C(R)\vee C(S)$ as $C(R\vee S)$; thus $R\vee S$ is analyzed as $A(R)\mathbin{□\!\to}(C(R)\vee C(S))$ (this is merely to transpose the argument for the analysis of $\mathord{-}R$ as $A(R)\mathbin{□\!\to}\mathord{-}C(R)$ from negation to disjunction). But then the problem arises that the analysis of a disjunction is not equivalent to the disjunction of the analyses of its disjuncts. For, as we saw, Dummett himself admits that the inference from $P\mathbin{□\!\to}(Q\vee R)$ to $(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)\vee(P\mathbin{□\!\to} R)$ ((V) above) is not generally valid (although its converse is), and his counterexample involves just the kind of conditionals which might occur in a behaviourist analysis of psychological statements (c.f. Fidel Castro). Indeed, Dummett’s critique of bivalence gives him good reason to reject (V), for a statement such as “Jones is acting bravely” is not itself supposed to be the source of the threat to bivalence, so that we can assert “Had Jones been in danger, he would have either acted bravely or not acted bravely”, from which (V) would allow us to infer what Dummett thinks we should not assert “Either had Jones been in danger he would have acted bravely or had Jones been in danger he would not have acted bravely”.27 Thus even reductionists who reject the principle of bivalence for statements in the given class should either deny that it is closed under disjunction or analyze disjunctions whose disjuncts are analyzed as subjunctive conditionals as disjunctions of subjunctive conditionals, not as individual subjunctive conditionals with disjunctive consequents. If at least one of these alternatives is acceptable for disjunction, why should the corresponding alternative ((i) or (ii)) not be acceptable for negation? But then the motive for rejecting the principle of bivalence disappears.

Dummett has one line of attack left. Consider the following plausible principle: a subjunctive conditional statement can be true or false only in virtue of categorical facts – that is, only if non-conditional facts make it true or make it false.28 The principle is less than crystalline, but can be clarified as much as present purposes demand; it could be denied, but I shall not deny it, preferring to resolve the tension between it and the assertion of bivalence for subjunctive conditionals that Dummett claims to discern. He presses the question: why suppose that there must be either categorical facts to make $P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q$ true or categorical facts to make it false? For if his opponent has no right to suppose this, then they also have no right, given the above principle, to suppose that $P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q$ must be either true or false.

My answer to Dummett’s question, relying on classical logic, ultimately defends bivalence. This does not beg the question against him, for Dummett’s aim is to bring to the surface internal tensions within the position of reductionists who say subjunctive conditionals are either made true by categorical facts or made false by them. If reductionists can resolve these purported tensions in their own position by use of the logic of that position, then Dummett’s strategy fails.

Reductionists can defend themselves as follows. Either there are categorical facts to make $P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q$ true or there are not (by the law of excluded middle). If there are, well and good. If there are not, then $P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q$ is made false (and $\mathord{-}(P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q)$ true) by the absence of such facts. Of course, “the absence of categorical facts to make $P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q$ true” need not denote an entity of a queer kind, an absence – it merely indicates that the statement “There are no categorical facts to make $P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q$ true” figures in the explanans of “$P\mathbin{□\!\to} Q$ is false”.29 For Dummett’s principle about conditionals and categoricals would lose its immediate claim to assent if it had to be read as ontologically committed to such things as facts. The above account certainly suggests that the explanation of the truth of a subjunctive conditional will differ in kind from the explanation of the falsity of one, but this should not occasion surprise. We already know that the explanation of the truth of an existential statement will differ quite similarly in kind from the explanation of the falsity of one; a particular $F$ which is $G$ can make “Some $F$ is $G$” true (indeed, this is a paradigm of a non-trivial explanation of the truth of a statement, which Dummett’s principle requires), whereas only the absence of $F$s which are $G$ may make it false.30 Thus the negative account of what makes a subjunctive conditional false merely exploits an apparatus to which anyone who recognizes the legitimacy of Dummett’s question will already be committed. It does not create new difficulties.

Now Dummett will doubtless have objections to the principle of bivalence for the underlying categorical statements, but we have seen that they will run along quite different lines from any which we have considered here; for example, how does our ability to conceive, of any particular categorical statement of some kind, that it is false give us the ability to conceive that all categorical statements of that kind are false? Our point has been that the non-existence of a right answer to questions about which of several possibilities would have been realized in a counterfactual situation poses no autonomous threat to the principle of bivalence.
