Anthropocentrism and truth

Timothy Williamson

Philosophia 17 (1) (1987): 33–53.

I

This paper argues that, intellectually speaking, one can bite off more than one can chew. More specifically: our thoughts need not be true only in virtue of what we can know. Thus I attack a form of anti-realism.1 However, such terms may cloud the issue, by seeming to confine it to the philosophy of language; and hardly anyone is sure what they mean. To bring out wider significances, I discuss thought rather than language; but would not object were speech and writing taken as forms of thought – even (most implausibly) as its only forms. I use the first person plural for studied ambiguity between collections of subjects and collective subjects: I need not care whether thought is formulated by private consciousness or speech community. I do however consider only thoughts formulated in space and time, which should soothe nominalist scruples. By such neutrality I hope to keep clear the links with both contemporary semantics and traditional debates on idealism.

Some opposing arguments are confined to language; for instance, those based on conditions for communication. My aim here is to show that they must harbour fallacies – not where these lie – by direct attack on their conclusion. The argument below is still valid if restricted to verbalized thought.

My strategy is to sketch a rejection of anthropocentric views of truth; this initially motivates a form of anti-realism, against which, further pursued, it finally turns.2 I do not mean to suggest that other arguments to the same conclusion – such as those which appeal to the inaccessibility of the past – are invalid; I simply want to bring out a particular kind of implausibility in anti-realism.

The argument also has consequences for the theory of truth. For instance, it suggests why the Tarskian relativization of truth to particular languages should be dropped, and how this does not threaten Tarskian solutions to the semantic paradoxes.

In what follows, the word ‘true’ will be used in its everyday sense, which obeys (although it is not necessarily defined by) the Tarskian biconditionals. Realists are sometimes accused of using the word ‘true’ in a special philosophers’ way which they have not shown to be coherent. Richard Rorty, for example, invites us to ‘consider the homely use of “true” to mean roughly “what you can defend against all comers”’,3 but his following argument that ‘true’ and ‘what you can defend against all comers’ do mean roughly the same in homely usage is that it is difficult to explain the difference between them to freshmen students; a similar argument would show that the difference between ‘accident’ and ‘mistake’ is a philosopher’s invention. Now anti-realism, as here understood, is the claim that the everyday concept of truth has a certain character, while realism — far from invoking a new sense of ‘true’ — is the claim that the everyday concept of truth does not have the character which the anti-realist claims it to have. Thus both sides can use the word ‘true’ in its ordinary sense without begging the question against each other.

II

Why should we not believe the man who says that man is the measure of all things? For much the reason we would not believe an economist explaining why the only escape from recession is to pay each economist a million pounds a year. Though not learned enough in economics to pinpoint his fallacy, we could be justifiably confident that he had made one. As Nietzsche shows, objections ad hominem may be below the belt, but they hurt. Where men claim a divine attribute for themselves we confidently diagnose, at best, wishful thinking. Omniscience is such an attribute, even when defined in terms of potential knowledge; I shall argue that anti-realism grants its defender this honour. Now here the supposed metaphysical privilage may be an unintended consequence of other claims, but chauvinism nowhere reveals itself more clearly than in what it takes as unproblematic (think of the phrase ‘professors and their wives’).

More soberly, homo sapiens is one possible species amongst many. The kinds of thought open to other species are surely constrained by the structure of their brains and what sense organs they have. Very likely, cats can think some simple thoughts but are physiologically incapable, without becoming a new species, of thinking about complex numbers. By parity of reasoning, we must assume that creatures can evolve whose thoughts could be as closed to us as ours are to cats. Even were language made a condition of thought, to claim that no creatures could speak a language in part unintelligible to us is to live in a pre-Darwinian world. Of these thoughts that no human could think, some would be true. In particular, if a thought is bivalent and humanly unthinkable, either it is true and humanly unthinkable or its negation is: for we can think a thought if we can think its negation. This is not to assume bivalence for undecidable thoughts: a humanly unthinkable thought could have a decision procedure verifying or falsifying it in a finite time and making it (almost) uncontroversially bivalent; but humans might not be able to grasp what the decision procedure was. Truth outruns human thinkability. A human constructivist would not admit that there are true humanly unthinkable thoughts since they could not specify one; but they should still deny the grossly implausible claim that no humanly unthinkable thoughts are true. Were they to concede it, they would also have to concede the even less plausible claim that no humanly unthinkable thought has a decision procedure. For given that if we can think ¬p\neg \mathrm{p} then we can think p\mathrm{p} and that decidable thoughts are bivalent, the claim about truth entails the claim about decidability.

The possibility of true humanly unthinkable thoughts may sound contentious. For instance, in ‘On the Very Idea of a Conceptual Scheme’, Davidson has argued for the incoherence of the hypothesis of creatures with a radically different conceptual scheme from “our own”; if we could not translate their supposed language into ours, what basis would we have for the claim that they were speaking, not just making noises? Now one might well object to what looks like an unsupported verificationist premise: that if we cannot know that they have a language, they cannot have a language.4 If this is Davidson’s premise, the anti-realism in question – that our thoughts are true only in virtue of what we can know — entails it: for we can think that they have a language – ‘language’ is our word – so, on that view, this can be so only in virtue of what we can know. Thus denial of the premise would anticipate the argument. One might regard Davidson’s implausible conclusion as already a reductio ad absurdum of his premise, but I shall not be so high-handed. For I can, for the sake of argument, accept his conclusion in the form: all possible natural languages have a large core roughly translatable into English.

This allows a language, henceforth to be called ‘Martian’, to contain many sentences in no way translatable into English. For instance, ‘AX’ might have no English equivalent, but if Martians also uttered ‘AY’, ‘BX’ and ‘BY’, where ‘BY’ did have an apparently equivalent English sentence, we could be justified in taking all four sequences as Martian sentences, if other such evidence pointed in the same direction. Of course, to group segments of different utterances together, perhaps on the basis of phenomenal similarity, as tokens of the same linguistic type is to make an hypothesis; but an amply justified one if it imposes some gross order on Martian noises. This observable integration of the translatable and untranslatable parts would be perfectly adequate evidence that they constituted one language. The incompatible replies, that the untranslatable parts, though syntactically integrated, must lack meaning or that their semantic integration must give enough clues for them to be translated, are mere arbitrary dogmas. Alternatively, the Martians might learn English and speak to us in it, assuring us that what they spoke amongst themselves was also a language. We might claim that they did not understand our word ‘language’ in our sense; but how hollow that claim would ring after what to all appearances was an intelligent conversation with a Martian about philology. The Martians might even tell us that only their more jejune expressions had English equivalents. For the rest, they would find us too obtuse and narrow-minded, too bound by the exigencies of human experience, to latch onto their English explanations of their more interesting thoughts. Now translation into one’s own language is not the only or best way of learning a new language: but our attempts with the direct method might fail too on most of the untranslatable part. Moreover if the Martians made many assertions with sentences we could not understand (they might tell us, on our translation, that these were assertions), by Davidson’s own principle of charity we should assume that many of the beliefs expressed, unthinkable by us, were true.

Imagine that human mathematicians have found a class of equations which, after prolonged effort, they are unable to solve. The Martians tell us that the concepts which they apply to such problems are far more sophisticated than those used to formulate the equations themselves. Given such an equation, they confidently write down strings of marks on paper and eventually produce a value which, human mathematicians confirm, is a root of the equation. The Martians try to teach us their concepts, but our best mathematicians cannot make head or tail of their explanations. Like untalented schoolchildren they are utterly bewildered by what their teacher says but justifiably confident that it is true. I conclude that Davidson’s argument allows us justified true belief that the Martians know many truths unkowable by us. My argument will require no more. He himself admits:

A language may contain simple predicates whose extensions are matched by no simple predicates, or even by any predicates at all, in some other language… We can be clear about breakdowns in translation when they are local enough, for a background of generally successful translation provides what is needed to make the failures intelligible.5

Indeed, whereas Davidson was principally denying that two groups may have disjoint systems of concepts, I am principally asserting that one system may be a proper subset of the other. His target raises the spectre of relativism: my view does not. Charity forbids the interpretation of Davidson as undercutting the rather obvious possibility of truths unthinkable by us that I have been emphasizing.

Someone might reply that Martians, like ourselves, are finite creatures: since most anti-realists allow that we can, in principle, decide whether a given enormous integer is prime or not, because beings like ourselves except for having a finely extended life span could do so, they should also allow that we can know what the Martians know because beings like ourselves except for having a finitely extended neurophysiological structure could do so. This move sublimely ignores the distinction between a method of discovery that we, as we actually are, sometimes use and one that we never do. We know how to decide whether an integer is prime; we do not know how to do Martian mathematics. ‘Know how to find out’ is a natural explication of ‘can in principle know’ and one which the intuitionists, for instance, adopt. Moreover, anti-realism as stated in terms of this concept of knowledge is a thesis with philosophical bite. In contrast, anti-realism as stated in terms of what finite extensions of us could do is not. For given any putatively unanswerable question, why could not a finite extension of a human be innately disposed to answer that question correctly? Since anti-realists understand truth in terms of knowledge and thus cannot understand knowledge in terms of antecedently understood truth, they are in no position to argue that such a creature’s belief would be too far from what it was true in virtue of to constitute knowledge.

Another line of reply would invoke Putnam’s ‘division of linguistic labour’, counting the Martians as part of our speech community on the basis of such communication as we can have with them and casting them as experts on the untranslatable heights of their language, just as scientists underpin lay use of ‘beech’ and ‘elm’.6 This involves a similar confusion. Putnam does not deny that lay people grasp the concept of an elm: he merely argues that they do so partly in virtue of what other members of their speech community know. Thus the word ‘elm’ can be used in correct attributions of propositional attitudes to them; someone who cannot tell an elm from a beech unaided can still know that elms are not beeches, not just that ‘elm’ and ‘beech’ have disjoint extensions. In contrast, we do not grasp some Martian concepts, which therefore cannot be used in correct attributions of knowledge to us. Someone who cannot grasp topological concepts cannot know that a compact subset of a Hausdorff space is closed, although they may know that the extension of ‘closed’ includes that of ‘compact subset of a Hausdorff space’. Putnam had to argue that natural kind terms lack intensions in the traditional sense; but at least some mathematical concepts have such intensions, which one must know to grasp the concept. Some of the Martian terms we cannot understand can be of this kind. Thus the division of linguistic labour, even if the anti-realist accepts it, is irrelevant to at least some of the cases around which my argument can be built; vicarious knowledge is not enough.

Someone might deny that to restrict truth to the humanly thinkable is anthropocentric, by claiming that truth is itself an anthropocentric concept. This would be like the economist saying that to demand a million pounds a year for himself and his colleagues is not economist-centred, since money is itself an economic concept. Truth is a human concept in the boring sense that we have a word for it, but the previous argument shows that it is not a human concept in the sense of applying only in more or less human contexts. To be a boy is to be, or to be very like, a young male human: we might say that boyhood, unlike rabbithood, is an anthropocentric concept; in this sense truth is not an anthropocentric concept. In a third sense rabbithood is an anthropocentric concept if, were our interests entirely different, we would have no use for it: even if truth were an anthropocentric concept in this third sense, that would not show that all truths are humanly thinkable, just as the anthropocentrism of the concept of a rabbit in this third sense would not show that all rabbits are humanly catchable. Moreover, the supposed set of interests with no place for a general concept of truth remains to be described. For whatever our interests, they will sometimes call for the recognition that some creature with which we are dealing is apprised of some truth as yet unknown to us but relevant to our interests. The only greater danger in philosophy than a sense of the obvious is lack of one. The envisaged situation with the Martians is obviously possible, and of this we must keep hold.

III

The admission that not all truths are thinkable by us has immediate impact on the theory of truth: we must deny that Tarski said it all. A Tarskian definition of truth for some given sentences can be given only in terms out of which those sentences or their translations can be formed. Since Martian contains sentences unthinkable by us, any Tarskian truth theory for it is equally unthinkable by us. I assume here that the unthinkability of Martian is not due solely to the syntactic complexity of its sentences: for we could understand a Tarskian truth definition for a simple first order language, some of whose sentences we could not understand in the sense that, since they contained a trillion quantifiers, we could not as it were hold their meaning before us. However, our superiority over cats is not just of this kind, otherwise all our thoughts could be formulated in terms of felinely graspable concepts, which is implausible for the concept of a barrister, say. Correspondingly, I assume that some Martian thoughts are beyond us for more than combinatorial reasons, so that we could not even think the individual clauses of a Tarskian truth definition for Martian.7

The Martians may assure us that they can give a Tarskian truth theory for any fragment of Martian in an extended fragment of Martian: but that theory is no part of our understanding of truth for Martian, since we could not grasp its clauses, we could grasp only that they were true — which makes the explanation, for us, viciously circular. No doubt it would eliminate some candidates on formal grounds, but, notoriously, many would remain: ‘“La neve è bianca” is true if and only if grass is green’ is not formally incorrect. Tarskian truth theories may be valuable where we have them; they are not the whole content of ‘true’. Where a Martian sentence s\mathrm{s} has an English translation, we will assert the standard disquotational equivalence ‘s\mathrm{s} is true if and only if P\mathrm{P}’; where it does not, we will not be alarmed. Perhaps, in raising the question of truth for Martian thoughts, we imagine thinking them – in a schematic way that does not entail being able to think them – and so being in a position to grasp a Tarskian truth theory for them: but that does not mean that we imagine ourselves thinking them, any more than when I imagine being Napoleon, I imagine myself being Napoleon (for I am Timothy Willliamson and in no possible circumstances is he Napoleon).8

Now a Tarskian might object that they can show, on independent grounds, that no truth predicate is universal: for does not the opposite assumption yield the semantic paradoxes? Truth must be relativized: it is an ‘indefinitely extendible’ concept. However, in the application of Tarskian hygiene to natural languages – a project in which Tarski himself showed little interest, but which is the present issue – two kinds of relativization must be distinguished. For truth must be relativized both to a particular natural language and to a particular level in the semantic hierarchy. These two relativizations can be separated; Tarski’s solution to the paradoxes requires only the relativization to a level i\mathrm{i} in the semantic hierarchy, while the objection to my argument requires the relativization to a natural language. For even if one accepts Tarski’s diagnosis that no language can contain its own truth predicate, one cannot deduce that the extension of each truth predicate must be restricted to the sentences of a single object language.

As usual, one divides and regiments English into a hierarchy, English(0), English(1), English(2),…, where English(0) contains no semantic terms (such as ‘truth’) and English(i+1\mathrm{i}+1) contains English(i\mathrm{i}) and semantic terms whose extension is restricted to English(i\mathrm{i}). Similarly we have Italian(0), Italian(1), Italian(2), … and so on for each natural language. But now let us depart from the Tarskian model and fuse English(i\mathrm{i}), Italian(i\mathrm{i}), etc., into an overall level i\mathrm{i}, for each i\mathrm{i}. These overall levels are not hybrid languages – in none of them can one say ‘“La neve è bianca” is true if and only if la neve è bianca’ — but they delimit possible extensions for the semantic terms of each regimented natural language. Thus, instead of restricting ‘true(0)’ to sentences of English(0) and ‘vero(0)’ to sentences of Italian(0), we would allow both to apply to sentences of level 0 from all natural languages, each being in effect the disjunction of the restricted level 0 truth predicates for each language. Their extensions would be identical rather than disjoint. Strictly speaking, the original regimentation allowed both ‘true(i\mathrm{i})-in-English’ and ‘true(i\mathrm{i})-in-Italian’ in English and ‘vero(i\mathrm{i})-in-inglese’ and ‘vero(i\mathrm{i})-in-italiano’ in Italian, but such qualifications are now anyway unnecessary. Without them, one returns to something less distant from actual ordinary language truth locutions. This hierarchy of truth predicates, each applicable to sentences of all natural languages below a given level, is already enough to block the paradoxes, for the usual Tarskian reason that ‘true(i\mathrm{i})’ does not apply to sentences containing ‘true(j\mathrm{j})’ or its equivalvents for any j≥i\mathrm{j} \ge \mathrm{i}.

Tarski might defend the relativization of truth to a language on the grounds that the same sounds might be used to say something true in one language and something false in another. However, this can hardly be a reason for relativization to a language, since exactly the same problem arises for ambiguity within a single language. In any case, the presence of indexical expressions in natural language requires us to attribute truth to sentences as spoken in particular contexts; this apparatus will normally allow us to select the appropriate truth conditions in cases of inter- or intra-linguistic ambiguity. Even if someone intends the same utterance in several ways at once, directed at a multilingual audience, we can handle it as we would an utterance which exploited intra-linguistic ambiguity. Of course, once the truth predicate is not restricted to one language, the set of relevant semantic primitives may no longer be finite, so that an explicit Tarskian definition becomes impossible. Since such definitions have already been argued not to give the content of ‘true’, we need not hamstring ourselves in the effort to facilitate them.

I shall disfigure what follows with indices for ‘true’, and other possibly related terms such as ‘know’, according to their level in the hierarchy of levels, only where absolutely necessary. The sceptical reader will find that they can be consistently inserted everywhere else, thus ensuring that none of the usual paradoxical inferences have been slipped in. I assume that I thereby also adhere to whatever restrictions less draconian solutions than Tarski’s would impose, for his prescription seems sufficient but scarcely necessary to avoid paradox (Kripke’s treatment of the paradoxes does not require the relativization of truth to levels). From more liberal viewpoints the argument should look cramped, not invalid.

When we de-relativize ‘true’, we do not assume that “all natural languages”, let alone “all possible natural languages”, form a “completed totality”, any more than we had previously assumed that all the sentences of a single natural language form a “completed totality”, for that would risk begging the question against the anti-realist.9 I shall not need to make universally quantified statements about all (possible) natural languages or about all the sentences of a single natural language as though these were finite collections whose members one could exhaustively enumerate. The quantified statements with which I shall be concerned will be existential rather than universal in form, and such statements do not raise the same problems for the anti-realist: just as the anti-realist can assert ‘This emerald is green’ and ‘Some emeralds are green’ without being able to survey all emeralds, so they can assert ‘This sentence is true’ and ‘Some sentences are true’ without being able to survey all sentences.

So far in this section I have followed Tarski in using ‘true’ as a predicate of sentences. However, once each ‘true(i\mathrm{i})’ applies to sentences of each natural language, the hierarchy is as much one of thoughts as of sentences: level 0, for instance, consisting of all thoughts not involving semantic concepts. Not only does this better fit the terminology of the rest of the paper: since the restiction of truth to verbalized thought is anyway artificial, we have another argument for the present approach.

The arguments of section II now extend naturally to show that not even truth(0) is confined to thoughts thinkable by us. For nothing suggested that the Martian thoughts we could not think had to involve semantic concepts: they might be awesomely sophisticated mathematics or physics, descriptions of experience based on sense modalities we lack, or of emotional nuances too subtle for us to catch. Thus, we must allow that truth(0) may be unthinkable by us.

IV

The Tarskian theory does not explain our coherent application of ‘true’ to Martian sentences. In this respect the redundancy theory seems little better. It offers the formula:

For some P\mathrm{P}, P\mathrm{P} and humans cannot think that P\mathrm{P}

If the quantifier is understood substitutionally—which may be the best way of avoiding certain forms of the paradoxes to which second order quantification brings one dangerously close10—this fails: for if the substitutions are confined to humanly thinkable sentences the formula comes out false, while if humanly unthinkable substitutes are allowed it is in effect a disjunction some of whose disjuncts we cannot think, and thus as a whole is humanly unthinkable – and to say that we can merely think that it is true is just to deny the redundancy theory. If the quantifier is not understood substitutionally, we can understand it only if we are told what its ‘range of variation’, in some sense is, and how can that be done without specification of a range of items? But then we must know what it is for an item to satisfy the open sentence ‘P\mathrm{P} and humans cannot think that P\mathrm{P}’, and the only answer seems to be that the item must be true and such that humans cannot think it, making the account circular. Upholders of the redundancy theory repeatedly claim to be using a form of quantification which is neither substitutional nor objectual. However, one might feel that they have not adequately clarified the meaning of their formulae, and could not do so without lapsing onto one horn or the other of the dilemma.

Michael Dummett has followed another line of attack, arguing that, if one wants to explain understanding of a sentence as knowledge of its truth conditions, one must reject the redundancy theory.11 Roughly, if ‘“grass is green” is true’ amounts only to ‘grass is green’, to know that ‘grass is green’ is true if and only if grass is green is only to know that grass is green if and only if grass is green: but knowledge of a tautology cannot amount to understanding of a sentence. So if understanding is knowledge of truth conditions, truth is not redundant.

I do not claim that these considerations are decisive; but they do motivate the search for a more substantive theory of truth, to explain what we are saying about a Martian thought when we say that it is, or might be, true. The obvious answer is that we are saying that it corresponds to the facts. However, two millenia of dicussion have contributed litttle to the elucidation of such phrases. If the facts are taken raw they seem incommensurable with the thought, while to cook them seems to make thought judge and jury in its own case. Anti-realism can be seen as an attempt to escape from this tangle while preserving the core of the correspondence theory intact, by thinking of ‘the facts’ as what we can know, not as what brutely is.12 For what we can know is both certainly so and commensurable with what we can think.

A crude form of anti-realism says that any thought can be true only in virtue of what we can know. The critique of anthropocentrism immediately rules this out. We would not say that our thoughts could be true only in virtue of what a cat could know; even if chimpanzees developed a simple language we would feel little inclination to say that our thoughts could be true only in virtue of what they could know. Why then should we say that Martian thoughts could be true only in virtue of what we could know? In certain cases a thought that we could not think might be true in virtue of what we could know: for instance, if we know p\mathrm{p} and cannot think q\mathrm{q}, where q\mathrm{q} is false, the disjunction p∨q\mathrm{p} \vee \mathrm{q} is true in virtue of what we can know, the truth of p\mathrm{p}, but is unthinkable by us, since one of its disjuncts is. Indeed, an infinite disjunction of thoughts that we can think might be unthinkable by us, yet be incapable of being true except in virtue of what we can know, since it could be true only in virtue of the truth of one of its disjuncts, each of which we might be in a position to know. However, the kinds of Martian thought previously suggested as being unthinkable by us – to do with sophisticated sciences or emotions, for instance – are not of this kind; to claim that they can be true only in virtue of what we can know seems just to be unacceptable anthropocentrism.

A weak form of ‘anti-realism’ says that any thought can be true only in virtue of what some possible being could know. This is almost vacuous, especially if omniscient beings are allowed. For any class of thoughts, why should not some being reliably intuit them as true just when they are true? The concept of truth seems to be determining the concept of a possible being, not vice versa. Indeed, Michael Dummett has written that the realist

might, and I think ought to, agree to the following weaker principle: that a statement cannot be true unless it is in principle capable of being known to be true… The fundamental difference between the anti-realist and the realist lies in this: that…the anti-realist interprets ‘capable of being known’ to mean ’capable of being known by us’, whereas the realist interprets it to mean ’capable of being known by some hypothetical being whose intellectual capacities and powers of observation may exceed our own’.13

This gift to the realist may be a Trojan horse,14 and it has been contested; but it is clearly not an anthropocentric view, and I shall not contest it here.

The form of anti-realism to be discussed here says that the thoughts of a subject (whether individual or group) can be true only in virtue of what that subject can know. Since truth has been relativized to levels, this view must be taken as a schema: for each i\mathrm{i}, the thoughts(i\mathrm{i}) of a subject can be true(i\mathrm{i}) only in virtue of what that subject can know(i\mathrm{i}). For Martian thoughts, to be true is to be true in virtue of what Martians, but perhaps not humans, can know: that is the content of our application of ‘true’ to their thoughts. This seems to do justice to our anti-anthropocentric considerations. Our cognitive powers limit what questions we can understand – to those whose answer, if they have one, we can find – but they do not limit reality, for they do not limit the true descriptions of it, which we cannot think, by creatures whose cognitive powers are greater than our own. How better to put man in his place? Anything said of him is said of every other species too. In Kantian terms, some facts are noumenal for us, but may be phenomenal for some other being.

Unfortunately, this defence tries to take a God’s eye view of why there is no God’s eye view. I shall argue that it entails a kind of generalized anthropocentrism which is no more plausible than that so far considered.

V

Imagine a Martian formulating a thought, call it p\mathrm{p}—the thought that P\mathrm{P}, say15 – by this I mean taking any rather explicit propositional attitude, of an event rather than state form, towards it: uttering it, entertaining it, suddenly wishing it to be true, or whatever. Suppose that p\mathrm{p} is true(0) – it contains no semantic terms – and unthinkable by us. ‘P\mathrm{P}’ is in sentential place, although by hypothesis no sentence we understand can replace it; but the Martians can supply such a sentence (unless p\mathrm{p} is ineffable). Martians, like us, are physical creatures, at least in the sense that their thoughts are formulated in space and time, or anyway we can index them by the place and time of the physiological event which corresponds to their occurrence. Let p\mathrm{p} be formulated at spatio-temporal location s\mathrm{s}, on some co-ordination of space and time expressible in English. Assume that no other thought is formulated at s\mathrm{s}. Thus p\mathrm{p} is the thought formulated at s\mathrm{s}. Now ‘the thought formulated at s\mathrm{s}’ is a definite description in English denoting p\mathrm{p}. Thus we can think the thought – call it q\mathrm{q} – that the thought formulated at s\mathrm{s} is true(0). Since the thought formulated at s\mathrm{s} is p\mathrm{p}, and p\mathrm{p} is true(0), q\mathrm{q} is true(1). Since q\mathrm{q} can be our true thought, on the anti-realism in question it can be true only in virtue of what we can know. That is, we are said to be able to know something in virtue of which the thought that the thought formulated at s\mathrm{s} is true(0) is true(1). The obvious candidate for that ‘something’ is: that the thought formulated at s\mathrm{s} is true(0). This may not be all we must know, but we must know at least this much if we know something in virtue of which the level 1 thought is true(1). Thus we can know that the thought formulated at s\mathrm{s} is true(0).

This does not entail that we can know that P\mathrm{P}, for even if we can know that the thought formulated at s\mathrm{s} is true, and although the thought formulated at s\mathrm{s} is the thought that P\mathrm{P}, this does not entail that we can know that the thought that P\mathrm{P} is true, since the replacement of one singular term by another with the same denotation notoriously fails to preserve truth in such epistemic contexts. In this case, we cannot even know that the thought formulated at s\mathrm{s} is the thought that P\mathrm{P}, for if we could know it we could think it and so think the thought that P\mathrm{P}, which is part of it; but we cannot think that P\mathrm{P}. Again, even if we can know that the thought formulated at s\mathrm{s} is true, we cannot know, of the thought formulated at s\mathrm{s}, that it is true.

I have here tacitly equated knowledge that P\mathrm{P} with knowledge that the thought that P\mathrm{P} is true. Given that the redundancy view has been rejected, these two items of knowledge may not be identical; but since on any view if one can think that P\mathrm{P} one can know that the thought that P\mathrm{P} is true if and only if P\mathrm{P}, to know that P\mathrm{P} is to be in a position to know that the thought that P\mathrm{P} is true and vice versa (if one has the concept of thought, which we do).

Given that knowledge of q\mathrm{q}, not of p\mathrm{p}, is in question, how is it to be achieved? We must be able to know that the thought formulated at s\mathrm{s} is true without being able to think the thought formulated at s\mathrm{s}. Now here anti-realism seems to have an answer. For p\mathrm{p} is a true Martian thought, so they can know something in virtue of which it is true. Why should we not sit back and watch them get to know it for us? That is, we could know that the Martians knew something in virtue of which the thought formulated at s\mathrm{s} was true. We also know that the Martians’ knowing something in virtue of which the thought formulated at s\mathrm{s} is true trivially entails that the thought formulated at s\mathrm{s} is true. So if potential knowledge is closed under known trivial entailment, we can know that the thought formulated at s\mathrm{s} is true. We can allow anti-realism to use a concept of potential knowledge on which this last inference is valid; it is surely entitled to whatever uncontoversially intelligible concepts it deems appropriate to itself. For this reason, we should not contest its understanding of an ability as not requiring that we can initiate its exercise at will – for our ability to know that the Martians can know something in virtue of which the thought formulated at s\mathrm{s} is true depends on their willingness to investigate the truth of what was formulated at s\mathrm{s}: they may not cooperate. In this sense, we can know something about the sun, even if doing so depends on the occurrence of an eclipse.

Other grounds for doubt intrude themselves. That we can perceive the Martians at all, let alone recognize them as thinkers, is already a large assumption: perhaps good grounds for suspicion. However, imperceptible Martians are perhaps too fanciful an hypothesis in which to find the reductio ad absurdum of a philosophy, so I shall not insist on them. Again, our observations of the Martians are unlikely to yield strictly sufficient conditions for the truth of their thought: we may misread them, they may mislead us and, even if we rightly think they think they know something, they may be wrong. However, fallibility does not exclude knowledge; the Martians might even assure us, in English, that they had ascertained what was formulated at s\mathrm{s} to be true, and testimony is commonly allowed to be a channel for knowledge. One might also invoke Peirce’s notion of the final outcome of inquiry here, and insist that, with however many mishaps on the way, the truth will out.

The central objection is more straightforward. Our thought q\mathrm{q} is supposed to be true only in virtue of what we can know. That is to say more than that we can know the truth of q\mathrm{q}: for it is also to say that, if the truth of q\mathrm{q} consists in the truth of some other thought r\mathrm{r}, we can know the truth of r\mathrm{r}. This is not in turn to say that the truth of a thought always consists in the truth of some other thought – which would open an at least dangerous regress – only that, where the truth of one thought consists in the truth of another, if we can know the first we can know the second. Now the idea of the truth of one thought “consisting in”, or being “in virtue of”, the truth of another, may not be wholly transparent, as the old debate over “negative facts” reminds us. If the idea turns out to be incoherent, so is the anti-realism under attack, which is stated in terms of it. So assume for the sake of argument that the idea is coherent.

The simplest case of the truth of one thought consisting in the truth of another is a disjunction with exactly one true disjunct: the truth of the whole consists in the truth of that part. In this respect existential thoughts look like infinite disjunctions: if Spencer Perceval was the only British Prime Minister to be assassinated, the truth of the thought that some British Prime Minister was assassinated consists in part in the truth of the thought that Spencer Perceval was assassinated. More generally, if only x\mathrm{x} is F\mathrm{F} and G\mathrm{G}, the truth of the thought that some F\mathrm{F} is G\mathrm{G} consists in the truth of the thought that x\mathrm{x} is F\mathrm{F} and G\mathrm{G}. As a natural extension: if x\mathrm{x} is uniquely F\mathrm{F}, the truth of the thought that the F\mathrm{F} is G\mathrm{G} consists in the truth of the thought that x\mathrm{x} is uniquely F\mathrm{F} and is G\mathrm{G} – at least where ‘the F\mathrm{F}’ is used attributively, to mean ‘the F\mathrm{F} (whatever it is)’. The truth of the thought that the only British Prime Minister to be assassinated was male consists in the truth of the thought that Spencer Perceval was the only British Prime Minister to be assassinated and was male. If these are not examples of the truth of one thought consisting in the truth of another, how are we supposed to get a grip on this idea at all?

Now the phrase ‘the thought that x\mathrm{x} is the F\mathrm{F} and is G\mathrm{G}’ is difficult. Is x\mathrm{x} supposed to enter the thought under some mode of identification or as it were naked? In either case, we must have some such mode in mind when we think the thought (whether different modes correspond to different thoughts that x\mathrm{x} is the F\mathrm{F} and is G\mathrm{G} or only one): which modes of identifying x\mathrm{x} give a/the thought that x\mathrm{x} is the F\mathrm{F} and is G\mathrm{G}? Definite descriptions seem not to help: the truth of the thought that the F\mathrm{F} is G\mathrm{G} does not consist in the truth of the thought that the H\mathrm{H} is the F\mathrm{F} and is G\mathrm{G}, for the latter is only a more complex thought of the same kind and still does not tell us that x\mathrm{x} is the H\mathrm{H} or the F\mathrm{F}. Luckily, general obscurity in the idea of identification does not infect the idea of identifying a thought, since a thought can be part of the mode in which another thought identifies it. Thus if the F\mathrm{F} is the thought that P\mathrm{P}, the truth of the thought that the F\mathrm{F} is G\mathrm{G} consists in the truth of the thought that the thought that P\mathrm{P} is the F\mathrm{F} and is G\mathrm{G}. In particular, since the thought formulated at s\mathrm{s} is the thought that P\mathrm{P}, the truth of the thought that the thought formulated at s\mathrm{s} is true consists in the truth of the thought that the thought that P\mathrm{P} is uniquely formulated at s\mathrm{s} and is true. So by the anti-realism under attack, since we can know the former we can know the latter. But we cannot know that the thought that P\mathrm{P} is uniquely formulated at s\mathrm{s} and is true, for we cannot think the thought that P\mathrm{P} and so cannot think any thought that contains it. This completes the reductio ad absurdum of the claim that our thoughts are true only in virtue of what we can know, which entails an anthropocentric denial of the obvious possibility of such Martians.

This anthropocentrism is of a peculiar kind: for it puts any other intelligent species at the centre too. Our potential omniscience (for all physically thinkable thoughts), given anti-realism, comes not because we are human but because we can grasp thoughts of the form: the thought formulated at s\mathrm{s} is true. Hence any other species that can grasp such thoughts is also potentially omniscient in the same sense. The privilege is not exclusive to man: but it is no more plausible for that. The economist may buy off his colleagues in philosophy by claiming a million pounds a year for them too: the ordinary man will be no more impressed. Anti-realism makes claims for us which are absurd given what we know of our limits: that it makes these claims for many other species too is no defence.

VI

A weaker claim might still be made: that our thoughts are true only if we can know them to be true. Here the anthropocentrism is less clear cut. We are still supposed to be able to know that the thought formulated at s\mathrm{s} is true, but perhaps solely on Martian testimony, with no requirement that we know that P\mathrm{P}. However, this weaker claim makes the link between truth and knowledge ad hoc in a way that the stronger claim did not. For the link is more plausible if the content of thoughts cannot be specified independently of the condition for them to be known: it is internal and organic, not external and imposed. In particular, a theory of what it is to grasp a thought, as the ability to know if it is true, would explain this otherwise puzzling link.

Now, presumably, we grasp a thought by grasping the concepts of which it is composed. In particular, we can conceive that there are F\mathrm{F}s by being able to conceive thoughts of the form: a\mathrm{a} is an F\mathrm{F}. If the grasp of a thought is the ability to know that it is true, if it is true, then the ability to know that there are F\mathrm{F}s must somehow depend on the ability to know particular things to be F\mathrm{F}s. The obvious way of meeting this requirement is the constructivist one: to define knowledge that there is an F\mathrm{F} as (potential) knowledge, of some x\mathrm{x}, that it is an F\mathrm{F}. That is, all knowledge of existence consists in the ability to produce an example: which is just the intuitionist semantics for the existential quantifier put in the material mode. Unfortunately, this leads straight to the strong form of anti-realism, for it entails that knowledge that some thought is true and uniquely formulated at s\mathrm{s} is, for some thought p\mathrm{p}, knowledge that p\mathrm{p} is true and uniquely formulated at s\mathrm{s} – which is just what we cannot have. The weaker anti-realism seems unable to rationalize itself by a theory of what it is to grasp a thought: for once our grasp is defined in terms of Martian abilities, the only likely conclusion is that our thoughts can be true only in virtue of what some being could think – and that is hardly anti-realism.

This paper has reproached anti-realism in somewhat mild terms: not because I think that nothing worse can be said of it, but because I have sought to explore the justice of the common claim that it is anthropocentric. The case turned out less straightforward than one might have imagined. In the upshot, anti-realism did make unreasonable claims for us: not because we are human, but because we have the concepts of thought and truth. Thus the appeal to modesty tells at the last against anti-realism. In the abstract, anti-realists can claim to be modest about what they can think (which is harshly constrained by what they can know) just as realists can claim to be modest about what they can know (which falls far short of what they can think). But anti-realists cannot be modest enough without denying their ability to grasp the concepts of thought and truth – an abnegation of the intellect which would leave their own claim unstatable. So grant realism that its opponent is so much epistemological obfuscation. What can realism say about truth for Martian?
