Some Admissible Rules in Nonnormal Modal Systems

Timothy Williamson

Notre Dame Journal of Formal Logic 34 (3) (1993): 378–400.

Abstract Epistemic logics for subjects of bounded rationality are in effect nonnormal modal logics. Admissible rules are of interest in such logics. However, the usual methods for establishing admissibility employ Kripke models and are therefore inappropriate for nonnormal logics. This paper extends syntactic methods for a variety of rules (e.g. the rule of disjunction) and nonnormal logics. In doing so it answers a question asked by Chellas and Segerberg.

1 Introduction The admissibility of a rule by a logic depends only on the logic’s set of theorems. It does not depend on a choice of semantics or proof system (for which reason the phrase “rule of proof” is not ideal; but see Humberstone [4]). However, the usual methods of proving the admissibility of a rule in modal logic are semantic; they use standard “possible worlds” model theory. This semantic treatment is applicable only to normal model systems (see below). Thus the usual methods do not allow one to prove the admissibility of a rule in a nonnormal modal system. The aim of this paper is to extend the use of syntactic methods for proving admissibility, methods applicable to both normal and nonnormal systems.

An important example is the rule of disjunction. A system SS provides (admits) this rule just in case for all formulas A1,…,AnA_1,\ldots,A_n:

if ⊢S□A1∨⋯∨□Anthen ⊢SAi for some i(1≤i≤n). \begin{aligned} \text{if }&\vdash_S□ A_1\vee\cdots\vee□ A_n\\ \text{then }&\vdash_S A_i\text{ for some }i\ (1\leq i\leq n). \end{aligned}

Lemmon and Scott established the rule of disjunction for a variety of modal systems by a model-theoretic technique that is now standard (Lemmon and Scott [9] pp. 44–46 and 79–81; Chellas [1] pp. 181–182; Hughes and Cresswell [3] pp. 96–100; see also Kripke [5], Lemmon [8], McKinsey and Tarski [10] and Segerberg [13]). Powerful though such techniques are, they are restricted to systems amenable to the model theory in question. Thus if a modal logic is nonnormal, because it lacks the rule of necessitation RN (if ⊢A\vdash A then ⊢□A\vdash□ A) or the K axiom schema (□(A→B)→(□A→□B)□(A\to B)\to(□ A\to□ B)), it cannot be shown in the standard way to admit the rule of disjunction. Such a limitation is serious for two reasons.

(a) If the admissibility of a rule is preserved when a normal system is weakened to a nonnormal one by the dropping of necessitation or the K schema (as is often the case), a model-theoretic technique obscures the generality of the phenomenon.

(b) The formalism of modal logic is often applied to epistemic or doxastic issues, with □□ read as “it is known that” or “it is believed that.” Normality is then equivalent to the closure of knowledge or belief under logical consequence: unbounded rationality. If one wishes to avoid this idealization, one will use a nonnormal logic. Even if one makes the idealization about individual subjects, but reads □□ as “someone knows that” or “someone believes that,” one may reject the schema (□A∧□B)→□(A∧B)(□ A\wedge□ B)\to□(A\wedge B) on the grounds that the perfect logicians who know or believe that AA may not include any of the perfect logicians who know or believe that BB, and normality is again lost. Yet one would still like to know what rules are admissible in the nonnormal epistemic or doxastic logic.

Standard “possible worlds” model theory can be generalized for systems without the rule of necessitation by the introduction of nonnormal worlds at which everything is possible. As a byproduct, Kripke showed that the nonnormal logics S2 and S3 admit the rule: if ⊢□A∨□B\vdash□ A\vee□ B, then either ⊢□A\vdash□ A or ⊢□B\vdash□ B (Kripke [6], p. 220). However, this semantics still enforces the K schema and the rule RM that if ⊢A→B\vdash A\to B then ⊢□A→□B\vdash□ A\to□ B. A much wider generalization is to neighbourhood semantics or minimal models, but this still enforces the rule RE that if ⊢A≡B\vdash A\equiv B then ⊢□A≡□B\vdash□ A\equiv□ B ([1] pp. 207–210; by definition, the classical logics of [13] admit RE). Similar remarks apply to the use of algebraic semantics (for a recent example of its application to problems of admissibility in modal logic see Rybakov [12]). Even this form of deductive closure is too strong for many epistemic and doxastic applications. It is avoided in the impossible worlds semantics of Rantala [11], but there the K schema is valid; yet real knowledge or belief cannot be assumed to be closed under modus ponens.

The proper response to the problem is not to seek further generalizations of the semantics, not least because anything general enough may be too trivial to be of use in establishing the admissibility of rules. It is more natural to develop a nonsemantic approach.

2 Framework The language is standard, with a countably infinite class of propositional variables p0,p1,p2,…p_0,p_1,p_2,\ldots; the only primitive operators are the 0-place ⊥\bot (falsity), the 1-place □□ and the 2-place →\to (material conditional). Other operators are treated as metalinguistic abbreviations, e.g. ¬A\neg A for A→⊥A\to\bot, ⊤\top for ¬⊥\neg\bot, ◇A◇ A for ¬□¬A\neg□\neg A. □0A=A□^0A=A; □i+1A=□i□A□^{i+1}A=□^i□ A. “AA”, “BB”, “CC”, … are metalinguistic variables over all wff; “pp”, “qq” and “rr” are metalinguistic variables over propositional variables. A function σ\sigma from wff to wff is a substitution iff σ⊥=⊥\sigma\bot=\bot, σ□A=□σA\sigma□ A=□\sigma A and σ(A→B)=σA→σB\sigma(A\to B)=\sigma A\to\sigma B for all AA and BB. If XX is a set of wff, σX={σA:A∈X}\sigma X=\{\sigma A:A\in X\}. The modal degree #A\#A of AA is defined as usual: #p=#⊥=0\#p=\#\bot=0; #(A→B)=𝑚𝑎𝑥{#A,#B}\#(A\to B)=\mathit{max}\{\#A,\#B\}; #□A=#A+1\#□ A=\#A+1.

A theory is a set of wff containing all classical truth-functional tautologies and closed under modus ponens (MP). A logic is a theory closed under the rule of uniform substitution (US). A subtheory (sublogic) of a theory is a subset of it that is a theory (logic). PC, the set of all classical truth-functional tautologies, is the smallest theory and the smallest logic. If SS is a theory, ⊢SA\vdash_S A just in case A∈SA\in S. If XX is a set of wff, X⊢SAX\vdash_S A just in case ⊢SB→A\vdash_S B\to A for some conjunction BB of members of XX. A logic is normal just in case it contains schema K and is closed under the rule of necessitation. The closure of a logic ℒ\mathcal L under a set of inference rules is the intersection of all logics containing ℒ\mathcal L that admit those rules.

A rule is treated as a sequent X⊩YX\Vdash Y, where XX and YY are sets of wff (written without { and }).

Definition 2.1 SS admits the rule X⊩YX\Vdash Y just in case for every substitution σ\sigma, if ⊢SσA\vdash_S\sigma A for all A∈XA\in X then ⊢SσB\vdash_S\sigma B for some B∈YB\in Y.

Thus the rule of necessitation RN is treated as a sequent p⊩□pp\Vdash □ p, the rule RE as p≡q⊩□p≡□qp\equiv q\Vdash □ p\equiv□ q, and Lemmon and Scott’s rule of disjunction (for fixed nn) as the sequent

□p1∨⋯∨□pn⊩p1,…,pn. □ p_1\vee\cdots\vee□ p_n\Vdash p_1,\ldots,p_n.

A special class of rules is of particular importance:

Definition 2.2 X⊩YX\Vdash Y is a □□-introduction rule just in case for some wff G1,…,GkG_1,\ldots,G_k and HH, Y={(⋀i≤k□G1)→□h}Y=\{(\bigwedge_{i\leq k}□ G_1)\to□^{h}\} and X⊢PC(⋀i≤kGi)→HX\vdash_{\mathrm{PC}}(\bigwedge_{i\leq k}G_i)\to H, where XX, GiG_i and HH are nonmodal (i.e., #A=0\#A=0 for A∈X∪{G1,…,Gk,H}A\in X\cup\{G_1,\ldots,G_k,H\}).

The special case k=0k=0 is allowed, where the rule is in effect X⊩□HX\Vdash □ H, and X⊢PCHX\vdash_{\mathrm{PC}}H. Some □□-introduction rules are:

p⊩□p p\Vdash □ p RN

p→q⊩□p→□q p\to q\Vdash □ p\to□ q RM

(p∧q)→r⊩(□p∧□q)→□r (p\wedge q)\to r\Vdash (□ p\wedge□ q)\to□ r RR

p≡q⊩□p→□q p\equiv q\Vdash □ p\to□ q RE

Note that a theory admits p≡q⊩□p→□qp\equiv q\Vdash □ p\to□ q just in case it admits p≡q⊩□p≡□qp\equiv q\Vdash □ p\equiv□ q. The rule of disjunction is obviously not a □□-introduction rule, even for n=1n=1; nor are p∨q⊩□p∨□qp\vee q\Vdash □ p\vee□ q and ¬p⊩¬□p\neg p\Vdash \neg□ p, for their conclusions are of the wrong form.

The case X={}X=\{\} is also allowed, where ⊢PC(⋀i≤kGi)→H\vdash_{\mathrm{PC}}(\bigwedge_{i\leq k}G_i)\to H. □□-introduction rules of this special kind will prove important, and deserve a special name:

Definition 2.3 X⊩YX\Vdash Y is an axiomatic □□-introduction rule just in case it is a □□-introduction rule and X={}X=\{\}.

Some axiomatic □□-introduction rules are:

⊩□⊤⊩(□(p→q)∧□p)→□q⊩(□p∧□q)→□(p∧q)⊩□(p∧q)→□p⊩□(p∧q)→□q \begin{array}{l} \Vdash □\top\\ \Vdash (□(p\to q)\wedge□ p)\to□ q\\ \Vdash (□ p\wedge□ q)\to□(p\wedge q)\\ \Vdash □(p\wedge q)\to□ p\\ \Vdash □(p\wedge q)\to□ q \end{array}

⊩□p\Vdash □ p is not an axiomatic □□-introduction rule, since not ⊢PCp\vdash_{\mathrm{PC}}p.

Proposition 2.4 A logic ℒ\mathcal L is normal just in case ℒ\mathcal L admits all □□-introduction rules.

Proof: If ℒ\mathcal L admits all □□-introduction rules, it admits RN and the rule ⊩(□(p→q)∧□p)→□q\Vdash (□(p\to q)\wedge□ p)\to□ q, so every instance of the K schema is a theorem; thus ℒ\mathcal L is normal. Conversely, let ℒ\mathcal L be normal, X⊩(⋀i≤k□Gi)→□HX\Vdash (\bigwedge_{i\leq k}□ G_i)\to□ H a □□-introduction rule, and σ\sigma a substitution. Suppose that ⊢ℒσA\vdash_{\mathcal L}\sigma A for all A∈XA\in X. By definition of a □□-introduction rule, X⊢PC(⋀i≤kGi)→HX\vdash_{\mathrm{PC}}(\bigwedge_{i\leq k}G_i)\to H, so σX⊢PCσ((⋀i≤kGi)→H)\sigma X\vdash_{\mathrm{PC}}\sigma((\bigwedge_{i\leq k}G_i)\to H). Hence ⊢ℒσ((⋀i≤kGi)→H)\vdash_{\mathcal L}\sigma((\bigwedge_{i\leq k}G_i)\to H), i.e. ⊢ℒ(⋀i≤kσGi)→σH\vdash_{\mathcal L}(\bigwedge_{i\leq k}\sigma G_i)\to\sigma H. Since ℒ\mathcal L is normal, ⊢ℒ(⋀i≤k□σGi)→□σH\vdash_{\mathcal L}(\bigwedge_{i\leq k}□\sigma G_i)\to□\sigma H, i.e. ⊢ℒσ((⋀i≤k□Gi)→□H)\vdash_{\mathcal L}\sigma((\bigwedge_{i\leq k}□ G_i)\to□ H). Thus ℒ\mathcal L admits the rule.

3 Cancellation rules The aim is to find ways of establishing the admissibility of rules generalizable to theories in which not all □□-introduction rules are admissible. Chellas gives an example of the kind of proof we are after ([1] pp. 124–125), in showing that the weakest normal logic K admits the rule

□p→□q⊩p→q. □ p\to□ q\Vdash p\to q.

In other words, for any wff AA and BB, if ⊢𝐊□A→□B\vdash_{{𝐊}}□ A\to□ B then ⊢𝐊A→B\vdash_{{𝐊}}A\to B (on an epistemic reading, this rule has a constructivist flavor: if knowledge of AA entails knowledge of BB then AA entails BB). Chellas uses a mapping τ\tau from wff to wff, defined as follows:

τpi=piτ⊥=⊥τ(A→B)=τA→τBτ□A=A \begin{aligned} \tau p_i&=p_i\\ \tau\bot&=\bot\\ \tau(A\to B)&=\tau A\to\tau B\\ \tau□ A&=A \end{aligned}

Consider an axiomatization of K with all truth-functional tautologies and all wff of the form □(A→B)→(□A→□B)□(A\to B)\to(□ A\to□ B) as the only axioms and MP and RN as the only primitive rules of inference (note that US is a derived rule). One can show by induction on nn that if ⊢𝐊A\vdash_{{𝐊}}A with a proof of nn lines then ⊢𝐊τA\vdash_{{𝐊}}\tau A, as follows. If AA is a truth-functional tautology, so is τA\tau A, for τ\tau commutes with all truth-functional operators. τ(□(A→B)→(□A→□B))=(A→B)→(A→B)\tau(□(A\to B)\to(□ A\to□ B))=(A\to B)\to(A\to B), a truth-functional tautology. Thus τ\tau maps axioms to axioms. Now assume the induction hypothesis for all proofs of less than nn lines, and that ⊢𝐊A\vdash_{{𝐊}}A with a proof of nn lines. If AA is an axiom we are done. If AA was derived by MP, then, for some BB, ⊢𝐊B\vdash_{{𝐊}}B and ⊢𝐊B→A\vdash_{{𝐊}}B\to A with proofs of less than nn lines; by induction hypothesis, ⊢𝐊τB\vdash_{{𝐊}}\tau B and ⊢𝐊τ(B→A)\vdash_{{𝐊}}\tau(B\to A), i.e. ⊢𝐊τB→τA\vdash_{{𝐊}}\tau B\to\tau A; by MP, ⊢𝐊τA\vdash_{{𝐊}}\tau A. If AA was derived by RN, then A=□BA=□ B for some BB such that ⊢𝐊B\vdash_{{𝐊}}B; but τA=τ□B=B\tau A=\tau□ B=B. This completes the induction. For any wff AA, if ⊢𝐊A\vdash_{{𝐊}}A then ⊢𝐊τA\vdash_{{𝐊}}\tau A; in brief, K is closed under τ\tau. Thus if ⊢𝐊□A→□B\vdash_{{𝐊}}□ A\to□ B then ⊢𝐊τ(□A→□B)\vdash_{{𝐊}}\tau(□ A\to□ B); but τ(□A→□B)=A→B\tau(□ A\to□ B)=A\to B. Hence K admits the rule □p→□q⊩p→q□ p\to□ q\Vdash p\to q.

The aim of this section is to make a detailed case study of the mapping τ\tau and its use in proving results of this kind. Later sections study other mappings. Two kinds of generalization of the result just proved are possible. We can generalize on the rule □p→□q⊩p→q□ p\to□ q\Vdash p\to q, and we can generalize on the system K. We begin with the former.

The closure of K under τ\tau implies that K also admits all of the following rules:

□p→◇q⊩p→q◇p→□q⊩p→q◇p→◇q⊩p→q□p≡□q⊩p≡q□p≡◇q⊩p≡q◇p≡◇q⊩p≡q□p⊩p◇p⊩p \begin{gathered} □ p\to◇ q\Vdash p\to q\\ ◇ p\to□ q\Vdash p\to q\\ ◇ p\to◇ q\Vdash p\to q\\ □ p\equiv□ q\Vdash p\equiv q\\ □ p\equiv◇ q\Vdash p\equiv q\\ ◇ p\equiv◇ q\Vdash p\equiv q\\ □ p\Vdash p\\ ◇ p\Vdash p \end{gathered}

The same proof technique works, for τ◇A=τ¬□¬A=¬¬A\tau◇ A=\tau\neg□\neg A=\neg\neg A, and it does not matter what truth-functional operator takes the place of →\to. Thus a general class of rules needs to be studied.

Definition 3.1 σ□\sigma_□ is the substitution such that σ□p=□p\sigma_□ p=□ p for every propositional variable pp.

Definition 3.2 X⊩YX\Vdash Y is a cancellation rule just in case X={σ□A}X=\{\sigma_□ A\} and Y={A}Y=\{A\} for some nonmodal wff AA (#A=0\#A=0).

Thus the following are cancellation rules: □p→□q⊩p→q□ p\to□ q\Vdash p\to q; □p≡□q⊩p≡q□ p\equiv□ q\Vdash p\equiv q, and □p⊩p□ p\Vdash p. □p→◇q⊩p→q□ p\to◇ q\Vdash p\to q, for example, is not a cancellation rule, but is admitted by a theory whenever the cancellation rule □p→¬□¬q⊩p→¬¬q□ p\to\neg□\neg q\Vdash p\to\neg\neg q is.

Proposition 3.3 If a theory is closed under τ\tau then it admits all cancellation rules.

Proof: Let SS be a theory closed under τ\tau, σ□A⊩A\sigma_□ A\Vdash A a cancellation rule and σ\sigma a substitution. What needs to be shown is that if ⊢Sσσ□A\vdash_S\sigma\sigma_□ A then ⊢SσA\vdash_S\sigma A. Suppose ⊢Sσσ□A\vdash_S\sigma\sigma_□ A. By hypothesis, ⊢Sτσσ□A\vdash_S\tau\sigma\sigma_□ A. Thus it suffices to show by induction on the complexity of AA that τσσ□A=σA\tau\sigma\sigma_□ A=\sigma A. Now τσσ□p=τσ□p=τ□σp=σp\tau\sigma\sigma_□ p=\tau\sigma□ p=\tau□\sigma p=\sigma p and τσσ□⊥=⊥=σ⊥\tau\sigma\sigma_□\bot=\bot=\sigma\bot. If τσσ□B=σB\tau\sigma\sigma_□ B=\sigma B and τσσ□C=σC\tau\sigma\sigma_□ C=\sigma C then τσσ□(B→C)=τσσ□B→τσσ□C=σB→σC=σ(B→C)\tau\sigma\sigma_□(B\to C)=\tau\sigma\sigma_□ B\to\tau\sigma\sigma_□ C=\sigma B\to\sigma C=\sigma(B\to C). This completes the induction; the case of □B□ B does not arise, for #A=0\#A=0 by definition of a cancellation rule.

We shall be concerned with proofs that a system admits all cancellation rules. Proposition 3.5 shows that not every such result can be proved as for K, by means of τ\tau. Proposition 3.4 is a lemma of general use, formulated relative to a particular choice of truth-functional primitives in the language.

Proposition 3.4 If θ\theta is a mapping from wff to wff such that θ⊥=⊥\theta\bot=\bot, and for all wff BB and CC, θ(B→C)=θB→θC\theta(B\to C)=\theta B\to\theta C, then PC\mathrm{PC} is closed under θ\theta.

Proof: If ⊢PCB\vdash_{\mathrm{PC}}B then B=σAB=\sigma A for some substitution σ\sigma and some wff AA such that #A=0\#A=0 and ⊢PCA\vdash_{\mathrm{PC}}A. Define σθ\sigma_\theta as the substitution such that σθpi=θσpi\sigma_\theta p_i=\theta\sigma p_i for all ii. One can then show that σθA=θσA\sigma_\theta A=\theta\sigma A by induction on the complexity of AA. Since PC is closed under US, ⊢PCσθA\vdash_{\mathrm{PC}}\sigma_\theta A, i.e. ⊢PCθσA\vdash_{\mathrm{PC}}\theta\sigma A, i.e. ⊢PCθB\vdash_{\mathrm{PC}}\theta B.

Proposition 3.5 Not every normal logic that admits all cancellation rules is closed under τ\tau.

Proof: Let ℒ\mathcal L be the smallest normal logic containing p≡□□pp\equiv□□ p. ℒ\mathcal L admits all cancellation rules but is not closed under τ\tau. A semantic proof of this uses standard correspondence theory: ℒ\mathcal L is the logic of a two-point frame in which each point is accessible from the other but not from itself, but τ(p≡□□p)=p≡□p\tau(p\equiv□□ p)=p\equiv□ p, which is not valid on that frame. However, in the spirit of this paper a syntactic proof of the same result will be sketched. One first shows that ⊢ℒ□(p→q)≡(□p→□q)\vdash_{\mathcal L}□(p\to q)\equiv(□ p\to□ q) and ⊢ℒ□⊥≡⊥\vdash_{\mathcal L}□\bot\equiv\bot. Then let σ□A⊩A\sigma_□ A\Vdash A be a cancellation rule and σ\sigma a substitution. By induction on the complexity of AA, ⊢ℒσ□A≡□A\vdash_{\mathcal L}\sigma_□ A\equiv□ A; hence ⊢ℒ□σσ□A≡σA\vdash_{\mathcal L}□\sigma\sigma_□ A\equiv\sigma A. Thus if ⊢ℒσσ□A\vdash_{\mathcal L}\sigma\sigma_□ A, by RN ⊢ℒ□σσ□A\vdash_{\mathcal L}□\sigma\sigma_□ A, so ⊢ℒσA\vdash_{\mathcal L}\sigma A. Thus ℒ\mathcal L admits the cancellation rule σ□A⊩A\sigma_□ A\Vdash A. To show that ℒ\mathcal L is not closed under τ\tau, let σπ\sigma_\pi be the substitution such that σπp2i=p2i+1\sigma_\pi p_{2i}=p_{2i+1} and σπp2i+1=p2i\sigma_\pi p_{2i+1}=p_{2i} for all ii, and define a mapping π\pi on wff by:

πpi=p2iπ⊥=⊥π(A→B)=πA→πBπ□A=σππA \begin{aligned} \pi p_i&=p_{2i}\\ \pi\bot&=\bot\\ \pi(A\to B)&=\pi A\to\pi B\\ \pi□ A&=\sigma_\pi\pi A \end{aligned}

Axiomatize ℒ\mathcal L with all theorems of PC and wff of the forms □(A→B)→(□A→□B)□(A\to B)\to(□ A\to□ B) and A≡□□AA\equiv□□ A as axioms and MP and RN as the only rules of inference. By induction on the length of proofs (using 3.4), if ⊢ℒA\vdash_{\mathcal L}A then ⊢PCπA\vdash_{\mathrm{PC}}\pi A (the converse also holds, but need not be proved). Finally, if ℒ\mathcal L is closed under τ\tau, ⊢ℒτ(p0≡□□p0)\vdash_{\mathcal L}\tau(p_0\equiv□□ p_0), i.e. ⊢ℒp0≡□p0\vdash_{\mathcal L}p_0\equiv□ p_0, so ⊢PCπ(p0≡□p0)\vdash_{\mathrm{PC}}\pi(p_0\equiv□ p_0), i.e. ⊢PCp0≡p1\vdash_{\mathrm{PC}}p_0\equiv p_1, which is absurd.

In spite of 3.5, there is a close connection between the mapping τ\tau and the admissibility of cancellation rules. A logic ℒ\mathcal L can admit all cancellation rules “because it is weak,” in the sense that every sublogic of ℒ\mathcal L (i.e. every logic all of whose theorems are theorems of ℒ\mathcal L) also admits all cancellation rules. This property of ℒ\mathcal L turns out to be equivalent to the property that τ\tau maps every theorem of ℒ\mathcal L to a theorem of PC. A strongest logic with this “weakness property” will be identified. Normal logics, such as K, lack this weakness property, for □□⊤□□\top is a theorem of K while τ□□⊤=□⊤\tau□□\top=□\top is not a theorem of PC; correspondingly, the weakest logic containing □□⊤□□\top is included in K but does not admit the cancellation rule □p⊩p□ p\Vdash p. However, it will be shown that if ℒ\mathcal L has the weakness property, then the closure of ℒ\mathcal L under any set of □□-introduction rules is closed under τ\tau and therefore admits all cancellation rules. K and a number of other normal logics are the closures of logics with the weakness property under the set of all □□-introduction rules. In this way we can achieve a wide generalization of the result about K.

Some preliminary lemmas are needed for the proof of the results just mentioned. In particular, it helps to consider wff without propositional variables, for if AA is any such wff, AA is the premise and τA\tau A the conclusion of an instance of a cancellation rule (see 3.9). Wff with propositional variables do not in general have this feature: for example, the proof of 3.5 shows that no cancellation rule has an instance of which p≡□□pp\equiv□□ p is the premise and τ(p≡□□p)=(p≡□p)\tau(p\equiv□□ p)=(p\equiv□ p) the conclusion. 3.8 (for which 3.6 and 3.7 are preliminaries) shows that if τ\tau maps a theorem of some logic to a nontheorem of PC, then it maps a theorem of that logic containing no propositional variables to a nontheorem of PC.

4 Cancellation rules in systems with T axioms It is natural to study nonnormal logics with the T axiom □p→p□ p\to p. For example, if □□ is read as “it is known that,” the T axiom should hold, since knowledge entails truth, yet ordinary knowledge is not closed under logical consequence. Logics with the T axiom are not in general closed under the mapping τ\tau, for although τ\tau maps the T axiom itself to the PC theorem p→pp\to p, τ\tau maps its instance □¬□p→¬□p□\neg□ p\to\neg□ p to ¬□p→¬p\neg□ p\to\neg p, whose addition to a logic with the T axiom yields modal collapse. Indeed, logics with the T axiom do not in general admit all cancellation rules. For example, we have ⊢𝐊𝐓¬□(p∧¬□p)\vdash_{{𝐊𝐓}}\neg□(p\wedge\neg□ p) but not ⊢𝐊𝐓¬(p∧¬□p)\vdash_{{𝐊𝐓}}\neg(p\wedge\neg□ p), so that KT does not admit the cancellation rule ¬□p⊩¬p\neg□ p\Vdash \neg p. Nevertheless, it is natural to ask whether logics with the T axiom admit modified forms of cancellation rules. This section gives a limited positive answer to that question.

We can adapt the mapping τ\tau to logics with the T axiom by combining it with the following mapping υ\upsilon:

6 Other rules Chellas and Segerberg [2] investigate what they call the MacIntosh rule:

p→□p⊩p,¬p. p\to□ p\Vdash p,\neg p.

The next proposition shows that 6.4 would be false if the qualification about RN were omitted.

Proposition 6.6 The closure of {¬□i⊥:i>0}\{\neg□^i\bot:i>0\} under RM\mathrm{RM} does not provide the MacIntosh rule.

Proof: Let the system be SS. Put ν□A=⊥\nu□ A=\bot for all AA, and let ν\nu commute with the other operators. It is easy to show by induction on the length of proofs in SS that if ⊢SA\vdash_S A then ⊢PCνA\vdash_{\mathrm{PC}}\nu A. Thus we cannot have ⊢S¬□¬⊥→⊥\vdash_S\neg□\neg\bot\to\bot; but ⊢S⊥→□⊥\vdash_S\bot\to□\bot.

Examples like those in 6.5 and 6.6 can be given to show the need for the qualifications to the propositions below. There is room for further investigation in this area. For example, the system SS in the proof of 6.5 does not provide the rule RE, since not ⊢S□⊥≡□¬⊤\vdash_S□\bot\equiv□\neg\top. Is there a set of □□-introduction rules including RN and RE under which the closure of {¬□i⊥:i>0}\{\neg□^i\bot:i>0\} does not provide both the rule of margins and the MacIntosh rule? In any case, some results are still obtainable when RM is weakened to RE.

Proposition 6.7 If SS is the closure of {¬□i⊥:i>0}\{\neg□^i\bot:i>0\} under some □□-introduction rules including RE\mathrm{RE} then SS admits the rule p≡□p⊩p,¬pp\equiv□ p\Vdash p,\neg p.

Proof: Suppose that SS admits RE and ⊢SA≡□A\vdash_S A\equiv□ A. By RE, ⊢S□iA≡□i+1A\vdash_S□^iA\equiv□^{i+1}A for all ii, so for all ii ⊢SA≡□iA\vdash_S A\equiv□^iA and ⊢S¬A∨□iA\vdash_S\neg A\vee□^iA, so ⊢S¬A\vdash_S\neg A or ⊢SA\vdash_S A by 6.2.

Proposition 6.8 If SS is the closure of {¬□i⊥:i>0}\{\neg□^i\bot:i>0\} under some □□-introduction rules including RE\mathrm{RE} and RN\mathrm{RN} then SS admits the rule p≡□p⊩◇p≡pp\equiv□ p\Vdash ◇ p\equiv p.

Proof: From 6.7 as 6.4 was proved from 6.3.

Proposition 6.9 The closure of {¬□⊥}\{\neg□\bot\} under some □□-introduction rules including RE\mathrm{RE} is the closure of {¬□i⊥:i>0}\{\neg□^i\bot:i>0\} under those rules.

Proof: It suffices to show that if SS admits RE and ⊢S¬□⊥\vdash_S\neg□\bot and ⊢S¬□i⊥\vdash_S\neg□^i\bot then ⊢S¬□i+1⊥\vdash_S\neg□^{i+1}\bot. But if ⊢S¬□i⊥\vdash_S\neg□^i\bot then ⊢S⊥≡□i⊥\vdash_S\bot\equiv□^i\bot, so by RE ⊢S□⊥≡□i+1⊥\vdash_S□\bot\equiv□^{i+1}\bot, so ⊢S¬□i+1⊥\vdash_S\neg□^{i+1}\bot iff ⊢S¬□⊥\vdash_S\neg□\bot.

Proposition 6.10 If ℒ\mathcal L is the closure of the D\mathrm{D} schema □A→◇A□ A\to◇ A under some □□-introduction rules including RM\mathrm{RM}, then there is a set of □□-introduction rules including those rules under which ℒ\mathcal L is the closure of {¬□i⊥:i>0}\{\neg□^i\bot:i>0\}.

Proof: It suffices to show that {¬□i⊥:i>0}\{\neg□^i\bot:i>0\} is a consequence of D by RM and that D is a consequence of {¬□i⊥:i>0}\{\neg□^i\bot:i>0\} by RM and the □□-introduction rule ⊩(□p∧□¬p)→□⊥\Vdash (□ p\wedge□\neg p)\to□\bot. The latter is trivial. For the former, suppose that SS admits RM and contains D. Since ⊢S⊥→¬⊥\vdash_S\bot\to\neg\bot, ⊢S□⊥→□¬⊥\vdash_S□\bot\to□\neg\bot by RM; but ⊢S□⊥→¬□¬⊥\vdash_S□\bot\to\neg□\neg\bot by D, so ⊢S¬□⊥\vdash_S\neg□\bot. The result follows by 6.9.

Proposition 6.9 allows {¬□i⊥:i>0}\{\neg□^i\bot:i>0\} to be replaced by {¬□⊥}\{\neg□\bot\} in propositions 6.3, 6.4, 6.6, 6.7 and 6.8 (recall that any theory providing RM also provides RE). Similarly, proposition 6.10 allows {¬□i⊥:i>0}\{\neg□^i\bot:i>0\} to be replaced by the D schema (i.e. the set of its instances) in propositions 6.3, and 6.4; it also gives modified versions of 6.1 and 6.2 in which SS is the closure of the D schema under some □□-introduction rules including RM.

Proposition 6.11 Let SS be axiomatized by all PC\mathrm{PC} theorems and the T\mathrm{T} schema with MP\mathrm{MP} and a set of □□-introduction rules including RE\mathrm{RE}. If ⊢SA0∨⋁1≤i≤k□j(i)Ai\vdash_S A_0\vee\bigvee_{1\leq i\leq k}□^{j(i)}A_i for some j(1),…,j(n)>#A0j(1),\ldots,j(n)>\#A_0, then ⊢SAi\vdash_S A_i for some ii (0≤i≤n0\leq i\leq n).

Proof: The strategy is a variant of that used for 6.1.

Claim (i): If ⊢SA\vdash_S A then ⊢SδS,0*A\vdash_S\delta^*_{S,0}A. Since δS,0*=δS*\delta^*_{S,0}=\delta^*_S, the argument is as for 5.5 (where RN was not used at the relevant points).

Claim (ii): For all mm, if ⊢SA\vdash_S A then ⊢SδS,m*A\vdash_S\delta^*_{S,m}A. We show this by induction on the length of proofs, assuming (i).

Proposition 6.12 If SS is the closure of the T\mathrm{T} schema under some □□-introduction rules including RE\mathrm{RE} then SS admits the alternative rule of disjunction.

Proof: Immediate from 6.11.

Proposition 6.13 If SS is the closure of the T\mathrm{T} schema under some □□-introduction rules including RE\mathrm{RE} then SS admits the rule of margins.

Proof: Suppose that ⊢SA→□A\vdash_S A\to□ A. By T, ⊢S□A→A\vdash_S□ A\to A, so ⊢SA≡□A\vdash_S A\equiv□ A. The proof then proceeds as for proposition 6.7, by appeal to 6.12 rather than to 6.2.

Proposition 6.14 If SS is the closure of T\mathrm{T} under some □□-introduction rules including RE\mathrm{RE} and RN\mathrm{RN} then SS admits the MacIntosh rule.

Proof: As for 6.4, by appeal to 6.13 rather than to 6.3.
