# Summary of Knowledge and its Limits

Timothy Williamson

Philosophical Books 45 (2004): 283–285.

Intelligent action is guided by awareness of the world. Although intelligent life does not consist solely of awareness, without awareness there is no intelligent life, and life is intelligent only insofar as it is related to awareness. Awareness is knowledge: to be aware of something that it has some property is to know of it that it has that property. Thus knowledge is central to intelligent life. The mental states of a creature are the states in virtue of which its life is intelligent. Consequently, the state of knowing is a central mental state.

That conclusion has been found paradoxical. Knowing is factive; one knows that something is the case only if it is indeed the case; otherwise one merely believes, and perhaps believes that one knows, that it is the case. Thus knowing is not a purely internal state. Whether one knows something about the external environment is not determined simply by how things are inside one; it also depends on how things are in the environment. Internalism in philosophy of mind is the view that mental states are purely internal. Internalists deny that knowing is a mental state. They regard it as a hybrid of purely internal mental states and states of the external environment, held together by their interrelations. For example, knowing that it is raining would consist, in part, of the mental state of believing that it is raining and the external condition of the environment, that it is raining. That suggests the programme of analysing knowledge as true belief plus further factors, such as justification.

Internalism has run into several kinds of trouble. Since Gettier’s refutation of the analysis of knowledge as justified true belief, one attempted analysis of knowledge after another has succumbed to counterexamples. The content even of mental states such as believing is not purely internal, as Twin-Earth thought experiments show. I argue for an externalist conception of the mind into which factive mental states, of which knowing is the most general, fit comfortably. In addition to the contents of mental states, the attitudes to those contents present another way in which mental states depend constitutively on the environment. This dependence allows them to play a full role in the explanation of intelligent action, especially action over an extended period with environmental feedback. Internal states would not be adequate substitutes. Furthermore, knowing is not reducible to a hybrid of internal and external factors. Rather, it is to be taken as basic and used to understand other mental states and cognitive phenomena.

We seem to have privileged access to our mental states, the ability to know without observation whether we are in a given mental state. But, when one is deceived, one fails to know without being in a position to know that one fails to know. I also argue that one sometimes knows without being in a position to know that one knows. Does that show that we lack privileged access to whether we know, and that knowing is therefore not a genuine mental state? An argument is constructed to show that virtually no state, mental or otherwise, is luminous in the sense that, whenever one is in it, one is in a position to know that one is in it. Thus virtually no mental state satisfies the strong privileged access constraint that one is always in a position to know without observation whether one is in it. That knowing does not satisfy it does not show that knowing is not a mental state. Mental states may typically satisfy a weaker privileged access constraint, that one is usually in a position to know without observation whether one is in them. But even knowing satisfies that weaker constraint.

Whatever evidence is, the anti-luminosity result applies to evidential states: one is not always in a position to know what one’s evidence is. This has disturbing implications for the theory of rationality, because one is not always in a position to proportion one’s beliefs to one’s evidence; rationality cannot be fully operationalised. The phenomenal conception of evidence, on which one’s evidence consists solely of present appearances to one, results from a futile attempt to operationalise rationality by identifying luminous evidential states. Leading sceptical arguments uncritically assume the phenomenal conception of evidence. Abandoning the aspiration to luminous evidential states as hopeless liberates one to adopt a conception of evidence much closer to scientific practice, on which one’s total evidence is simply the total content of one’s knowledge. To proportion one’s beliefs to one’s evidence is therefore to proportion one’s belief to one’s knowledge. Beliefs are justified by their relation to knowledge: knowledge can be used to explain justification, rather than vice versa, without circularity once knowledge is no longer supposed to be analysed as justified true belief plus further factors.

Knowledge is the norm of belief: a flat-out belief is fully justified if and only if it constitutes knowledge. Since the linguistic expression of a belief is an assertion, knowledge is also the norm of assertion. The speech act of assertion is characterised by the rule that one should assert that something is the case only if one knows that it is the case. If one merely appears to oneself to know, the belief and the assertion at best appear to one to satisfy their norms.

These ideas are capable of rigorous development within a formal framework that combines probability theory with possible worlds semantics for epistemic logic. The possible worlds capture the structure of the distinction between what is consistent with what one knows and what is not. The probabilities measure how far what one knows supports further propositions. This allows one to use the formal apparatus of Bayesianism while avoiding its epistemologically naïve subjectivism. The epistemic logic also enables one to explore Fitch’s so-called paradox of knowability, which is not really a paradox at all, except for those with old-fashioned verificationist presuppositions, but rather a significant result about the formal limits on what can be known.
