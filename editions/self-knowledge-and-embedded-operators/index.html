Self-knowledge and embedded operators

Timothy Williamson

Analysis 56 (4) (1996): 202–209.

Queen Anne is dead, and it is a fallacy to substitute a definite description for another designator of the same object in stating the content of someone’s propositional attitudes. The fallacy can take subtle forms, as when Gödel’s incompleteness theorems are used to argue against mechanistic views of mind. Some instances of the fallacy exemplify a more general logical phenomenon: the set of principles satisfied by one sentential operator can differ from, and even contradict, the set of principles satisfied by another sentential operator coextensive with the first. These notions will be formally defined. The phenomenon will be explored through a series of example, with particular attention to the misapplication of Gödel’s results.

According to the ‘KK’ principle, if one knows that something is so, then one knows that one knows that it is so. Although the principle, unqualified, is false, appropriately restricted or idealized versions of it may be true. What modes of presentation of oneself to oneself are relevant to such a qualified KK principle? Use the phrase ‘singular term’ broadly, for definite descriptions as well as names, demonstratives and pronouns, without prejudice to the question whether definite descriptions are quantifiers. For any singular term ‘t’, ‘t knows that …’ is therefore a sentence operator. Relative to a fixed context, say that ‘t’ fits the KK principle just in case for all P, if t knows that P, then t knows that t knows that P. Suppose (counterfactually) that in the context of its use by me ‘I’ fits the KK principle. Suppose also that, although I am in fact the man with a hole in his pocket, I do not know that I am the man with a hole in his pocket. In this context, does the definite description ‘the man with a hole in his pocket’ fit the KK principle? Suppose that the man with a hole in his pocket knows that it is sunny. Does it follow that the man with a hole in his pocket knows that the man with a hole in his pocket knows that it is sunny? It certainly follows that I know that it is sunny. Since ‘I’ fits the KK principle in this context, it follows that I know that I know that it is sunny. Thus the man with a hole in his pocket knows that I know that it is sunny. However, it does not follow that the man with a hole in his pocket knows that the man with a hole in his pocket knows that it is sunny, or that I know that the man with a hole in his pocket knows that it is sunny. For all I know, the man with a hole in his pocket is asleep and has no idea what the weather is like. Thus ‘the man with a hole in his pocket’ does not fit the KK principle in this context, even though in this context the definite description picks out the same individual as ‘I’ does, and ‘I’ fits the KK principle.

Even if an individual satisfies the KK principle in the intuitively intended sense, it does not follow that ‘t’ fits the KK principle whenever ‘t’ picks out that individual. One might stipulate that an individual x satisfies the KK principle just in case ‘I’ fits the KK principle when x is the speaker. This comes close to the intuitively intended sense of the principle. Sophisticated defenders of the KK principle entered just such qualifications, which its Cartesian spirit seems to demand. The failure of ‘t’ to fit the KK principle is not a failure of the KK principle itself when ‘t’ differs in content from ‘I’. For present purposes, the point is that, although the two sentence operators ‘I know that …’ and ‘The man with a hole in his trousers knows that …’ are coextensive in the present context, in the sense that one yields a truth when applied to a given sentence if and only if the other does, they do not satisfy the same principles; one satisfies the principle Op→OOp\mathrm{O}p \to \mathrm{O}\mathrm{O}p in place of ‘O\mathrm{O}’ and the other does not.

Switch from the subject of the propositional attitude to the attitude itself. On a cartoon version of the Stoic idea of wisdom, x is wise if and only if x believes only what x knows, i.e. for all P\mathrm{P}, if x believes that P\mathrm{P}, then x knows that P\mathrm{P}. Given the simplifying assumption that knowledge entails belief, the converse holds automatically. Now suppose that Socrates is wise. To ensure that the case is genuinely different from the previous one, assume that Socrates knows perfectly well that he is Socrates. The operators ‘Socrates knows that …’ and ‘Socrates believes that …’ are coextensive, but they need not satisfy the same principles. For example, Socrates knows that if Socrates knows that there is a form of mud then there is a form of mud, simply because Socrates knows that knowledge is factive, and applies that knowledge to the present case. It does not follow that Socrates believes that if Socrates believes that there is a form of mud then there is a form of mud. For although Socrates is wise, he may not believe that he is wise. More generally, the correctness of the schema O[Op→p]\mathrm{O}[\mathrm{O}p \to p] for the operator ‘Socrates knows that …’ in place of O\mathrm{O} does not imply its correctness for ‘Socrates believes that …’ in place of O\mathrm{O}, even granted the coextensiveness of the two operators.

Gödel’s second incompleteness theorem says that Peano Arithmetic (PA) – or any recursively axiomatizable system extending it – can prove its own consistency only at the price of inconsistency. Slightly more formally: if it is provable in PA that it is not provable in PA that 0=10=1 then it is provable in PA that 0=10=1. But this formulation still has one occurrence of the informal sentence operator ‘It is provable in PA that …’ inside the scope of another; it therefore treats the operator as coding something in the language of PA, whose intended interpretation concerns numbers, not proofs. Gödel codes each formula α\mathrm{\alpha} of LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}}, the language of PA, by a numeral ⌜α⌝\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner of LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}}, and provability in PA by a formula Bew⁡(x)\operatorname{Bew}(x) of LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}}. Thus the informal sentence operator is formalized by Bew⁡(⌜…⌝)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner) in LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}}. The second incompleteness theorem says that PA proves ¬Bew⁡(⌜0=1⌝)\neg\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner) only if PA proves 0=10=1.

Gödel could have used a different coding. What features of his coding justify the informal reading of Bew⁡(⌜…⌝)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner) as ‘It is provable in PA that …’? If LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}} had no intended interpretation, the informal reading would be hard to justify. But LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}} does have an intended interpretation, on which a sentence α\mathrm{\alpha} is true if and only if it is true in all standard models of PA. Since PA has standard models, and they are all isomorphic, exactly one of α\mathrm{\alpha} and ¬α\neg\mathrm{\alpha} is true. Since we have the notion of truth for LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}}, we can stipulate that a one-place sentential operator O\mathrm{O} in LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}} is a provability operator for PA just in case for each sentence α\mathrm{\alpha} of LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}}, Oα\mathrm{O}\mathrm{\alpha} is true if and only if PA proves α\mathrm{\alpha}. Bew⁡(⌜…⌝)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner) is a provability operator for PA in this sense.

Any two provability operators O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 for PA are coextensive: O1α↔O2α\mathrm{O}_1\mathrm{\alpha} \leftrightarrow \mathrm{O}_2\mathrm{\alpha} is true for each sentence α\mathrm{\alpha} of LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}}. But it does not follow that PA proves the biconditionals. The unprovability in PA of ¬Bew⁡(⌜0=1⌝)\neg\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner) does not imply the unprovability in PA of ¬O(0=1)\neg \mathrm{O}(0=1) for every provability operator O\mathrm{O} for PA (an example figures below). Coextensive provability operators satisfy different principles in PA.

One might hope to exclude unwanted provability operators by analysing ‘It is provable that …’ as the existential generalization of ‘x is a proof that …’. Since the latter notion is effectively decidable, it can be subjected to tighter controls. More specifically, say that Pf⁡[…](x)\operatorname{Pf}[\ldots](x) is a proof operator for PA just in case there is an effective procedure for assigning to each sentence α\mathrm{\alpha} of LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}} a formula Pf⁡[α](x)\operatorname{Pf}[\mathrm{\alpha}](x) of LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}} and coding each proof in an axiomatization of PA by a natural number such that for each natural number nn, if nn codes a proof of α\mathrm{\alpha} then PA proves Pf⁡[α](𝒏)\operatorname{Pf}[\mathrm{\alpha}](\boldsymbol{n}) (where 𝒏\boldsymbol{n} is the LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}} numeral for nn) and otherwise PA proves ¬Pf⁡[α](𝒏)\neg\operatorname{Pf}[\mathrm{\alpha}](\boldsymbol{n}). The operator of which Bew⁡(⌜…⌝)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner) is the existential generalization is a proof operator in this sense. The definition can be shown to imply that the existential generalization of a proof operator is a provability operator.

It turns out that not even the existential generalization of a proof operator need satisfy the same principles as Bew⁡(⌜…⌝)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner). For example, let Bew⁡(y)=∃xB(y,x)\operatorname{Bew}(y)=\exists x\,\mathrm{B}(y,x). Consider the operator B(⌜…⌝,x)∧…\mathrm{B}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner,x)\wedge\ldots. It is a proof operator: if nn codes a proof of α\mathrm{\alpha} in PA then PA proves B(⌜α⌝,𝒏)∧α\mathrm{B}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner,\boldsymbol{n})\wedge\mathrm{\alpha}; otherwise PA proves ¬(B(⌜α⌝,𝒏)∧α)\neg(\mathrm{B}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner,\boldsymbol{n})\wedge\mathrm{\alpha}). Now define a provability operator O+\mathrm{O}^+ as the existential generalization of that proof operator:

O+α=df∃x(B(⌜α⌝,x)∧α)\mathrm{O}^+\mathrm{\alpha} =_{\mathrm{df}} \exists x\,(\mathrm{B}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner,x)\wedge\mathrm{\alpha})

O+α\mathrm{O}^+\mathrm{\alpha} is equivalent in PA to Bew⁡(⌜α⌝)∧α\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner)\wedge\mathrm{\alpha}. Trivially, PA proves ¬O+(0=1)\neg \mathrm{O}^+(0=1). Thus the second incompleteness theorem does not apply to O+\mathrm{O}^+, even though O+\mathrm{O}^+ is the existential generalization of a proof operator. If ⊥\bot is a contradiction, O+\mathrm{O}^+ satisfies the principle O¬O⊥\mathrm{O}\neg \mathrm{O}\bot, which the coextensive operator Bew⁡(⌜…⌝)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner) does not satisfy.

The existence of deviant metamathematical codings was recognized early on, by Kreisel, Takeuti and Feferman. These days, the standard response is an appeal to the so-called Hilbert–Bernays–Löb derivability conditions. They are conditions on a predicate (contrast: operator) F\mathrm{F} and theory S\mathrm{S} in a language L\mathrm{L}, given a quotation functor ⌜…⌝\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner in L\mathrm{L} from sentences to singular terms. They state that for all sentences α\mathrm{\alpha} and β\mathrm{\beta} of L\mathrm{L}:

(1)
If S\mathrm{S} proves α\mathrm{\alpha} then S\mathrm{S} proves F(⌜α⌝)\mathrm{F}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner).
(2)
S\mathrm{S} proves F(⌜α→β⌝)→(F(⌜α⌝)→F(⌜β⌝))\mathrm{F}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\to\mathrm{\beta}\urcorner)\to(\mathrm{F}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner)\to \mathrm{F}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\beta}\urcorner)).
(3)
S\mathrm{S} proves F(⌜α⌝)→F(⌜F(⌜α⌝)⌝)\mathrm{F}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner)\to \mathrm{F}(\ulcorner \mathrm{F}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner)\urcorner).

If S\mathrm{S} satisfies (1) – (3) and elementary logic and syntax can be embedded in S\mathrm{S}, then S\mathrm{S} proves ¬F(⌜⊥⌝)\neg \mathrm{F}(\ulcorner\bot\urcorner) only if S\mathrm{S} proves ⊥\bot.

What (1) – (3) do not provide are general sufficient conditions on an arbitrary sentential operator O\mathrm{O} for the derivability of the second incompleteness theorem. One cannot simply replace the operator F(⌜…⌝)\mathrm{F}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner) by O…\mathrm{O}\ldots in (1) – (3), requiring that for all sentences α\mathrm{\alpha} and β\mathrm{\beta} of L\mathrm{L}:

(1′)
If S\mathrm{S} proves α\mathrm{\alpha} then S\mathrm{S} proves Oα\mathrm{O}\mathrm{\alpha}.
(2′)
S\mathrm{S} proves O(α→β)→(Oα→Oβ)\mathrm{O}(\mathrm{\alpha}\to\mathrm{\beta})\to(\mathrm{O}\mathrm{\alpha}\to \mathrm{O}\mathrm{\beta}).
(3′)
S\mathrm{S} proves Oα→OOα\mathrm{O}\mathrm{\alpha}\to \mathrm{O}\mathrm{O}\mathrm{\alpha}.

For it is not the case that whenever (1′) – (3′) hold and S\mathrm{S} embeds elementary logic and syntax, S\mathrm{S} proves ¬O⊥\neg \mathrm{O}\bot only if S\mathrm{S} proves ⊥\bot. Even when S\mathrm{S} is PA and O…\mathrm{O}\ldots is coextensive with Bew⁡(⌜…⌝)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner), (1′) – (3′) are not sufficient for O…\mathrm{O}\ldots to satisfy the second incompleteness theorem. The operator O+\mathrm{O}^+ defined above is a counterexample. It can easily be shown to satisfy (1′) – (3′), and PA proves ¬O+⊥\neg \mathrm{O}^+\bot but not ⊥\bot. Thus O+α\mathrm{O}^+\mathrm{\alpha} is not equivalent in PA to the application of any predicate F\mathrm{F} to ⌜α⌝\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner, for otherwise (1) – (3) would hold and the second incompleteness theorem for O+\mathrm{O}^+ would follow. The source of the trouble is the second conjunct of O+α\mathrm{O}^+\mathrm{\alpha}, α\mathrm{\alpha}; to factorize it into a predicate T\mathrm{T} and the quotation functor ⌜…⌝\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner would be to prove α↔T(⌜α⌝)\mathrm{\alpha}\leftrightarrow \mathrm{T}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner) for each formula α\mathrm{\alpha}, contrary to Tarski’s theorem on the undefinability of truth.

Such possibilities cast doubt on attempts to use Gödel’s incompleteness theorems to show that humans are not Turing machines (Lucas 1961 and Penrose 1989, 1994). One argument runs like this: if I am a Turing machine, then the set of mathematical and metamathematical sentences to which I eventually assent (given enough time and paper) is recursively enumerable, and therefore recursively axiomatizable; since I assent to elementary logic and syntax, the second incompleteness theorem applies; but I assent to my own consistency, so it would follow that I am inconsistent, which I am not; therefore I am not a Turing machine. The argument is obviously very loose as it stands. The supposition that I am a Turing machine is quite unclear until my relevant states have been specified; the inference to the recursive enumerability of the set of sentences to which I eventually assent is also dubious until the notion of assent and the role of input from the environment have been clarified. For the sake of argument, however, grant that these loose ends have been tidied up, and that a sense has been given to ‘I am a Turing machine’ from which it does follow that the set of sentences to which I eventually assent is recursively enumerable. Call the set ‘my system’. It can be recursively axiomatized; note that there is no automatic guarantee that everything provable in my system is true. A standard objection is then that no good reason has been provided to believe that my system is consistent. However, this objection is unsatisfying, for the argument from the incompleteness theorem seems to show that I must be inconsistent, if I eventually assent to my own consistency; yet it seems that, even if I do so, I might still be consistent.

A more significant fallacy in the argument is its neglect of the mode of presentation under which I assent to the consistency of my system. The Gödelian consistency sentence makes coded reference to my system by descriptive means: it codes a description of the axioms and inference rules of an axiomatized system that is in fact my own. The consistency sentence to which it seems that I can consistently assent makes reference to my system by indexical means, as my system. There is a corresponding difference between the descriptive provability operator ‘A Turing machine with machine table T\mathrm{T} can prove that …’, where ‘T\mathrm{T}’ abbreviates an appropriate description, and the indexical provability operator ‘I can prove that …’, in a context in which I am (i.e. realize) a Turing machine with machine table T\mathrm{T}. Although the two operators are extensionally equivalent, they may satisfy different principles under embedding, if I cannot prove that I am a Turing machine with machine table T\mathrm{T}. As Benacerraf pointed out, I might be a Turing machine without knowing which Turing machine I am. In particular, if I can prove that I cannot prove that 0=10=1, then it follows that a Turing machine with machine table T\mathrm{T} can prove that I cannot prove that 0=10=1, but it does not follow that a Turing machine with machine table T\mathrm{T} can prove that a Turing machine with machine table T\mathrm{T} cannot prove that 0=10=1, just as Bew⁡(⌜¬Bew⁡(⌜⊥⌝)⌝)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\neg\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\bot\urcorner)\urcorner) was seen above not to follow from O¬O⊥\mathrm{O}\neg \mathrm{O}\bot for an arbitrary provability operator O\mathrm{O} in PA. The present provability operators belong to a more expressive language than LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}}, but the logical point remains the same.

The objection applies even if the argument appeals to the first incompleteness theorem rather than the second. Unlike the latter, the former is not sensitive to differences between coextensive provability operators, because its statement – that in any consistent recursively axiomatizable extension of PA some sentence is neither provable nor disprovable – does not involve embedded occurrences of the provability operator. However, the anti-mechanistic argument claims that I can prove a supposedly undecidable sentence for my system; but the purported proof requires the consistency of my system as a lemma, so the same objection applies.

I might even know that I am a Turing machine without knowing which Turing machine I am (Reinhardt 1986). In effect, such a possibility is addressed by Penrose (1994: 132–37). Penrose offers only plausibility arguments against it. He finds it unlikely that I could prove each sentence enumerated by a given algorithm without being able to prove the generalization that every sentence enumerated by the algorithm is true, but such claims of likelihood are unreliable in an area so full of surprises. The execution of the algorithm may involve me in a progressively more complex thought procedure for each sentence. There is no guarantee that I can discern the common pattern in these procedures; even if I am told what it is, there is no guarantee that I will be convinced that it always yields correct results, even though, in each particular case, running through the procedure convinces me that that particular result is correct.

The point essentially concerns provability operators, not provability predicates. If Bew⁡I\operatorname{Bew}_{\mathrm{I}} is an indexical provability predicate, S\mathrm{S} is my system, and quotation marks are used in the ordinary way, then presumably Bew⁡I\operatorname{Bew}_{\mathrm{I}} would satisfy the derivability conditions (1) – (3), and my system provides the elementary logic and syntax needed. However, when diagonalization is applied to formulas involving Bew⁡I\operatorname{Bew}_{\mathrm{I}} in an attempt to prove the incompleteness theorems for Bew⁡I\operatorname{Bew}_{\mathrm{I}}, it yields an intuitively paradoxical sentence whose intended reading is something like ‘I cannot prove this sentence’; in contrast, Gödel’s use of diagonalization yields a sentence whose intended reading makes an ordinary statement about numbers. Not surprisingly, reasoning with genuinely self-referential sentences involving Bew⁡I\operatorname{Bew}_{\mathrm{I}} does lead to inconsistency (if consistency is claimed). The use of an indexical provability operator yields no such paradox, because it is not susceptible to diagonalization. For example, as noted above, the deviant provability operator O+\mathrm{O}^+ cannot be factorized into a provability predicate and a quotation functor.

The analogy with the earlier case of Socrates is strengthened if my system is defined as the set of sentences that express my mathematical and metamathematical knowledge, i.e. those which are provable on the basis of my knowledge-yielding axiom schemata and inference rules. Since no knowledge is false, I can give a trivial consistency proof for my system, so defined. Of course, I must use a knowledge operator rather than predicate to avoid Liar-like paradoxes.

The phenomenon of coextensive operators satisfying different principles is not confined to propositional attitudes. Here is an example involving metaphysical modalities. Nathan Salmon has argued that there are counterexamples concerning the possible constitution of artifacts to the analogue of the KK principle for metaphysical necessity (□□), the S4 axiom □p→□□p□ p\to□□ p. The soundness of his argument is not at issue here. What is relevant is part of his response to those who find the modal system S5 compelling as a logic for metaphysical necessity. S5 adds to the system S4 the other half of what is required for necessity always to be a non-contingent feature, the S5 axiom ¬□p→□¬□p\neg□ p\to□\neg□ p. S5 is the logic of necessity as truth in all possible worlds, on the assumption that it is not contingent what worlds are possible. Salmon suggests that those who find S5 compelling as a logic for metaphysical necessity confuse necessity with actual necessity, the property of being necessary in this, the actual world (1989: 30). For even if it is contingent whether it is necessary that P\mathrm{P}, it is noncontingent whether it is actually necessary that P\mathrm{P}, simply because it is noncontingent whether anything is actually the case. What is the case relative to a given world is not itself a world-relative matter. The logic of actual necessity is, uncontentiously, S5; necessity is easily confused with actual necessity, for the two are in some sense a priori equivalent.

It is time to give a precise general statement of the phenomenon just exemplified. Start with an interpreted language L\mathrm{L}, assumed to contain at least the truth-functional operators →\to and ⊥\bot. Suppose that the sentences of L\mathrm{L} are evaluated as true or false at members of a set of indices. Indices can be possible worlds, contexts of utterance, ordered pairs of worlds and contexts, or anything else; their role is simply to give the account generality. When the generality is not wanted, there can be just one index. In the examples above, the indices can be, respectively: contexts of utterance in which I (the speaker) am the man with a hole in his pocket; worlds in which Socrates is wise; contexts of utterance in which I am a Turing machine with machine table T\mathrm{T}; worlds treated as actual for the semantics of ‘actually’. The indices are not required to include all the points at which the semantics of L\mathrm{L} evaluates its sentences; rather, they are a subset of such points in which we happen to be interested. Truth-functional operators are assumed to behave according to their usual truth-tables at each index.

If O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 are sentence operators of L\mathrm{L}, say that O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 are coextensive just in case, for every sentence α\mathrm{\alpha} of L\mathrm{L} and every index ii, O1α\mathrm{O}_1\mathrm{\alpha} is true at ii if and only if O2α\mathrm{O}_2\mathrm{\alpha} is true at ii (when the indices are possible worlds, we could just as well speak of cointensiveness). A principle is a formula of the language of propositional modal logic, L□\mathrm{L}_{□}, with the primitive operators →\to, ⊥\bot and □□. If O\mathrm{O} is a sentence operator of L\mathrm{L}, an O-translation is a mapping *\text{*} from formulas of L□\mathrm{L}_{□} to sentences of L\mathrm{L} such that for all formulas α\mathrm{\alpha} and β\mathrm{\beta} of L□\mathrm{L}_{□}, *(α→β)=*α→*β\text{*}(\mathrm{\alpha}\to\mathrm{\beta})=\text{*}\mathrm{\alpha}\to\text{*}\mathrm{\beta}, *⊥=⊥\text{*}\bot=\bot and *□α=O*α\text{*}□\mathrm{\alpha}=\mathrm{O}\text{*}\mathrm{\alpha}. O\mathrm{O} satisfies a principle α\mathrm{\alpha} just in case *α\text{*}\mathrm{\alpha} is true at every index ii for every O\mathrm{O}-translation *\text{*}. Intuitively, the atomic sentence letters of L□\mathrm{L}_{□} are treated as universally quantified variables ranging over sentences of L\mathrm{L}, □□ is interpreted as O\mathrm{O} and →\to and ⊥\bot as themselves. The phenomenon of interest is just that not all coextensive operators satisfy the same principles.

The choice of L□\mathrm{L}_{□} to record principles is purely notational; it does not assume the operator O\mathrm{O} to have anything in common with a necessity operator beyond being a one-place sentential operator. The modal language has the merit of familiarity.

Coextensiveness and satisfaction of the same principles are conditions at the same level of generality; they are defined by quantification over the same set of indices. If coextensiveness had been defined by quantification only over actual items of some kind, and satisfaction of the same principles by quantification over all logically possible items of that kind, then the failure of the former to imply the latter would not have been surprising; but there is no such disparity in the definitions above.

The principles over which coextensive operators can differ always involve some self-embedding of □□. If a first-degree principle is one in which no occurrence of □□ lies within the scope of another, then coextensive operators satisfy exactly the same first-degree principles. But even the simplest kind of self-embedding, as in □□p□□ p, permits coextensive operators to differ in satisfaction of principles.
