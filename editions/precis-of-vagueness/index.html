Précis of Vagueness

Timothy Williamson

Philosophy and Phenomenological Research 57 (4) (1997): 921–928.

The central thesis of the book is that the proposition a vague sentence expresses in a borderline case is true or false, and we cannot know which. We are ignorant of its truth-value. This is the epistemic view of vagueness. It allows us to preserve both classical logic and disquotational principles about truth and falsity, with all their advantages: simplicity, clarity, power, past success, integration with well-confirmed theories in other domains. Consequently, the epistemic view has a head start over its rivals. The gap is widened by each rival theory’s specific disadvantages, many of them related to higher-order vagueness. The epistemic view is then strengthened by an explanation of our ignorance in borderline cases. The explanation predicts higher-order vagueness.

The epistemic view does not deny that there is vagueness. It is a theory of what vagueness is. Other theories of vagueness give rival explanations of the same phenomenon. We can usually recognize that phenomenon when we encounter it, even without knowing its underlying nature; it is the phenomenon of borderline cases. We can agree to use the term ‘vagueness’ for the phenomenon we are trying to understand, without prejudging its underlying nature. The term is neutral between epistemic, semantic and ontological accounts.

The first part of the book is historical. Vagueness first emerged as a problem for logic and philosophy in the guise of sorites paradoxes. How many grains make a heap? How few hairs must a bald man have? The Stoics formalized the paradoxes as arguments valid in Stoic logic, with apparently true premises and an apparently false conclusion:

One is few.
Not: one is few and two are not few.
Not: two are few and three are not few.
.
.
.
Not: nine thousand nine hundred and ninety nine are few and ten thousand are not few.

Ten thousand are few.

The Stoics were determined to preserve the principle of bivalence; every statement is either true or false. As I interpret them, they advocated an epistemic solution, on which the paradoxes exploit our ignorance of cut-off points, and were sensitive to the epistemic form of higher-order vagueness: if ii are not clearly few, it does not follow that ii are clearly not clearly few (the S5 principle fails for clarity). Later discussions of the paradoxes were less sophisticated.

The more serious one is about logic, the more serious one will be about the problem of vagueness and the sorites paradoxes. Thus the revival of logic last century caused the problem to re-emerge. Bertrand Russell made it a philosophical theme in its own right, although it was only with some difficulty that vagueness in the current sense was separated from other phenomena for which the term ‘vagueness’ is sometimes colloquially used, such as unspecificity. Russell and Frege responded to vagueness by banning it from a logically perfect language. Ordinary language philosophers welcomed vagueness, without doing much to understand it. But the desire for a systematic treatment of the logic of natural languages made the suitability of bivalent truth-conditional semantics for vague sentences an increasingly urgent question.

Many different forms of non-classical semantics have been proposed for vague languages. Here I can give only a flavour of the problems they face.

Many-valued logic is particularly popular amongst those who lack logical sophistication. The simplest version is three-valued logic, classifying sentences as true, false or neither. It faces an obvious objection from higher-order vagueness; the attempt to impose the three-way classification on a sorites series faces difficulties exactly like those that prompted the rejection of the two-way classification of sentences as true or false. An initially more plausible idea is that there is a continuous gradation between perfect truth and perfect falsity. This suggests using the real numbers between 0 and 1 as degrees of truth, the basis of fuzzy logic. For convenience, I will use the same notation for the three-valued approach, counting 0, ½\text{½} and 1 as its degrees of truth. An essential feature of the many-valued approach, three-valued or fuzzy, is that logical constants such as negation and conjunction are treated as degree-functional, in the sense that the degree of truth of a complex sentence with such a constant as its main connective is a function of the degrees of truth of its constituent sentences. For instance, if v(A)\mathrm{v}(A) is the degree of truth of AA, then v(¬A)=1−v(A)\mathrm{v}(\neg A) = 1-\mathrm{v}(A).

Degree-functionality has highly counterintuitive consequences in borderline cases. Let a1a_1 and a2a_2 be two qualitatively identical arrangements of grains in the same situation, and HaiHa_i mean that aia_i is a heap. Then v(Ha1)=v(Ha2)\mathrm{v}(Ha_1) = \mathrm{v}(Ha_2), whatever that degree of truth is, because a1a_1 and a2a_2 share all the properties on which heaphood supervenes. Intuitively, it would be completely false to say that a1a_1 is a heap and a2a_2 is not a heap, because there is no difference between them in that respect; thus v(Ha1&¬Ha2)=0\mathrm{v}(Ha_1 \mathbin{\&} \neg Ha_2) = 0 whatever the value of v(Ha1)\mathrm{v}(Ha_1). But now suppose that a1a_1 is a paradigmatically borderline case, with v(Ha1)=½\mathrm{v}(Ha_1) = \text{½}, so v(¬Ha2)=1−v(Ha2)=1−v(Ha1)=½\mathrm{v}(\neg Ha_2) = 1-\mathrm{v}(Ha_2) = 1-\mathrm{v}(Ha_1) = \text{½}. By degree-functionality, the degree of truth of a conjunction is some function *\text{*} of the degrees of truth of its conjuncts: v(A&B)=v(A)*v(B)\mathrm{v}(A \mathbin{\&} B) = \mathrm{v}(A) \mathbin{\text{*}} \mathrm{v}(B) for all sentences AA and BB. Furthermore, since repeating a conjunct makes no difference to degree of truth, v(A&A)=v(A)\mathrm{v}(A \mathbin{\&} A) = \mathrm{v}(A). These assumptions imply that v(Ha1&¬Ha2)=v(Ha1)*v(¬Ha2)=½*½=v(Ha1)*v(Ha1)=v(Ha1&Ha1)=v(Ha1)=½\mathrm{v}(Ha_1 \mathbin{\&} \neg Ha_2) = \mathrm{v}(Ha_1) \mathbin{\text{*}} \mathrm{v}(\neg Ha_2) = \text{½} \mathbin{\text{*}} \text{½} = \mathrm{v}(Ha_1) \mathbin{\text{*}} \mathrm{v}(Ha_1) = \mathrm{v}(Ha_1 \mathbin{\&} Ha_1) = \mathrm{v}(Ha_1) = \text{½}. In other words, degree functionality forces us to the view that it is half true to say that a1a_1 is a heap and a2a_2 is not a heap, which seems wrong. The problem affects every compositional semantics on which a sentence can have the same semantic value as its negation in a borderline case.

Fuzzy logic also has notorious difficulties with higher-order vagueness. Sentences assigning degrees of truth to vague sentences are themselves vague. If a vague language has a non-classical logic, then the logic of the metalanguage should be vague too. But fuzzy logic has not been systematically studied in a fuzzy metalogic. Moreover, higher-order vagueness undermines the reason for moving from two to more than two truth-values. If we are restricted to two truth-values, the question ‘What is the truth-value of this sentence?’ has no clear answer in borderline cases. Advocates of many-valued logic therefore increase the number of possible answers. But however many truth-values we have to choose from, the question still has no clear answer in some cases. We must sometimes answer ‘It’s not clear’ instead of adding more truth-values. But if that response is acceptable now, it could have been given in the original bivalent case; the tactic of increasing the number of truth-values is neither necessary nor sufficient to solve the problem.

A subtler approach is supervaluationism. It avoids most of the mistakes of many-valued logic. For the supervaluationist, truth is supertruth and falsity is superfalsity. A sentence is supertrue just in case each admissible sharpening of the language assigns it truth; it is superfalse just in case each admissible sharpening assigns it falsity. An admissible sharpening of a language is a classical assignment of semantic values to expressions of the language consistent with all semantic constraints (and the non-semantic facts). Supervaluationism therefore validates every logical truth of the classical predicate calculus, including the law of excluded middle.1 But it rejects the principle of bivalence, for although admissible sharpenings are bivalent, in a borderline case some admissible sharpenings assign the sentence truth and others assign it falsity, so the sentence is neither supertrue nor superfalse, and therefore neither true nor false. Supervaluationism can handle the problem of the two qualitatively identical heaps in a satisfying way, for although some admissible sharpenings assign Ha1Ha_1 truth and others assign it falsity, the semantic constraints ensure that each admissible sharpening assigns Ha1Ha_1 and Ha2Ha_2 the same truth-value; thus no admissible sharpening assigns truth to Ha1&¬Ha2Ha_1 \mathbin{\&} \neg Ha_2, so it is superfalse. Supervaluationism can even make some sense of the degree of truth of a sentence as the probability that a randomly chosen admissible sharpening assigns it truth. In contrast with fuzzy logic, the supervaluationist degree of truth of a conjunction is not a function of the supervaluationist degrees of truth of its conjuncts, just as the probability of a conjunction is not a function of the probabilities of its conjuncts.

Supervaluationism is an elegant theory, which almost succeeds in validating classical logic. Can it handle higher-order vagueness? The classification of sentences as supertrue, superfalse or neither is itself vague. Admissibility is vague, because it is vague what the semantic constraints are. Supervaluationism can respond by letting each admissible sharpening make its own ruling as to which sharpenings count as admissible.2 With a ‘Definitely’ operator in the language, the result resembles the standard possible worlds semantics for the modal system T; admissibility is reflexive but need not be transitive. In metalinguistic terms, some ascriptions of supertruth are neither supertrue nor superfalse. But a deeper problem remains. Supervaluationism does not validate the Tarskian disquotational schema ‘”AA” is true ≡A\equiv A’ (where ≡\equiv is the material biconditional), otherwise it would validate bivalence. But a notion of truth, truthT, that does validate the schema is definable within the supervaluationist framework. TruthT disquotes; supertruth does not. Disquotation is central to our conception of truth. Why does the supervaluationist equate truth with supertruth rather than with truthT? The original supervaluationist idea was that since ‘”AA” is trueT’ is just as vague as ‘AA’, the notion of truthT is inappropriate for theoretical purposes; one must use the precise notion of supertruth instead. But higher-order vagueness means that supertruth is vague too. Thus the appeal to precision no longer defeats the presumption that truth is truthT rather than supertruth. The supervaluationist rejection of bivalence is an artifact of the misidentification of truth as supertruth.

Could one identify truth with truthT while maintaining the apparatus of admissible sharpenings? The difficulty would be in distinguishing this view from an epistemic one. For one can now show that exactly one assignment of truth-values to sentences is correct, in the sense of assigning truth to all and only the true sentences of the language and falsity to all and only the false ones. Thus if two admissible sharpening differ in their assignments of truth-values to sentences, at least one of them is incorrect. Since incorrect sharpenings can be admissible, their admissibility seems to be just an epistemic matter.

Henceforth, I will assume both classical logic and these Tarski-inspired characterizations of truth and falsity for an utterance uu:3

(T)
If uu says that PP, then uu is true if and only if PP.
(F)
If uu says that PP, then uu is false if and only if not PP.

By classical logic, in particular the law of excluded middle, one can deduce this form of the principle of bivalence from (T) and (F):

(B)
If uu says that PP, then uu is true or uu is false.

Any utterance that says anything is true or false. (T), (F) and (B) are all neutral with respect to this disquotational principle:

(D)
An utterance of ‘PP’ says that PP.

For example, an utterance of ‘He walks’ in a language class may not say that anything is the case, because no attempt is made to supply a referent for the pronoun, and an utterance of ‘I am hungry’ by you does not say that I am hungry. But, on the epistemic view, when a vague sentence is uttered in a borderline case with the intention of saying that something is the case, the corresponding instance of (D) as uttered in the same context will normally be correct.4 For example, my present utterance of ‘Four grains make a heap’ says that four grains make a heap. Then the consequents of (T), (F) and (D) can be detached; my utterance is true if and only if four grains make a heap, false if and only if four grains do not make a heap, and either true or false.

Suppose that four grains are indeed a borderline case for ‘heap’. Then we cannot know that four grains make a heap, and we cannot know that four grains do not make a heap. ‘Cannot’ here does not mean metaphysical impossibility; who knows what we could know if we had infinitely powerful minds? The sense in which we are entitled to claim impossibility is the sense in which ‘cannot ϕ\mathrm{\phi}’ is roughly equivalent to ‘do not ϕ\mathrm{\phi}, and haven’t the faintest idea how to come to ϕ\mathrm{\phi}’. We do not know whether four grains make a heap, and haven’t the faintest idea how to find out. In this sense there is a truth that we cannot know, for it is either true that four grains make a heap or true that they do not.

Some attempted explanations of vagueness cite only the contextual variation in reference of vague terms; if they were correct, there would be no general obstacle to knowing the truth in a given context, just as the contextual variation in the reference of ‘I’ constitutes no general obstacle to knowing whom ‘I’ refers to in a given context. But in most cases we cannot know how many grains make a heap even relative to a given context. Although vague terms typically do vary in reference across contexts, that is a different phenomenon from their vagueness.

The argument so far is that borderline cases involve unknowable truths. Thus a necessary condition of vagueness is epistemic. This is not yet to say that vagueness can be characterized in epistemic terms, for the necessary condition is not a sufficient condition. Not all unknowable truths involve borderline cases. If Goldbach’s Conjecture (that every even number greater than 2 is the sum of two primes) is an unknowable truth, as for all we know it is, its unknowability has nothing to do with vagueness. Can we distinguish borderline cases from other cases of unknowable truths in epistemic terms?

Consider first the general phenomenon of inexact knowledge. On a walk, I glance at the view and say ‘That tower is at most a mile away’. In fact, it is just an inch less than a mile away. I judged truly. But I did not know that the tower was at most a mile away. My powers of visual discrimination are limited; I could too easily have made the same judgement even if the tower had been two inches further away, in which case I would have judged falsely; my true belief did not have a reliable enough basis to constitute knowledge.5 The proposal is that borderline cases involve an obstacle to knowledge of this general kind. But whereas I can in principle find out whether the tower is at most a mile away by exact measurement, no such resolution of borderline cases is available. The reason is, of course, that the term ‘mile’ is defined so as to facilitate exact measurement in miles, whereas nothing of the kind has been done for most vague terms. Since we do not know their cut-off points, we cannot calibrate instruments to measure them.

What form does inexact knowledge take in borderline cases? Let nn grains be a borderline case for ‘heap’; suppose for definiteness that nn grains do in fact make a heap (the argument is parallel if we suppose that they do not). If I judge that nn grains make a heap, I judge truly. But I do not know that nn grains make a heap, for my true belief does not have a reliable enough basis to constitute knowledge. It is too close to being false, for n−in-i grains do not make a heap, where ii is very small. But now the source of the inexactness is subtly different; it is a limitation of my powers of conceptual rather than perceptual discrimination. The case is borderline even if my eyesight is so sharp that I can see the difference between nn grains and n−1n-1. Moreover, to say that nn grains make a heap is to say that it is possible for there to be a heap of nn grains; given that that is a truth, it is a necessary truth. Thus even if things had been different, I would not have judged falsely that nn grains make a heap. However, I could easily have made a judgement in the words ‘nn grains make a heap’ even if in other respects our dispositions to use ‘heap’ had been very slightly different, shifting its extension so as to exclude the case of nn grains, in which case I would have judged falsely. Thus the basis on which I judge that nn grains make a heap is unreliable because it could too easily have produced a false judgement in the same words. This explanation is discussed more fully by both commentators, and by me in my reply to them.

Higher-order vagueness is a natural corollary of the foregoing account. For suppose that nn grains are a borderline case for ‘heap’ and n−in-i grains are not, where ii is very small. By a similar argument, although I can judge truly that nn grains are a borderline case for ‘heap’, I cannot know that nn grains are a borderline case for ‘heap’. Thus there are borderline cases for ‘borderline case for “heap”’, and so on. This can be all reflected in the object-language by use of a ‘Clearly’ operator, whose semantics is like the supervaluationist semantics for ‘Definitely’ without the non-standard account of truth.

The explanation of our ignorance in borderline cases assumes a connection between the meaning of vague terms and our use of them. Indeed, on the epistemic view it is plausible that meaning supervenes on use and the environment in the following sense:

(#)
If an expression ee in a possible situation ss has the same nonintentionally characterized use and environment as an expression ff in a possible situation tt, then ee in ss has the same meaning as ff in tt.

Thus the epistemic view does not make meaning independent of use. What one cannot do is to use supervenience principles like (#) to work out whether a vague term applies to a borderline case by examining the statistics of use, even if ‘meaning’ is taken in something like the sense ‘boundaries of application’. Although the boundaries of a term’s application are a necessary consequence of its nonintentionally characterized use and environment, that does not make them a knowable consequence. The core of the ignorance that constitutes vagueness is ignorance of necessary truths.6
