Review of R. Sorensen, Blindspots

Timothy Williamson

Mind 99 (393) (1990): 137–140.

You are reading this review without my supposing that you are; the number of books now in the Bodleian is even and no one will ever know it; some of my beliefs are not true; there are no beliefs. Each of these propositions is true or could have been. Yet each evades cognition in some way. I cannot truly suppose the first and no one can ever know the second (if supposition and knowledge distribute over conjunction); my belief in the third ensures that some of my beliefs are not true; eliminativists’ belief in the fourth ensures its falsity. All are blindspots in Roy Sorensen’s sense: consistent propositions to which certain individuals cannot take certain propositional attitudes, perhaps relative to certain norms or other constraints. The book overflows with examples and classifications of blindspots. Sorensen displays their potential as a rich source of objections to philosophical theories, particularly to those of an anti-realist cast. His main purpose, however, is to argue that blindspots are the common root of many philosophical paradoxes, notable the sorites, the prediction (or surprise examination) paradox and a group of decision-theoretic problems such as iterated prisoners’ dilemma. His characteristic method is to assemble a wide range of variant paradoxes and rival and preliminary solutions, in an attempt to show that only his final view solves the problem at the requisite level of generality. His positive account is sometimes hard to find in the cornucopia of argument, example and theory. One can browse through the useful and curious information as in Brewer’s Dictionary of Phrase and Fable. Nevertheless, a central thread of argument is there.

Sorensen’s response to sorites arguments, following James Cargile and Richmond Campbell, is the epistemic theory of vagueness. On this view some proposition of the form ‘n+1\mathrm{n} + 1 grains make a heap and n\mathrm{n} grains do not make a heap’ is true but unknowable; it is a blindspot. Thus the major premiss of the paradoxical argument is false. Sorensen claims ‘Resistance to the epistemic theory of vagueness can only be motivated by a prior resolve to allow no unknowable truths’ (p. 245). The present reviewer is a counter-example, resistant to the theory but well-disposed to unknowable truths. One does not need to be an anti-realist to believe that predicates do not acquire their satisfaction-conditions by magic. Sorensen betrays no curiosity as to how our word ‘heap’ has acquired the determinate extension he claims for it, or as to why we cannot know what that extension is. It is not just our dispositions to apply or withhold the word that fail to specify boundaries; one can be quite prepared to invoke causal relations to natural kinds in explaining the realist satisfaction-conditions of some predicates without supposing that heaps form a natural kind. Even if the epistemic theory of vagueness is correct, it cannot claim to have solved sorites paradoxes until it has dispelled these mysteries. Sorensen neither argues that there cannot be a substantive theory of reference nor indicates a theory that might explain the nature of the link between ‘heap’ and its extension compatibly with his account of vagueness. No doubt our understanding of reference is poor, but the issues need to be addressed. Sorensen’s insensitivity to them is manifest in his handling of Benacerraf’s argument that no identification of numbers with sets is correct, since there is no reason to prefer one identification to its rivals; this too is treated as a crude move from unknowability to untruth. Are we to suppose that Shakespeare may have used ‘seven’ to refer to the corresponding Zermelo set rather than the von Neumann one? Sorensen’s arguments risk proving too much, by ruling out semantic indeterminacy altogether. Surely it can occur, as when I define the term ‘cammal’ only by the stipulations: (1) all cats are cammals; (2) all cammals are mammals. He would need to provide a positive account of this fairly unproblematic kind of semantic indeterminacy to show that vague predicates do not manifest a subtler form of the same phenomenon.

Blindspots provide a more plausible account of the prediction paradox and its relatives. When the famously reliable teacher tells her pupils that there will be a surprise test next week, they know that her announcement will be fulfilled. However, when they argue that the test cannot be on the last day, because by then it would not be a surprise, they assume that if there is no test by the eve of the last day then they will still know at that later time that her announcement will be fulfilled: but the assumption is false, for otherwise they would in effect know ‘There will be a test tomorrow and we do not know it’, which is a blindspot for them. Thus their original argument that there will be no test does not get started. As given, the diagnosis turns on ‘the existential premiss’: if the announcement is fulfilled, there will be a test. Sorensen argues that it is inessential to the underlying problem by considering puzzles without it in decision theory. His response is to suggest a way in which an existential premiss, and therefore blindspots, may be introduced by the actions of rational agents rather than by the original announcement.

Sorensen does not mention a closer variant of the surprise examination without an existential premiss. A test is conditionally predictable if the pupils know the night before ‘If there is a test this week, it will be tomorrow’. The teacher announces just that there will be no conditionally predictable test the following week. If there is no test at all, the announcement is fulfilled. Thus not only can the pupils know at the time of the announcement that it will be fulfilled, they can continue to know it when no test has occurred by the eve of the last day; at that time they will be able to work out that no test will be given. So what is to stop them from working that out at the start of the week? Sorensen himself provides the materials to fill the apparent lacunae in the pupils’ argument. Yet a conditionally unpredictable test on Tuesday is clearly possible. It is not clear that Sorensen’s blindspot analysis of the decision-theoretic cases extends to this one. Even if Sorensen’s account of the prediction paradox is insufficiently general, his criticisms of rival approaches are generally effective, and he provides a splendid variety of data against which all future work on the topic should be tested. Historians of philosophy should also enjoy his account of the paradox’s development. It seems to have begun in about 1943, when the Swedish mathematician Lennart Ekbom reflected on a radio announcement that there would be a surprise civil defence exercise in the following week. Sorensen traces the way in which journal discussion of the issue gradually revealed its depth, partly through dubious assimilation to recognized paradoxes (such as the Liar), after some early responses of delightful superficiality. It may provide a paradigm of one kind of progress in analytic philosophy.

Sorensen’s long book contains so many arguments that even its unusually high ratio of valid to invalid ones leaves far more of the latter than can be mentioned here. Just one further issue will be raised. Individual blindspots such as ‘It is raining and I do not believe it’—in contrast to universal blindspots such as ‘It is raining and no one believes it’—seem to have a special connection with the first person. The irrationality or impossibility of my believing ‘It is raining and TW does not believe it’ or ‘It is raining and the man who left his manuscript in his garden does not believe it’ seems to depend on my belief that I am TW or the man who left his manuscript in his garden; the irrationality or impossibility of my believing ‘It is raining and I do not believe it’ is not derivative in this way. Sorensen denies the contrast. He argues that (A) ‘I’ is not referentially transparent and asserts that (B) there is no other reason to postulate the contrast (p. 32). (B) is false; Sorensen does not eliminate the possibility of basing the contrast on the point that a belief expressible by its subject as ‘It is raining and I do not believe it’ gets its subject into trouble no matter who he or she is, which is not the case for ‘It is raining and TW does not believe it’. As for (A), ‘suppose the evil demon sends a signal that will later produce the thought “I am now thinking” in Descartes. By the time Descartes thinks the thought, the evil demon has committed suicide. Since “I” was produced by the evil demon, it refers to him’ (p. 31; this is not the whole of Sorensen’s argument). If the evil demon has simply caused Descartes to think a certain thought, there is no reason to suppose that the ‘I’ of the thought refers to the demon rather than to Descartes. The more interesting suggestion is that the demon is using Descartes’ consciousness as a surface on which to scrawl token thoughts of which Descartes is not the thinker. Descartes will be aware of the thoughts, and wrongly believe himself to be their thinker. However, this is no counter-example to the claim that the reference of ‘I’ in a thought is transparent to its thinker, for Descartes is by hypothesis not the thinker of the demon-inscribed token (the point is more obvious for belief in place of thought). Sorensen may have confused this token with Descartes’ (mis)understanding of it, for in being aware of it he may (falsely) think ‘That is a token of the thought: I am now thinking’ and thereby produce a token of ‘I am now thinking’ whose ‘I’ does refer to him, but it is quite distinct from the former token. Could Descartes think a token of ‘I am now thinking’ under the misapprehension that its thinker was the demon? He might have the thought ‘I am now thinking’ and later or elsewhere in his mind think ‘The demon is the thinker of that thought’ about it; I can write ‘I am now thinking’ on a piece of paper and come to believe that it was written by someone else. If Sorensen is to undermine the apparent link between blindspots and ‘I’, he needs to attack not the infallibility of my thoughts about my ‘I’-thoughts but my self-acquaintance as the referent of ‘I’ in my thinking of the ‘I’-thought itself.

I recommend watching this book snap at the heels of philosophical dignitaries; its teeth sometimes close on clay.
