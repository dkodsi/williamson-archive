On knowledge of the unknowable

Timothy Williamson

Analysis 47 (1987): 154–158.

If it is an unknown truth that pp, it is an unknowable truth that it is an unknown truth that pp (cf F. B. Fitch, ‘A logical analysis of some value concepts’, The Journal of Symbolic Logic 28, 1963, pp. 135–42, at p. 138). It follows, by classical logic, that if all truths are knowable then all truths are known. This hardish fact makes life difficult for the verificationist who wishes to assert that all truths are knowable, but to deny that all truths are known. He might try rejecting classical logic (cf. my ‘Intuitionism disproved?’, Analysis 42.4, October, 1982, pp. 203–7). Dorothy Edgington has recently suggested a different way out (cf. ‘The paradox of knowability’, Mind 94, 1985, pp. 557–68). She admits that there are unknowable truths in the sense of Fitch’s argument, but interestingly revises the verificationist principle, to give it this form: if, in a situation ss, it is a truth that pp, then there is a possible situation s′s' in which it is known that, in ss, pp. Although the truth (for some values of ‘pp’) that, in ss, it is an unknown truth that pp cannot be known in ss, it might be known in some possible situation other than ss. I have argued elsewhere that this account faces difficulties of principle in explaining how the knower in s′s' can pick out the situation ss in thought with adequate specificity (cf. ‘On the paradox of knowability’, Mind forthcoming). I waive those objections here, to examine Edgington’s application of her principle to a particular case of knowledge in one situation that something is an unknown truth in another. I shall argue that her account does not generalize to the full range of cases which it needs to cover.

Suppose that, in the actual situation ss, it is an unknown truth that it rained last night. Nevertheless, someone could have known that it rained last night. How would this provide knowledge that, in ss, it is an unknown truth that it rained last night? According to Edgington, ‘the possible knower that it rained last night has to know that it still would have rained, had neither he nor anyone else noticed. And any possible knower that it rained does, or at least can, know this’ (p. 567). Such an account obviously does not apply to cases in which the corresponding counterfactual is false, but, Edgington says,

If there are truths which fail to satisfy the principle

(1)
pp would still have been true, had no one known that pp,

that I am in pain, for example, then they satisfy the principle,

(2)
If pp, then someone knows that pp

and, a fortiori, they satisfy [the verificationist principle] (ibid., my numbering).

Evidently, the above account of knowledge that, in ss, it is an unknown truth that pp presupposes that it is knowable that pp (in the ordinary sense, in which Edgington concedes that not all truths are knowable). It therefore does not cover a case of the following kind. Let ‘pp’ abbreviate a description of all the neurophysiological events which ever actually take place in any brain, conjoined with the proposition that no other neurophysiological events take place in any brain. It is an unknown truth that pp. However, it does not even seem knowable by creatures such as us that pp, for such knowledge would have to be recorded in a brain by different states from those which record actual knowledge, making it false that pp. Thus Edgington’s account does not show how there could be knowledge that, in ss, pp, for this value of ‘pp’. This limitation noted, one can look at those cases where it is known in some situation s′s' that pp.

Suppose that (1) is known in s′s' to be true by someone who also knows that pp. Edgington’s argument requires this to constitute knowledge that, in ss, it is an unknown truth that pp. Now clearly, in s′s', (1) could not tell one that it would still have been true that pp in ss, unless ss would or might have obtained, had no one known that pp; otherwise, its being true that pp in ss would not be a necessary condition for the truth of (1). For the truth of a counterfactual does not require its consequent to be true in all possible situations in which its antecedent is, but only in those which are most similar to the situation at which the counterfactual is being evaluated, for some appropriate respect of similarity. Thus Edgington’s argument requires the following counterfactual not to hold in s′s':

(3)
ss would not have obtained, had no one known that pp

For this reason, s′s' should be chosen so as to differ as little as possible from ss, given that it is known that pp in s′s' but not in ss (and that (1) is known in s′s' but not in ss — this may force further, unwanted differences between s′s' and ss). Even when s′s' is chosen in this way, however, (3) often will hold in s′s'. For example, suppose that in ss a flower bloomed on this spot at time tt (= pp), where tt is a past time at which there were no relevant thinking beings. It is an unknown truth in ss that pp. Nevertheless, it could have been known that pp; in some s′s', human beings evolve earlier and see a flower here at tt. Their counterfactual thought (1), that a flower would still have bloomed here at tt had no one known that it did, is true. However, it is true simply because the flower would still have bloomed here, had they not gone for a walk in the forest that day; for situations of that kind, in which the general evolution of the human race up to tt is more or less the same as in s′s', are the most relevantly similar ones to s′s' in which the antecedent of (1) is true. In other words, (3) holds in s′s', for, in ss, the human race has not even begun to evolve at tt. Hence, as argued above, knowledge in s′s' of (1) does not give knowledge of actuality. Note that it is not even clearly true in s′s' that a flower would have bloomed here at tt had the human race not evolved until it did in ss; the most relevantly similar situations to s′s' in which it does evolve later might be ones in which the absence of human beings altered the ecology of the area in such a way that a flower did not bloom here at tt.

Thus, even if someone can know both (1) and that pp, Edgington’s account of knowledge that, in ss, it is an unknown truth that pp breaks down for some cases. Her other line of argument in the quoted passage is intended to apply when (1) is false. There is thus another lacuna: what if (1) were true but unknowable, or at least not knowable by whoever knew that pp (in the sense in which Edgington concedes that not all truths are knowable)? However, instead of pressing this point, I shall examine Edgington’s claim that if (1) is false then (2) is true, in which case the verificationist has no problem.

Note first that the difficulty arises when (1) is false in s′s', the situation which would have obtained had it been known that pp. If the falsehood of (1) entails the truth of (2), then presumably what follows is that (2) is true in s′s', which is no surprise since the consequent of (2) is true by hypothesis in s′s'; but since the truth of (2) in s′s' does not seem to rule out the possibility that it is an unknown truth in ss that pp, no way seems to have been provided of knowing either in s′s' or in ss that, in ss, it is an unknown truth that pp. Thus the revised verificationist principle is still under threat. Note that it would be irrelevant to try to argue to the truth of (2) in ss from the falsity of (1) in ss, for, by hypothesis, both the antecedent and the consequent of (1) are true in ss. Thus it would have to be claimed that the falsity of (1) in s′s' entails the truth of (2) in ss. But there is no reason why such an entailment should hold. For example, suppose that it is an unknown truth in ss that exactly nn lists of a hundred or more items were completed in Carthage from its foundation to its destruction (= pp; I assume that 100<n100<n). The corresponding s′s' is presumably a situation in which a list of all the nn relevant lists, including itself, is completed just as Carthage is about to be destroyed. Let s″s'' be a situation as relevantly similar to s′s' as is compatible with its not being known in s″s'' that pp; s″s'' is presumably a situation in which the list of lists is not completed, and no lists are completed which were not completed in s′s'. Thus at most n−1n-1 relevant lists are completed in s″s'', so in s″s'' it is not the case that pp. Hence, by the choice of s″s'', (1) is false in s′s'. Nevertheless, (2) is not true in ss — the Carthaginians did not actually make such a list of lists. In this example, it might be claimed that a modified version of Edgington’s argument would go through, in which s′s' would be chosen as a situation in which the list of lists is completed and contains n+1n+1 items including itself; in s′s', it would not be the case that pp, but it would be known that it would have been the case that pp had it not been known that exactly n+1n+1 relevant lists were completed; this might be claimed to be knowledge of ss. However, such a move is ad hoc: there is no longer any general way of finding a situation s′s' of the right sort, nor any reason to think that there is any such situation. Moreover, there are more recalcitrant examples. Suppose that our best physical theory tells us that one can know what state a ?-particle is in only by interacting with it in ways which unpredictably change its state. Suppose also that it is an unknown truth in ss that ?-particle zz is in state kk (= pp). Then (1) is not true in the corresponding s′s', but (2) is not true in ss. Of course, a verificationist might deny that there can be unknown truths in cases of this kind: such a claim represents, however, a sharp increase in the price to be paid for the dubious advantages of verificationism. Certainly, there is no reason to regard the falsity of (1) in the relevant situation as any general guarantee of the actual truth of (2).

Edgington has offered a model of non-actual knowledge that, in the actual situation, it is an unknown truth at pp. I have argued that, even if one accepts the model for the simple examples which she gives, it seems to break down for more complicated ones. Nor is there any obvious prospect of adapting the model to these cases.

The model therefore provides no reason to think that the revised verificationist principle, which requires such knowledge, is universally true. Edgington also discusses weaker forms of verificationism, in which the concept of knowledge is replaced by that of reasonable belief, or of evidence; the arguments of this paper apply just as much to them. If revised verificationism lacks independent plausibility, Edgington would probably wish to rest her defence of it on an appeal to considerations about our grasp of concepts (p. 557). It would be interesting to have a detailed account of what it is about such considerations in virtue of which they lead to revised verificationism rather than to the fundamentalist principle, in the sense in which Edgington rejects it, that all truths are knowable.
