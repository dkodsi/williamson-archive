# Sense, validity and context

Timothy Williamson

Philosophy and Phenomenological Research 57 (3) (1997): 649–654.

How does Frege’s distinction between sense and reference apply to terms whose reference depends on context? The stakes are high; a good account of the sense of ‘I’ might constitute an analysis of self-consciousness. Frege’s own remarks on this matter are notoriously unsatisfactory. In Past, Space, and Self, John Campbell does better. The depth and freshness of his investigations are outstanding. He uncovers a wide variety of evidence, both logical and psychological, to which any account must do justice.

For Campbell, our primary fix on the notion of sense is by the logical notion of valid inference. Suppose that I argue thus:

(I)
This is rough.
This is red.
--------
Something is rough and red.

The two tokens of ‘this’ express the same sense if and only if my argument is valid. Since the identity in linguistic type of the two tokens does not settle the question of validity either way, Campbell treats arguments as constituted of token sentences or acts of judgement; I will follow him in this for the moment. Since the identity in reference of the two tokens seems not to guarantee validity either, even granted the identity in linguistic type—the first demonstration might be tactile, the second visual—identity in reference seems to be insufficient for identity in sense (reference does not determine sense). But if the two tokens refer to different objects, then the argument is invalid, for the truth of the premises does not imply the truth of the conclusion; so the senses are distinct. In this case at least, identity in reference is necessary for identity in sense (sense determines reference).

On Campbell’s account, the question ‘What is the sense of “I”?’ becomes ‘Which arguments involving “I” are valid?’. If you say ‘I am hungry’ and I say ‘I am sleepy’, there is no valid argument to the conclusion ‘Someone is hungry and sleepy’ in anyone’s mouth. Thus the two tokens of ‘I’ cannot be said to have the same sense. Since we both used ‘I’ with its standard linguistic meaning, that shared meaning is not the sense of tokens of ‘I’. One can specify the shared linguistic meaning by stating the reference rule for ‘I’:

Token-reflexive rule Any token of ‘I’ refers to whoever produced it.

The rule explains why your token of ‘I’ refers to you and mine refers to me. Campbell rightly puts the token-reflexive rule at the centre of his account. He combines it with the assumption that the premises and conclusion of a genuine inference must be produced by a single subject (p. 105). Thus all tokens of ‘I’ in an inference are guaranteed coreference, and have the same sense. Any genuine inference of this form is valid:

(II)
I am hungry.
I am sleepy.
--------
Someone is hungry and sleepy.

Campbell’s account undermines the Fregean claim that senses are public, even for context-independent expressions. There is no valid inference from your token of ‘37 is prime’ to mine, for they were produced by different subjects; thus there is no basis for assigning them the same sense. Equally, there is no invalid inference from your token to mine; thus there is no basis for assigning them different senses. The account seems to make all your senses incommensurable with mine.

How persuasive is Campbell’s assumption that the premises and conclusion of an inference must be produced by a single subject? Imagine a logic class in which one pupil produces a premise, another produces a second premise, a third produces a conclusion, and the teacher asks the others ‘Was that a valid argument?’. The blanket answer ‘It wasn’t an argument at all’ does not seem compelling. Even if the act of inferring a conclusion must always be performed by a single subject (which is doubtful), it does not follow that the question of validity arises only when the premises were produced by the same subject. Why can’t I validly infer my token of ‘37 is prime’ from yours? One might instead try to preserve the connection between validity and sense by allowing multi-subject arguments.

In other respects, Campbell allows valid inferences to straddle contexts. For example, he takes his earlier token of ‘I am $F$’ to be logically related to his later token of ‘I was $F$’ (p. 84). He does not assume that the premises and conclusion of an inference must be produced at a single time (the word ‘simultaneously’ on p. 105 obscures this). He allows ‘now’, unlike ‘I’, to change its reference in the course of an inference. If ‘I’ were allowed to change its reference in the course of an inference too, the asymmetry would disappear.

By what principle is the argument from Campbell’s earlier token of ‘I am $F$’ to his later token of ‘I was $F$’ valid? The obvious proposal is to combine a token-reflexive account of ‘I’ and tense with context-independent information about the tokens to generate context-independent truth-conditions for them, then apply a standard account of validity for context-independent arguments. Thus a token of ‘I am $F$’ uttered by J. C. at noon on 29 July 1996 is true if and only if J. C. is [tenselessly] $F$ at noon on 29 July 1996; a token of ‘I was $F$’ uttered by J. C. at noon on 30 July 1996 is true if and only if J. C. is [tenselessly] $F$ before noon on 30 July 1996; the inference from ‘J. C. is $F$ at noon on 29 July 1996’ to ‘J. C. is $F$ before noon on 30 July 1996’ is context-independently valid. Unfortunately, this account makes too much information accessible to pure reason. The inference from the earlier token of ‘I am $F$’ uttered by J. C. to a later token of ‘J. C. was $F$’ comes out valid by the same standard; Campbell denies its validity (p. 84). Similarly, a token of ‘Now = noon on 29 July 1996’ uttered at noon on 29 July 1996 becomes a logical truth (logically valid); but logic should not tell us the time. It would not help Campbell to bite the bullet, accept these inferences and argue that we are not always in a position to recognize validity when we see it; for then there would be no good reason to make validity sensitive to anything more fine-grained than reference; the distinction between sense and reference would disappear. The notion of validity is hard to apply directly to inferences between token-reflexive tokens in different contexts.

The original reason for not applying the notion of validity to arguments constituted of sentence types was that an argument type involving demonstratives, such as (I), has both valid and invalid instances. But that point does not force one all the way to applying the notion of validity to arguments constituted of sentence tokens. One could handle it by subscripting demonstratives within sentence types under a convention of coreference for identically subscripted demonstratives; thus (I) becomes valid when and only when the two occurrences of ‘this’ receive the same subscript (compare David Kaplan, ‘Afterthoughts’, in Joseph Almog, John Perry and Howard Wettstein, eds., Themes from Kaplan (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1989), at p. 587). Validity could then be explained as truth-preservation with respect to each context of utterance. The context must not vary from premises to conclusion, otherwise even the argument from ‘It is dark here’ to ‘It is dark here’ would count as invalid (of course, our ability to understand the premises and conclusion may depend on experience in other contexts, but that does not imply variation in the context of utterance within the argument itself). Since the token-reflexive rule fixes the reference of ‘I’ in any context, ‘I’ will not be subscripted.

There is a problem. On this account, the validity of an argument tells us nothing about truth-preservation when the premises and conclusion are instantiated in different contexts. But inference takes time; we judge the premises and conclusion successively. Thus the account of validity looks irrelevant to the inferences we actually make. Consider this argument:

(III)
Judy is dancing.
Judy is singing.
--------
Someone is both dancing and singing.

If names need to be subscripted, assign the two occurrences of ‘Judy’ the same subscript, so that coreference is not in doubt. Thus (III) is valid: at any one context of utterance, its conclusion is true if its premises are. But if the premises and conclusion of (III) are judged in that chronological order, then the three tokens of the present-tense ‘is’ occur at three successive times; the tokens of the premises might be true and the token of the conclusion false. Intuitively, the problem is rather trivial; (III) could represent a cognitively praiseworthy piece of reasoning. How can that be?

Throughout an extended period of time, you might have a belief that Judy is dancing, a belief that Judy is singing, and a derived belief that someone is both dancing and singing. At each moment in that interval, your beliefs can be expressed by the premises and conclusion of (III); they instantiate a valid argument. Things go deductively wrong only when the premises and conclusion of (III) are realized as a series of occurrent judgements. In the circumstances, this realization may be psychologically necessary for the production of the inferred belief. We sometimes need to be in conscious control of our inferences, which requires their premises and conclusions to be realized successively as conscious judgements. We are psychologically capable of being in many simultaneous belief states; we are not psychologically capable of performing many simultaneous acts of conscious judgement. The single context of utterance is a context of purely notional utterance. But the function of this process is mainly instrumental; what matters is the deductive relation between the beliefs at any one time, in the interval, not the deductively invalid relation between the judgements at successive times in the interval. The simultaneous states of belief constitute a proper application of (III); the successive acts of judgement do not.

Our inferential practice exploits a convenient insensitivity to the specific context of our judgements. As a result, we sometimes run into trouble. One example may be the philosophical tendency to suppose that change is contradictory: the disposition to connect judgements inferentially as in (III) might overspill into the inference from the true judgement ‘Judy is dancing’ and the true judgement made a second later ‘Judy is not dancing’ to the self-contradictory judgement ‘Someone is both dancing and not dancing’.

An epistemological objection remains. If there are no logical relations over time between tensed judgements, how can we retain tensed knowledge? Suppose that I judge ‘It is raining’, and on that basis later judge ‘It was raining’. If the latter judgement is supposed to derive its epistemic status from the former, but the two are logically independent, how can the latter express knowledge?

The objection overplays the role of logic in extending our knowledge. Consider my knowledge that Paris is on the Seine. My current present-tense belief ‘Paris is on the Seine’ was caused by my present-tense belief a minute ago ‘Paris is on the Seine’; I have received no independent confirmation. There is no deductive connection between the two beliefs; it is logically possible for the earlier belief to be true and the later belief false, the Seine having cataclysmically altered its course. Nevertheless, if no such cataclysm has been a serious possibility, my later belief that Paris is on the Seine does constitute knowledge. The present fact that Paris is on the Seine and my present belief that it is are connected, in a way sufficient for knowledge, with their common cause in the past fact that Paris was on the Seine some time ago. This is not to assume that knowledge is definable in causal terms; what is plausible is that the common cause plays a central role in these specific cases of knowledge.

Recall (III). No doubt my belief at time $\mathrm{\tau}$ that someone is both dancing and singing is caused by my beliefs at $\mathrm{\tau}-\mathrm{\partial}$ that Judy is dancing and that Judy is singing, not by the corresponding beliefs at $\mathrm{\tau}$ itself; causation is not instantaneous. The truth of the cause beliefs does not strictly imply the truth of the effect belief. Nevertheless, in most circumstances in which the present-tensed beliefs at $\mathrm{\tau}$ that Judy is dancing and that Judy is singing would constitute knowledge, even if they were caused by the corresponding present tensed beliefs at $\mathrm{\tau}-\mathrm{\partial}$, rather than by subsequent perception of Judy, the inferred belief at $\mathrm{\tau}$ that someone is both dancing and singing also constitutes knowledge. Although the time-specific belief states by which it was caused are not those to which it is deductively related, this mismatch does not undermine knowledge in favourable circumstances.

Where does all this leave the notion of sense? Valid arguments are constituted of sentence types. If one explained sense in terms of validity as Campbell does, then sense would become a property of sentence types, not tokens. ‘I am hungry’ would express a single sense, irrespective of the speaker. If the sentence expresses different beliefs when uttered at different times or by different speakers, then senses would not be the objects of belief (compare Kaplan’s claim at p. 539 of ‘Demonstratives’ in Themes from Kaplan, op. cit., that characters, not contents, are the bearers of logical truth). Sense would no longer determine reference. That notion of sense is blind to the preservation of reference across context, the object of Campbell’s study.

Has too much been expected of the notion of validity? We lack clear intuitions about the validity of inferences involving context-dependent expressions in different contexts. This is not to deny all connection between validity and sense. Since any reasonable notion of sense should determine a reasonable notion of validity, constraints on validity imply constraints on sense. If the notion of sense is required to invalidate the inference from ‘Hesperus is bright’ to ‘Phosphorus is bright’, then ‘Hesperus’ and ‘Phosphorus’ must be assigned different senses. It often seems clear that the questions ‘Does this logically follow from that?’ and ‘Does this have the same sense as that?’ should have the same answer, but in many of those cases it is unclear what that answer should be. Similarly, considerations of validity give no easy answer to the question whether ‘I’ as used by the same subject at different times expresses the same sense. Logic does not need a general notion of sense.

The felt need for a distinction between sense and reference is connected with a felt need for a distinction between a priori and a posteriori knowledge. The dependence of reasoning on memory points up obscurities in both distinctions.
