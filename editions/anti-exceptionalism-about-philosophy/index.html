Anti-exceptionalism about philosophy

Timothy Williamson

Croatian Journal of Philosophy 13 (2013): 1–3.

Abstract

I briefly rehearse the positive conception of philosophy in my book The Philosophy of Philosophy, as an introduction to the symposium on it that follows.

Keywords: Metaphilosophy, metalinguistic, abduction, axiom, thought experiment, counterfactual, imagination, a priori

Philosophers tend to regard philosophy as exceptional amongst disciplines, just as the practitioners of most other disciplines tend to regard their own as exceptional. No two disciplines are exactly alike, but we should not exaggerate the differences. Philosophy resembles other disciplines much more closely than many philosophers like to suppose.

It is not the case that philosophical questions differ in kind from questions in all other disciplines. They are not to be interpreted in some special way. Rather, they are normally to be taken at face value. For example, the question ‘What is justice?’ is primarily a question about the nature of justice itself, not about the word ‘justice’ or about our concept of justice. Although there are philosophical questions about words and concepts too, they have no special privilege. Philosophers tend to ask about very general and non-contingent matters, but are not alone in doing so. The main aim of philosophy is to know the answers to its questions.

Like mathematics, philosophy is an armchair discipline. The reason is not that observation and experiment are in principle irrelevant to answering philosophical questions. They are always at least indirectly relevant in principle, and sometimes in practice, just as they are in mathematics. However, as the case of mathematics shows, that does not mean that reforming the discipline to concentrate on making observations and carrying out experiments would be an improvement, even if we outsourced that work to the natural sciences. We have ample experience that some problems are most effectively tackled by more theoretical methods.

The central role of rigorous deductive proof in mathematics, compared to its rather marginal role in philosophy, might seem to make the mathematical precedent irrelevant for philosophy. However, abductive methods also play a significant role in mathematics, in the justification of its axioms. To a first approximation, contemporary mathematics is built up from the Zermelo-Fraenkel system of set theory with the axiom of choice (ZFC). A large part of mathematicians’ justification for implicitly or explicitly relying on ZFC in their proofs is that the axioms of ZFC constitute a simple, elegant, unitary basis from which the standard theorems of all the less abstract branches of mathematics can be derived and which has never been found inconsistent with anything we know. Proposed additional axioms for set theory, to mitigate its incompleteness and resolve mathematical problems undecidable in ZFC (such as Cantor’s continuum hypothesis) are evaluated in a similar way. The abductive aspect of mathematical methodology is a significant precedent for philosophy. As the mathematical precedent shows, the use of abductive methods does not require philosophy to become a natural science.

In philosophy, the data for abductive arguments are often supplied by fictional cases and thought experiments. Such data are also typically used as counterexamples to rival philosophical theories. For let S be the hypothetical scenario as initially described in more or less neutral terms, and S* our verdict, our final description of the scenario in the terms at issue (such as ‘just’ or ‘know’). When things go well, we come to know by reflection on such cases that S could have obtained, and that if S had obtained then S* would also have obtained. We can therefore conclude that S* could have obtained. Thus we have a counterexample to any philosophical theory that entails the metaphysical impossibility of S*, and we may have abductive evidence in favour of a philosophical theory that entails the metaphysical possibility of S*. To acquire such evidence, we must be capable of knowing ascriptions of metaphysical modality (such as ‘S could have obtained’) and counterfactual conditionals (such as ‘If S had obtained then S* would have obtained’). Since the metaphysical modalities are definable in terms of counterfactual conditionals, what matters here is fundamentally the epistemology of the latter.

Our capacity to know counterfactual conditionals is not distinctively philosophical. In everyday life, most of them are highly contingent and particular. However, the very same cognitive capacities that enable us to evaluate everyday counterfactual conditionals with some degree of reliability are also applicable to philosophically relevant counterfactual conditionals. Indeed, the two classes are not far apart. For instance, the initial scenario S for thought experiments in epistemology and ethics is often not at all science fictional. Thus it is hard to maintain scepticism about philosophical thought experiments without extending it to an implausibly extreme scepticism about our evaluation of counterfactual conditionals in general. Sometimes we can even substitute real life cases for fictional ones; doing so does not significantly strengthen or weaken the philosophical argument. In coming to a verdict on the real life cases, we apply our cognitive capacity to make reliable online judgments with the term at issue (‘just’, ‘know’). In coming to a verdict on the fictional cases, we apply a corresponding cognitive capacity to make reliable offline judgments with the same term in the scope of a hypothetical supposition. We get from the former capacity to the latter by an exercise of the imagination in default mode to develop the initial stipulation (S) in a non-stipulative way permeated by background knowledge, which is closely related to such ability as we have to predict the future. The distinction between a priori and a posteriori knowledge reveals itself as quite unhelpful when it comes to classifying such cases; it fails to cut at an epistemological joint.

Naturally, the form of cognitive processing just described is highly fallible, like all other general forms of human cognitive processing, such as perception and memory. But, like those other forms, in favourable conditions it provides knowledge. Any truth we happen to know forms part of our total evidence and can be used as such. We cannot expect our evidence in philosophy to be scepticism-proof in principle, any more than we can elsewhere in philosophy. Nor can we expect it to be philosophically neutral: challenges to purported evidence are far from unusual in science.

Controversy about basics, such as underived rules of inference, is endemic to philosophy. Typically, it cannot be dissolved by pre-theoretic clarification, because what presents itself as such clarification turns out to rely implicitly on contestable tacit theoretical assumptions. In particular, although disambiguation is sometimes needed, in philosophy as elsewhere, it does not take us very far, for philosophers who differ in the inferential rules they accept for a given term of natural language are typically not using it in different senses. Rather, all of them are linguistically competent with it and use it with its standard sense in the public language, in which meaning is socially determined. Their disagreement is at the level of the object language. Even in logic, the fundamental disputes are not metalinguistic or metaconceptual. But none of this constitutes an insuperable barrier to progress in philosophy. We can develop rival philosophical theories and compare them by the same overall abductive standards that are used elsewhere in science: strength, explanatory power, simplicity, elegance, and consistency with what we know.
