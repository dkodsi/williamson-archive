# Clarification by theory construction

Timothy Williamson

In Dirk Quadflieg, Dina Emundts, and Karen Koch (eds.), Das Selbstverständnis der Philosophie und ihr Verhältnis zu den anderen Wissenschaften: Stuttgarter Hegel-Kongress 2023 (2025), 563–576. Frankfurt: Klostermann.

# Introduction

According to one influential strand of twentieth-century philosophy, the proper activity for philosophers is not constructing theories but clarifying concepts. Both Wittgenstein and Carnap held versions of such a view. For the later Wittgenstein the clarification was to be done in an ordinary natural language, and was to leave the concepts just as they were, though no longer misunderstood, whereas for Carnap it was to be done by means of an artificial formal language, and was to improve and reconstruct the concepts. However, the purpose of this paper is not to clarify the concept of clarifying a concept. For definiteness, we will start with a familiar simple model of conceptual clarification as conceptual analysis, and use it to see why the term ›concept‹ and its associated ideology are not fit for purpose. Clarification will turn out not to be an alternative to theory construction, but rather something best achieved by theory construction.

# Clarification by Analysis?

Questions of the form ›What is X?‹ go back to the origins of philosophy. On the Socratic (or at least Platonic) model, one starts with a question such as ›What is justice?‹ or ›What is knowledge?‹ Minimally, such questions generate a mini-dialogue like this:

Q: What is X?
A: YZ….

Here ›X‹ is normally replaced by a simple expression such as ›justice‹ or ›knowledge‹, which does not wear its semantic structure on its sleeve, while ›YZ…‹ is normally replaced by a complex expression, an account of what justice or knowledge is, since a one-word answer would be unsatisfying. All this amounts to the equation ›X = YZ…‹, an elementary model of analysis.

In contemporary philosophy, prototypes of analysis are typically divided into two kinds, which might be labelled ›analytic‹ and ›synthetic‹.

This question / answer exchange corresponds to a prototype of the ›analytic‹ side:

Q: What is a bachelor?
A: An unmarried man.

The corresponding analysis is ›A bachelor is an unmarried man‹ or ›To be a bachelor is to be an unmarried man‹. That is regarded as a conceptual analysis, and as knowable a priori. The conceptual analysis is supposed to reveal the structure of the concept bachelor, not the structure of the bachelor himself: the concept bachelor is composed of the constituent concepts unmarried and man, but the bachelor himself is not composed of being unmarried and manhood.

A different question / answer exchange corresponds to a prototype of the ›synthetic‹ side:

Q: What is water?
A: $\mathrm{H_2O}$.

The corresponding analysis is ›Water is $\mathrm{H_2O}$‹. That is regarded as a scientific analysis, not a conceptual one, and as knowable only a posteriori. The scientific analysis is supposed to reveal the structure of water itself, not the structure of the concept water: water itself is composed of the constituent elements hydrogen and oxygen, but the ordinary concept water is not composed of the constituent concepts hydrogen and oxygen.

Unfortunately, there is no corresponding difference in form between ›analytic‹ and ›synthetic‹ analyses. In questions of the form ›What is X?‹, ›X‹ does not usually refer to a concept. Justice is a virtue, water is a substance, and a bachelor is a person; virtues, substances, and persons are not concepts, although there can of course be concepts of virtues, substances, and persons. If you want an analysis of the concept justice or of the concept water, you should explicitly ask ›What is the concept justice?‹ or ›What is the concept water?‹ Although philosophers who ask questions like ›What is justice?‹ or ›What is knowledge?‹ are often interpreted as implicitly asking about the corresponding concepts, the fidelity of those interpretations is doubtful.

According to the traditional view, the concept bachelor and the concept unmarried man are identical, while the concept water and the concept $H_2O$ are distinct. The analysis ›A bachelor is an unmarried man‹ is regarded as an informal presentation of the concept identity ›The concept bachelor = the concept unmarried man‹ and is treated as a (very elementary) prototype of clarification. The analysis ›Water is $\mathrm{H_2O}$‹ is not regarded as a presentation of the (mistaken) concept identity ›The concept water = the concept $H_2O$‹ and is not treated as a case of clarification. How deep the difference between the two cases really goes is not obvious.

# The Individuation of Concepts

An immediate concern with the two prototypes of analysis is that the ›bachelor‹ prototype is conceptual but trivial, while the ›water‹ prototype is non-trivial but non-conceptual, so neither of them provides a model of non-trivial conceptual analysis. If the method of philosophy is supposed to be conceptual analysis, that is bad news for philosophy.

It gets worse, for the ›bachelor‹ analysis is trivial because it is conceptual. The point is quite general. Suppose that the concept X = the concept YZ…. Then the expressions ›X‹ and ›YZ…‹ are synonymous, because they express the same concept. Thus, the identity sentence ›X = YZ…‹ is synonymous with the trivial identity ›YZ… = YZ…‹, by the compositionality of meaning. But a sentence synonymous with a trivial truth is itself a trivial truth, so the identity ›X = YZ…‹ is itself trivial. This is a version of C. H. Langford’s ›paradox of analysis‹: a candidate analysis is correct only if trivial (Langford 1942).

The paradox is specific to conceptual analysis. It does not arise for the analysis of water, for the chemical term ›$\mathrm{H_2O}$‹ has the chemical terms ›H‹ and ›O‹ as semantic constituents, which is quite different from the semantic structure of the ordinary natural kind term ›water‹. Thus, no analogous argument shows that ›water‹ and ›$\mathrm{H_2O}$‹ are synonymous if coreferential or that ›Water = $\mathrm{H_2O}$‹ is trivial if true.

The paradox of analysis depends on individuating concepts by something like cognitive significance. More precisely, where $E_{\mathrm{i}}$ is the concept expressed by the term ›$\mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{i}}$‹, concepts are abstracted from linguistic expressions by this abstraction principle:

concepts
The concept $E_1$ = the concept $E_2$ if and only if ›$\mathrm{E}_1$‹ and ›$\mathrm{E}_2$‹ are exactly alike in cognitive significance.

Thus, if the identity sentence ›YZ… = YZ…‹ is trivial while the identity sentence ›X = YZ…‹ is non-trivial, then the two sentences differ in exact cognitive significance, which requires the terms ›X‹ and ›YZ…‹ to differ in exact cognitive significance (otherwise substituting one for the other would make no cognitive difference), in which case by concepts the concept X is distinct from the concept YZ…. concepts is reminiscent of Frege’s individuation of senses by cognitive significance. Thus, it makes concepts something like Fregean senses.

One trouble with concepts is that it tends to reduce the identity of concepts to the identity of words. For consider any two synonymous words, such as ›furze‹ and ›gorse‹, which are simply two natural kind terms for the very same kind of shrub. Thus, one might expect them to express the very same concept of that natural kind. But a native speaker of English, a rational person competent with both words by normal linguistic standards, might on reflection have slight doubts as to the identity ›Furze = gorse‹, but no doubts as to the identity ›Furze = furze‹. This would not require them to associate different descriptions with the two words. They might even link both words to the same mental file. They may just consciously think ›Perhaps the two words differ in extension over some case I can’t think of right now; perhaps they are linked to different mental files.‹ Although such doubts may be over-scrupulous, they are not downright irrational. Thus, the sentences ›Furze = gorse‹ and ›Furze = furze‹ differ in exact cognitive significance, so, by the same sort of reasoning as before, the words ›furze‹ and ›gorse‹ also differ in exact cognitive difference. Therefore, by concepts, the concept furze ≠ the concept gorse. But this difference in concepts is just an epistemological corollary of the difference in words. One can construct an analogous argument about any other two synonyms, for the point does not rely on anything special about the synonymy of ›furze‹ and ›gorse‹. Consequently, the level of concepts becomes redundant, a useless intermediary between words and their referents.

The argument may extend to a single word as used by a competent speaker at different times. For let ›$\mathrm{E}_{\text{today}}$‹ be ›E‹ as they use it today, and ›$\mathrm{E}_{\text{yesterday}}$‹ be ›E‹ as they used it yesterday. In fact, we may suppose, there is no difference in use. Nevertheless, the speaker could on reflection have slight doubts as to the identity ›$\mathrm{E}_{\text{today}} = \mathrm{E}_{\text{yesterday}}$‹, but no analogous doubts as to the identity ›$\mathrm{E}_{\text{today}}
= \mathrm{E}_{\text{today}}$‹. They may think ›Perhaps the two uses determine subtly different extensions in some way I’m not aware of right now‹. Again, such doubts may be over-scrupulous, but they are not downright irrational. Thus, the sentences ›$\mathrm{E}_{\text{today}} = \mathrm{E}_{\text{yesterday}}$‹ and ›$\mathrm{E}_{\text{today}} = \mathrm{E}_{\text{today}}$‹ differ in exact cognitive significance, so, reasoning as before, ›$\mathrm{E}_{\text{today}}$‹ and ›$\mathrm{E}_{\text{yesterday}}$‹ also differ in exact cognitive difference. Therefore, by concepts, the concept $E_{\text{today}}$ ≠ the concept $E_{\text{yesterday}}$. But this difference in concepts is just an epistemological corollary of the difference in time. One can make the same argument about any expression as used at different times.

Similarly, the argument may extend to a single word as used by two competent speakers at the same time. For let ›$\mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{I}}$‹ be ›E‹ as I use it, and ›$\mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{you}}$‹ be ›E‹ as you use it. In fact, we may suppose, there is no difference in use. Nevertheless, I could on reflection have slight doubts as to the identity ›$\mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{I}} = \mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{you}}$‹, but no doubts as to the identity ›$\mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{I}} = \mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{I}}$‹. I may think ›Perhaps the two uses determine subtly different extensions in some way I’m not aware of right now‹. Yet again, such doubts may be over-scrupulous, but they are not downright irrational. Thus, the sentences ›$\mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{I}} = \mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{you}}$‹ and ›$\mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{I}} = \mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{I}}$‹ differ in exact cognitive significance, so, reasoning as before, ›$\mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{I}}$‹ and ›$\mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{you}}$‹ also differ in exact cognitive difference. Therefore, by concepts, the concept $E_{\mathrm{I}}$ ≠ the concept $E_{\mathrm{you}}$. But this difference in concepts is just an epistemological corollary of the difference in person. One can make the same argument about any expression used by different people.

The upshot of those two arguments is that, given concepts, concepts cannot be shared by different people, and cannot be retained by a single person over time.

The resort to concepts or Fregean senses illustrates a more general strategy for understanding cognition by postulating a cognitively transparent ›realm of reason‹ for which there is no distinction between appearance and reality, and all rational relations are transparent to the thinker. Another instance of this strategy is Bertrand Russell’s resort to sense data or sensibilia with which we are supposed to be acquainted in such an intimate way that illusions of existence or of identity or distinctness cannot arise. He articulated the underlying rationale in his Principle of Acquaintance: ›Every proposition which we can understand must be composed wholly of constituents with which we are acquainted‹ (Russell 1910–11, his italics). Such magical thinking creates a redundant level of middle management, mediating unhelpfully between words and their referents. We can theorize better by dispensing with such a category in our theorizing. There is no cognitively transparent level between words and their referents.

Of course, concepts is not the only conceivable way of individuating postulated entities called ›concepts‹. For example, one sometimes hears the suggestion that the concept expressed by a term is the explanation given of the term. However, that suggestion does not help. For example, the word ›bachelor‹ is typically explained as ›unmarried man‹. But either the explanation is identified with the words ›unmarried man‹ themselves, or with the concept they express. The first horn of the dilemma identifies concepts with words and so makes talk of ›concepts‹ redundant. The other horn of the dilemma is blatantly circular as an account of what ›concepts‹ are. Either way, we are no further forward.

Naturally, far more can be said about concepts and their individuation than has been said here. To cut a long story short, no clear principle has emerged from the literature on conceptual analysis as the working standard for individuating concepts.

Without a clear standard for individuating concepts, we have no clear standard for the success of a proposed conceptual analysis. The ideology of ›concepts‹ is far more in need of clarification than are the familiar terms of ordinary language to which it has so often been applied, though without conspicuous success. After all, philosophically central words like ›know‹ and ›cause‹ (or ›make‹) have much better-established uses than ›concept‹, which philosophers use as a term of art, a theoretical term not backed up by much in the way of theory.

Indeed, nothing in the use of most words for which philosophers seek conceptual analyses suggests that they have complex analyses. On pain of circularity or infinite regress, not all words have complex conceptual analyses; no plausible reason is on offer to exclude philosophically central words from those which do not. For example, words like ›know‹ and ›make‹ are typically not applied on the basis of complex reasoning. Moreover, attempts to provide conceptual analyses for philosophically central words of natural language have a long track-record of failure. When non-circular, they are normally subject to counterexamples, failing even to provide necessary and sufficient conditions. Philosophers’ quest for conceptual analyses is a degenerating research programme.

# Conceptual Clarification Without Conceptual Analysis?

In a much looser sense of ›analysis‹, conceptual analysis may consist in elucidating ›conceptual connections‹ amongst terms. Such connections may also be described as ›analytic‹, even if they involve no breaking down of a whole into its constituent parts. For example, relations of colour inclusion and exclusion are often so described. Every shade of scarlet is a shade of red but no shade of green is a shade of red, even though definitions of the colour terms are not expected to reduce those relations to truths of pure logic. Such conceptual connections are closer to what Carnap and the later Wittgenstein in their different ways had in mind by conceptual clarification than are conceptual analyses in the strict form of definitions. They present a more feasible aim for philosophical inquiry, and they are less vulnerable to the paradox of analysis.

However, labelling some declarative sentences ›Conceptual Connections‹ is clarificatory only if the label is itself clear. When does a declarative sentence express a conceptual connection? In the tradition of Carnap and Wittgenstein, conceptual connections are in effect rules constitutive of the relevant language. For a competent speaker, rejecting them amounts to an attempt to change the language. I have argued extensively against this picture of language elsewhere, and will not repeat the details here (Williamson 2007). There is no good linguistic evidence that any sentence has that privileged status. For any declarative sentence S, a competent speaker of the language can, on sophisticated theoretical grounds, come to reject S in its current sense as mistaken. However crazy their rejection of S may sound on first hearing, they can make it clear that they are using the words with their usual public meanings, and accusing those who accept S of some more or less subtle error. A language is a forum for disagreement. Linguistic competence is not a matter of doctrinal orthodoxy. The point is borne out by the history of alternative logics over the past century. Every familiar principle of classical logic has been challenged on some philosophical ground or other, by competent speakers of the language; for instance, they may blame it for some paradox. In particular, dialetheists such as Graham Priest respond to paradoxes by asserting contradictions like ›The set of all sets that are not members of themselves is and is not a member of itself‹. In my view, all these challenges to classical logic are themselves mistaken, but the point is that they can intelligibly be made in standard English or any other natural language, by evidently competent speakers of the language.1 To insist on interpreting non-classical logicians otherwise is, in most cases, hermeneutically perverse. Since elementary principles of logic are normally treated as paradigms of conceptual connections, non-logical candidates for that status do at least as badly.

The view that clarifying concepts rather than advancing theses is the proper business of philosophy mandates a kind of dogmatism observable in its proponents. For if they start providing evidence for what they say, they in effect concede that they are advancing theses for which a reasonable person might expect supporting evidence. Their own view obliges them to regard their opponents as deeply confused, not merely mistaken.

The picture of philosophy as clarifying concepts also manifests itself in the claim that philosophy provides understanding rather than knowledge. This alleged contrast between understanding and knowledge is questionable. The standard way of supporting it is to point out ›You can know that something is the case without understanding why it is the case‹. But the operative contrast there is between ›that‹ and ›why‹, not between ›know‹ and ›understand‹. After all, one can equally well point out ›You can know that something is the case without knowing why it is the case‹. Someone who does not know why something is the case does not understand why it is the case, and someone who does not understand why something is the case does not know why it is the case. The identity of propositional understanding with propositional knowing can be argued for in detail.2

Of course, one can know someone without understanding them. For example, although one has often met them, one does not understand why they behave the way they do, but then one also does not know why they behave that way. Similarly, one can understand someone without knowing them. For example, although one has never met them, one understands why they behave the way they do (by report), but then one also knows why they behave that way. Admittedly, the word ›understand‹ is associated more strongly with some kinds of knowledge than with others, especially with knowing why and knowing how, and stereotypes of ›knowing‹ differ from stereotypes of ›understanding‹. Such differences can easily give the unwary the impression of a fundamental semantic difference between ›understand‹ and ›know‹, and a corresponding fundamental difference between cognitive relations, but that impression is not borne out on more careful inspection.

One might wonder how the idea of philosophy as conceptual clarification came to be so influential, especially given its own unclarity. An immediate point is that the unclarity became obvious only gradually. The initial picture seemed clear enough to be getting on with, with impressive historical antecedents, and one could hope that residual difficulties would be cleared up as things developed. But as time passed, and the anomalies proved impervious to all attempts at treatment, optimism has looked increasingly out of touch with reality.

The doctrine of philosophy as conceptual clarification also spoke to deeper anxieties caused by the progress of modern science. What does it leave for the a priori methods of philosophy to achieve? If philosophy finds itself competing with the natural and social sciences to answer the same questions, but without going to the trouble of doing experiments or making measurements, it is playing a losing game. By contrast, philosophy could derive reassurance as well as humility from its self-image as sharpening intellectual instruments for the sciences to use, or at least as sweeping rubbish out of the sciences’ way. It would be doing useful if not glamorous work, a deposed emperor finding employment in the gardens of what was once his palace.

One might wonder, though, how much call there would really be for philosophy’s services. Although things can often be made clearer, perfect clarity is surely an unattainable ideal. A more realistic aim is clarity enough for working purposes. But those best-placed to judge how much clarity and of what kind is enough for working purposes are presumably those who have to do the work – the scientists themselves. Although philosophy sometimes helps, one may expect most of the clarification to be done within the sciences as they go along: not in contrast to theory construction, but as part of it.

We can develop this line of thought by considering the clearest and most precise of all the sciences: mathematics.

# Mathematical Clarity

Fully explicit definitions of theoretical terms seem to play a larger role in mathematics than in any other science. Most of these definitions are stipulative, though far from arbitrary. For example, in algebra, the definitions of terms for kinds of structure such as ›group‹, ›ring‹, and ›field‹ do not purport to concern groups, rings, or fields in the ordinary senses of those terms.

Some definitions in mathematics make precise already extant mathematical terms. For example, Weierstrass’s famous $\mathrm{\delta}$,$\mathrm{\varepsilon}$-definition of ›continuous function‹ replaced vaguer ways of using the same term, to which it was as faithful as was compatible with precision. In the 1930s, various different ways were proposed by Alonzo Church, Alan Turing, and others, of making the vague-seeming term ›computable function‹ precise; they all turned out to demarcate the same class of functions, indicating that a mathematically natural kind had been identified. Such a newly defined term may have the same extension as its predecessor, but it is not intended to have the same cognitive significance: the clarification is intended to improve cognitive significance, not to preserve it. If concepts are individuated by cognitive significance, these are not analyses of concepts, but replacements of unarticulated concepts by articulated ones.

Chains of definition are not infinitely regressive; sooner or later, they come to an end with undefined terms. In modern mathematics, as standardly formalized, that stopping point is the language of set theory. Its primitive expressions are in effect ›set‹ and ›member of‹ (∈). Standardly, they are left as undefined terms and are not intended to capture the pre-mathematical meanings of those words, even in extension. In a typical textbook of set theory, they are introduced by a few vague, informal comments and examples. The work of clarifying them is done not by definitions but by the axioms of a standard system of set theory, such as ZFC (Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory with the Axiom of Choice). For example, the Powerset Axiom says that for every set s, there is a set whose members are all and only the subsets of s. These axioms are not ›implicit definitions‹ or ›implicit analyses‹ of the terms ›set‹ or the symbol ∈, and cannot be reworked into that form without circularity. Formally, the axioms by themselves do not determine unique referents for the symbols. Someone might say that the axioms implicitly define the expression ›model of ZFC‹, but not even that would help, for the term ›model‹ is itself defined in set-theoretic terms.

Indeed, an independent-minded mathematician could in principle deny any single axiom of ZFC in its current sense on elaborate but eccentric theoretical grounds, without revealing a lack of linguistic competence in the current language of set theory or implicitly proposing a change in its semantics. Presumably, the mathematician’s grounds would be more or less deeply mistaken, but theoretical error is not linguistic incompetence.

Although the axioms of ZFC (or any other standard set theory) do not tell us what a set is, or what it is to be a member of a given set, they do enable us to reason about sets and their members in very powerful ways, and for mathematical purposes that is what we need. They clarify much – though not everything – about how sets are collectively organized. Moreover, since ZFC is a formal theory (though an interpreted one), there is a clear formal decision procedure to determine in a finite number of steps whether something is a correct proof of a theorem in the formal theory, although there is no formal decision procedure to determine in a finite number of steps whether a given formula is provable in the theory (we cannot survey all potential proofs in a finite time to determine that a formula is not provable). For most mathematical purposes, that is all the clarity we need.

There are also a few informal accounts of what sets are, such as the iterative conception, on which sets are built up from non-sets in stages, in an indefinitely iterated ›process‹; at each stage, one builds all sets of what one already has. Such an informal account is not a proto-definition or proto-analysis but simply a proto-theory. It is heavily metaphorical and far from clear except in loose outline. In practice, there is typically much unclarity and dispute as to what does or does not follow from such an informal story, by contrast with the formal theory itself. The informal conception is not what does the heavy lifting in mathematics; it is more like a picturesque synopsis of the axioms.

Set theory illustrates the method of clarification by theory construction. The clarification is not at the level of the term ›set‹ or the symbol ∈, but at the level of the theory as a whole. It gives a clear picture of the structure of the hierarchy of the sets. Of course, a picture can be clear without being accurate, just as a statement can be precise without being true. But the clarity of a picture facilitates testing it for accuracy, just as the precision of a statement facilitates testing it for truth. That is as it should be in science. Admittedly, the picture of the structure of the hierarchy of sets drawn by formal set theory is also provably incomplete (if consistent), for example in neither proving nor refuting Cantor’s Continuum Hypothesis, but it is still informative enough for almost all ordinary mathematical purposes. The clarity of mathematics in general depends on the clarity of set theory.

Of course, set theory is a branch of mathematics, and philosophy is not reducible to mathematics. Nevertheless, there are many similarities between set theory and theories in fundamental metaphysics. First, the theory is formulated in a language whose distinctive basic terms are treated as too fundamental to be further defined. Second, the theory is at the highest level of generality. For instance, the Pairing Axiom tells us that for any things x and y there is the pair set {x, y} of which they are the only members. In pure set theory, the axiom applies only to pure sets x and y, where a set is pure if and only if all its members are, but in impure set theory a set can have ur-elements (non-sets) as members, and the Pairing Axiom applies to any things x and y whatsoever. Third, the theory is implicitly treated as metaphysically necessary. For example, one can use set theory just as one can use less basic mathematics in reasoning about any metaphysically possible situation. Fourth, the epistemology of set theory is treated as more or less a priori. Laboratory experiments and perceptual observations are not considered relevant.

In all those respects, formal set theories resemble formal theories of mereology, which give a moderately clear picture of the structure of part-whole relations. Although set theory is normally considered a branch of mathematics, while mereology is normally considered a branch of metaphysics, for good historical and sociological reasons, and those affiliations have obviously marked the development of both fields, the manifest differences should not be allowed to obscure the strong underlying similarities.

The case of set theory helps bringing out just how inadequate and misleading the old dichotomy of the ›empirical‹ and the ›conceptual‹ is. To try to reduce the epistemology of set theory to the empirical or the conceptual, or some hybrid of the two, is to misunderstand very badly what is going on. The same goes for the attempt to reduce the epistemology of mereology to the empirical or the conceptual, or some hybrid of the two. Properly to understand such abstract theorizing in both mathematics and philosophy, one must let go of such lingering traces of logical empiricism, and take a closer, fresher look at what theorizing in different fields, including philosophy, mathematics, and the natural sciences, is actually like.

# Clarification by Theory Construction in Philosophy

Clarification by theory construction is a realistic and productive alternative aim for philosophers in their attempt to clarify basic matters without futile attempts to analyse them in even more basic terms. Although philosophical theories rarely attain the same level of formalization as set theory, they may still achieve significant progress in analogous ways.

One might worry that many areas of philosophy do not lend themselves to the kinds of informative exceptionless universal generalization typical of mathematical theories, because they concern phenomena too messy, complicated, and unruly to conform to such neat schemata. More specifically, at least as pursued by human philosophers, they concern the human world, and ›of the crooked timber of humanity no straight thing was ever made.‹ For example, although epistemology sometimes deals with the knowledge and belief of non-human animals, robots, AI, and ideally rational agents, it mainly concerns human knowledge and belief. Similarly, the philosophy of mind and language mainly concerns human mind and language, moral and political philosophy mainly concerns human action and society, the philosophy of science mainly concerns human science, the philosophy of art mainly concerns human art, and so on. Although metaphysics takes a wider view of reality as a whole, of which human reality is just one tiny part, some topics in metaphysics are more restricted: thus, the metaphysics of personal identity mainly concerns the identity of human persons.

Many branches of science deal with complicated, messy phenomena: not just the human world but the biological world more generally, and beyond that the macroscopic world – for example, geology and astronomy. Even at the microscopic level, many systems are too complicated for their behaviour to be rigorously derived from exceptionless first principles. Scientists have learnt how to theorize about such messy phenomena without abandoning mathematical rigour, by constructing mathematical models of the phenomena, and rigorously investigating their consequences. The model provides a clear but highly simplified picture of the complicated, messy target phenomena: as if it were a cartoon. For example, planets are treated mathematically as point masses, of course with no suggestion that planets really are point masses. The relation between the cartoon and the phenomena themselves is less clear – some kind of very loose approximation – but not so unclear as to prevent many models from providing deep insights into their target phenomena, as the track record of model-building in many sciences amply demonstrates.3 Scientific progress often consists in the development of better and better models of the target, rather than the discovery of exceptionless general laws about it – at the relevant level, there may be no such laws.

The model-building methodology may often be appropriate for philosophical theorizing too, especially when it targets the human world. In some areas, it has already been adopted. For example, decision theory and game theory work mainly with highly simplified formal models, such as Prisoner’s Dilemma. They have already provided considerable clarification in both moral and political philosophy, even though one cannot expect such formal methods to do all the work by themselves. Similarly, formal epistemology in the traditions of both Bayesian probability theory and epistemic logic relies on a formal model-building epistemology, and is gradually becoming more closely integrated with mainstream discursive epistemology. Many puzzling epistemic situations are too subtle to be understood without the clarification provided by a formal model. In the philosophy of language, simplified formal models are used to clarify the dynamics of conversation and its participants’ evolving common ground. As in other sciences, progress in philosophy can, and sometimes does, consist in the development of better and better simple models of the target phenomena, with no discovery of exceptionless general laws.4

Despite such examples, the understanding and application of a model-building methodology has not yet spread very far in philosophy. We may therefore expect that many opportunities for its use have so far been missed. In the future, much clarification in philosophy is likely to take this model-building form.
