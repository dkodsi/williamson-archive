# Review of A.W. Moore, Points of View

Timothy Williamson

Philosophical Books 40 (1) (1999): 43–45.

The Basic Argument of Points of View starts from the Basic Assumption that ‘‘Representations are representations of what is there anyway’’ (p. 68, following Bernard Williams) and reaches the conclusion that ‘‘Absolute representations are possible’’ (p. 74, capitals and italics Moore’s). What does Moore mean?

Moore conscientiously defines most of his terms. ‘‘A representation is anything which has content and which, because of its content, is either true or false’’ (p. 283). He does not define ‘‘anyway’’. If the Basic Assumption meant that representations are representations of what would have been there even if the representing had not occurred, it would succumb to obvious counterexamples (‘I am speaking’). Moore intends something else; the Assumption ‘‘involves a cluster of interrelated ideas about the unity, substantiality and autonomy of reality’’ (p. 74). Perhaps it just means whatever it has to mean in order to validate the Basic Argument. As for the Argument’s conclusion: ‘‘A representation is absolute iff it is from no point of view…. Equivalently, a representation is absolute iff it can be integrated by simple addition with any other possible representation’’ (p. 280). To integrate two representations by simple addition is to produce a conjunctive representation whose conjuncts match the originals in content and type, where ‘‘The content of a representation is how things must be if it is true’’ (p. 280) and ‘‘The type of a representation is the role it must play in the psychology of its producer if it expresses a belief of its producer’’ (p. 283). The model for an absolute representation is a non-indexical sentence, but for Moore indexicality is not the only way of being from a point of view. The definition of ‘type’ should clarify this metaphorical extension. Unfortunately, it does not work as intended unless representations are individuated by type in a prior sense of ‘type’; although the notion of linguistic meaning or character provides such a prior sense, it reintroduces the problem of extrapolation from a comparatively clear linguistic phenomenon to something more general and less clear.

The Basic Argument itself is disappointing. Someone who doubted its conclusion might think that sometimes two representations $\mathrm{R}$ and $\mathrm{S}$ are true, yet the points of view from which one can see the content of $\mathrm{R}$ do not overlap the points of view from which one can see the content of $\mathrm{S}$. A way reality is—being both the way that makes $\mathrm{R}$ true and the way that makes $\mathrm{S}$ true—makes both $\mathrm{R}$ and $\mathrm{S}$ true, but this conjunctive way cannot be seen from any point of view. Moore’s response to such suggestions is that the conjunctive way ‘‘comes to nothing if it is not so much as possible to indicate what that way is’’ (p. 69). This is bluff. His opponent, who need not be any kind of verificationist, can reply that reality’s being a certain way comes to exactly the same thing whether or not it is possible to indicate what that way is. The Basic Assumption is too inchoate to help at this point.

Moore interprets Nietzsche, Goodman and Derrida as denying the Basic Assumption. He considers using transcendental idealism to reconcile their views with his by denying the Assumption only transcendentally. Moore rejects this on the plausible grounds that transcendental idealism is incoherent. But since its deep-sounding language continues to attract him, he finds an ingenious way to keep the words while admitting that they are nonsense. He defines ‘‘$A$ is shown that $x$’’ as ‘‘$A$ had ineffable [non-representational] knowledge, and when an attempt is made to put what $A$ knows into words, the result is: $x$’’ (p. 157). Moore argues that what replaces ‘$x$’ is nonsense in this context, and that formulations of transcendental idealism are such nonsense.

Ineffable knowledge is knowledge that is not knowledge-that. Is there such knowledge? The cheap traditional way to argue that there is would be to argue that knowledge-how is not knowledge-that; knowing how to $\mathrm{\phi}$ is just being able to $\mathrm{\phi}$. Moore rejects this way for good reasons, following Paul Snowdon. To lose one’s legs is not to forget how to swim; one might still use one’s knowledge how to swim in teaching others. Conversely, someone who does not know how to break bad news may be only too able to do so (he just does it very badly). Logically and linguistically, ‘know how’ belongs to the same general family as ‘know who/what/which/when/where/why/whether’, as Hintikka has emphasised. They are all ways of describing knowledge-that. To know when to $\mathrm{\phi}$ is, roughly, to know of some time $t$ that $t$ is the time to $\mathrm{\phi}$, to know how to $\mathrm{\phi}$ is, roughly, to know of some way $w$ that $w$ is the way to $\mathrm{\phi}$. No verbal description of $t$ or $w$ is needed; one’s grasp of the propositional content may be distinctively practical (‘$\mathrm{\phi}$ now!’; ‘$\mathrm{\phi}$ like this!’). Nevertheless, Moore argues that some practical knowlege is not knowledge-that. He frequently characterises this knowledge as knowledge-how: ‘‘our receiving the world as we do involves our knowing, ineffably, how to do so’’ (p. 210). This is a mistake. The logico-linguistic argument for assimilating knowing-how to knowing-that permits no such exceptions. Knowing how to receive the world (if there is such knowing) is knowing of some way $w$ that $w$ is the way to receive the world, and is therefore not ineffable in Moore’s sense.

Moore’s prime example of ineffable knowledge is one’s understanding of one’s own idiolect. He claims that it has nothing to answer to; ‘rabbit’ denotes hares in my idiolect if I think it does (p. 184). Work by Burge and others undermines this conception of an idiolect. One is fallible even about one’s own idiolect and one’s own concepts. Since grasping a concept $\mathrm{C}$ is knowing how to exercise $\mathrm{C}$, it is knowing of some way $w$ that $w$ is the way to exercise $\mathrm{C}$, which is effable. So is understanding one’s idiolect. Ineffable knowledge is an illusion. What may have misled Moore is that the knowledge in question is knowledge of necessary truths; noting that reality could not have falsified it, he concludes that it answers to nothing.

Moore finds further examples of ineffable knowledge in Dummettian anti-realism. His discussion depends on the inference from the denial of ‘‘There is at least one proposition whose truth or falsity we cannot determine’’ to ‘‘there cannot be any harm in assuming that every proposition is either true or false’’ (p. 242). Dummett’s anti-realist should reject this inference; intuitionistically, $\boldsymbol{\neg}\boldsymbol{\exists} \mathrm{P}\boldsymbol{\neg}(\mathrm{P} \vee \boldsymbol{\neg}\mathrm{P})$ is consistent with $\boldsymbol{\neg}\boldsymbol{\forall} \mathrm{P}(\mathrm{P} \vee \boldsymbol{\neg}\mathrm{P})$ and there are arguments for both.

Moore holds that ‘‘for any representation, there is a finite extension of our powers enabling us to know whether or not it is true’’; following Joseph Melia, he formulates his anti-realism this way and refuses to say that ‘‘for any true representation, there is a finite extension of our powers enabling us to know that it is true’’ (p. 139) in view of Fitch’s argument that if all truths are knowable then all truths are known. But what seems to motivate his anti-realism is a Dummettian argument which concludes ‘‘the proposition must not be true without our being able to tell that it is’’ (p. 242), the form of anti-realism previously rejected.

The book’s flaws contrast with very many shrewd and subtle points made in passing. If one must listen to the unfresh sirens of transcendental idealism, one should tie oneself to the mast rather more firmly than Moore has done.
