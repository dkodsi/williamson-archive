Précis of Knowledge and its Limits

Timothy Williamson

Philosophy and Phenomenological Research 70 (2) (2005): 431–435.

1 A creature that is not aware of anything does not lead a genuinely intelligent life. Its activity is unintelligent because unguided by (conscious or unconscious) awareness. Although intelligent life does not consist solely of awareness, it is intelligent only where it is intimately related to awareness. Awareness of anything involves some awareness of how things are in some respect. Even awareness merely of how things appear to be is awareness of how they are in respect of appearance. Awareness of how things are is awareness, concerning some way, that they are that way. But awareness that they are that way is knowledge that they are that way. Thus all intelligent life involves an intimate relation to knowledge. The mental states of a creature are the states that make its life intelligent. Consequently, the state of knowing is a mental state; it is central to mentality.

We ascribe states of knowing in the same way as we ascribe other mental states such as perceiving, remembering, believing, desiring and intending, by means of propositional attitude constructions. Knowing resembles perceiving and remembering but differs from believing, desiring and intending in one salient respect: it is factive. People sometimes believe that things are some way although in fact they are not that way, but if one knows that they are that way then they are indeed that way; otherwise one at most believes that one knows that they are that way. Thus knowing is a factive mental state. Arguably, it is the most general factive mental state, in the sense that if one is in any factive mental state with the content that things are a given way then one knows that they are that way.

The function of the cognitive system is to produce knowledge; when it malfunctions it produces mere belief, belief that is defective because it does not constitute knowledge: typically false belief, sometimes true belief by serendipity. If one knows that things are some way, one believes that they are that way; but if one merely believes that they are that way, one does not know that they are that way. Mere believing is to be understood in relation to knowing, misperceiving in relation to perceiving and misremembering in relation to remembering, as malfunctioning is to be understood in relation to successful functioning. In particular, believing is to be understood as a state with similar immediate effects to its special case of knowing. Thus causal explanations of actions in terms of the immediately preceding mental states often appropriately cite states of belief rather than knowledge even when the agent does in fact know. But many actions such as searching, hunting and feeding typically involve complex feedback loops with the environment over extended periods of time. In such cases knowledge and belief at a given time play quite different causally-explanatory roles because they are differently correlated with long-term effects. Mere belief tends to be less stable than knowledge since the misapprehensions on which it rests are liable to exposure. Moreover, in successful intelligent action, the agent does something because it is a good thing to do, in a sense of ‘because’ that is reason-giving and not merely causal. The ‘because’ statement is not elliptical for the causal claim that the agent performed the action because she believed that it was a good thing to do. If the agent did something because it was a good thing to do, then it follows that it was a good thing to do; indeed, on the reason-giving reading of ‘because’ it is plausible that the agent did something because it was a good thing to do only if she knew that it was a good thing to do. If she merely believed that it was a good thing to do, then perhaps in the causal sense of ‘because’ she did it because she believed that it was a good thing to do, and perhaps she believed that she was doing it because it was a good thing to do in the non-causal sense of ‘because’, but the latter belief was false.

The knowledge-centred conception can be extended to propositions the truth-value of which the agent is not in a position to know. For it can be argued that the agent’s total evidence is simply the total content of the agent’s knowledge, so that the probability of a hypothesis on the evidence for the agent is its probability conditional on whatever the agent knows. Given that the norm of credence is to proportion one’s degree of belief to the evidence, the appropriate degree of belief in a hypothesis is in turn its probability conditional on whatever the agent knows. Thus knowledge sets the standard for degrees of belief as well as for outright belief. Knowledge is what justifies belief.

This conception can be extended from thought to language through the analogy between belief and assertion. The corresponding rule for assertion is that one should assert that something is the case only if one knows that it is the case. Thus knowledge sets the standard for assertion too.

The picture just sketched, with resolute avoidance of any but a broad brush, can be filled in with considerable detail, particularly through the use of the probability calculus to analyse aspects both of the causal explanation of action and of the relation between theory and evidence, and supported by far more specific argument at crucial points. Nevertheless, it remains highly controversial. The different sources of opposition help to bring out distinctive features of the knowledge-centred conception.

(1) One source of opposition is internalism in the philosophy of mind. For internalists, purely mental states supervene on what is internal to the agent’s mental life (intrinsic brain states or perhaps qualia) in the sense that agents who are internal duplicates are in exactly the same mental states. Since knowing is factive, knowing something about the external environment does not supervene on the internal and is therefore not a purely mental state by the internalist standard. Internalists view it as a hybrid of purely mental states and conditions on the external environment, thereby marginalizing it in their conception of the mind. However, internalism is already under pressure on other sides. There are strong independent arguments for the semantic externalist view that the content of propositional attitudes can vary between internal duplicates, because what agents are thinking about constitutively depends on what they have causally interacted with. Thus most propositional attitudes to given contents are not purely mental states by the internalist standard: not even paradigm mental states of believing, desiring or intending count. Although internalists may postulate a core of purely mental states that supervene on the internal and are not adequately represented by natural languages, the reasons to believe in such a core are flimsy. They are supposed to play a privileged role in the causal explanation of action, because they are local. But most actions are themselves non-local; moreover, internalist constraints on causal explanation tend to force one to use explaining conditions that are either inadequately general or inadequately unified. Externalism is a natural outcome of a conception on which the function of having a mind is to enable one’s activity to be guided by knowledge of the world, typically of the external environment, and mental life in general is to be understood in relation to the cases in which it successfully discharges its function. Mental states that are externalist in virtue of their content are complemented by mental states that are externalist in virtue of the attitude to that content. Factive mental states such as knowing, perceiving and remembering are examples of the latter kind. They can play a full role in the explanation of action.

(2) A second source of opposition to the knowledge-centred conception of mind is the idea that knowledge must be non-circularly analysable, either conceptually or ontologically, into necessary and sufficient conditions of some more basic kind. In particular, since both truth and belief are necessary conditions for knowledge, someone might expect there to be a further condition, whatever must be added to true belief to give knowledge, such that knowledge could be analysed as true belief plus that extra condition. If so, belief would presumably count as more basic than knowledge, since the former is used to analyse the latter. Moreover, one would not expect to understand evidence and justification in terms of knowledge, because one would want them to be available for non-circular use in the analysis of knowledge. But it is a fallacy to assume that necessary conditions can always be ‘subtracted’ to leave a further necessary condition that, added to them, gives a non-circular necessary and sufficient condition for what one first thought of. Being coloured is a necessary condition for being red, but if one asks what must be added to being coloured to give being red, one comes up only with answers stated in terms of being red: being red itself, or being red if coloured. There was no good prior reason to expect non-circular necessary and sufficient conditions for knowledge in terms of truth, belief and other factors. Moreover, the failure of the extensive post-Gettier research programme over several decades to come up with such conditions provides posterior inductive reason to think that there are none.

(3) A third source of opposition is the limited capacity of the subject to discriminate states of knowledge from states of ignorance. As a victim of misleading evidence, one may falsely believe that things are some way, and therefore not know that they are that way, without being in a position to know that one does not know that they are that way. Less obviously, in other cases one may know that things are some way without being in a position to know that one knows that they are that way. Someone may therefore deny that agents have the kind of privileged access to whether they know that would be required for knowing to be a mental state. Alternatively, even someone who rejects such a general requirement of privileged access on mental states may still hold that privileged access to whether one knows would be required for knowing to be the norm for belief or assertion in the way described above, since it would be unfair to judge agents ultimately by a standard compliance with which they are in no position to recognize. One response to these complaints is the anti-luminosity argument. A state is luminous if and only if being in it is sufficient for being in a position to know that one is in it. It can be argued that only trivial states (such as conditions that one is always or never in) are luminous, because a gradual transition can start with one in a given non-trivial state and end with one not in it, and near the boundary one’s judgements as to whether one is in it are insufficiently reliable to constitute knowledge. In particular, the argument applies to paradigm mental states such as feeling hot. Thus paradigm mental states fail the privileged access requirement. They may meet some weaker requirement—for example, that agents typically be in a position to know whether they are in them—but knowledge states meet that weaker requirement too. Similarly, the anti-luminosity argument shows that no non-trivial norm is such that one is always in a position to know whether one is complying with it. Thus the failure of knowing to meet that requirement is compatible with its being the norm for belief and assertion. The appeal of sceptical arguments is traced to their exploitation of the appeal of that exaggerated requirement on norms. Since evidence is normative for belief, they assume that we must always be in a position to know what our evidence is. Evidence from knowledge of the external world fails that requirement, since one is not in a position to recognize its absence in sceptical scenarios. The requirement therefore drives one to a purely phenomenal conception of evidence, on which the evidence in favour of our beliefs about the external world is so weak as to motivate scepticism. But even that retreat to inner evidence is futile, because the anti-luminosity argument shows that not even non-trivial phenomenal conditions are luminous. Rather, one must abandon the over-strong privileged access requirement on evidence. Epistemology can never be fully operationalized; whatever the epistemic norms, we are not always in a position to know whether we are complying with them.
