More Williamsonian scepticism about the a priori–a posteriori distinction

Timothy Williamson

In Dylan Dodd and Elia Zardini (eds.), The A Priori: Its Significance, Sources, and Extent (2026), 95–105. Oxford: Oxford University Press.

In ‘How Deep Is the Distinction between A Priori and A Posteriori Knowledge?’ (Williamson 2013), I developed a theme in The Philosophy of Philosophy (Williamson 2007) to argue that the distinction fails to cut at an epistemological joint. In their Chapter 4 in this volume, ‘Williamsonian Scepticism about the A Priori’, Giacomo Melis and Crispin Wright oppose that argument. This chapter explains why my argument is not vulnerable to their criticisms. It is divided to follow the main strands of their objections. They also identify various aspects of my argument with which they agree, and others from which they withhold assent; to avoid repetition, I will not emphasize them.

1. A Methodological Preliminary

Melis and Wright (section 5) query what they see as a puzzling difference between my treatments of the distinction between the a priori and the a posteriori and of that between knowledge and ignorance (Williamson 2000). In both cases, I find various proposed analyses of the distinction inadequate. In the case of knowledge, my reaction is very far from dismissing the distinction as shallow. Instead, I regard it as too deep to be analysed in more basic terms, and as fundamental to epistemology. So why dismiss the distinction between the a priori and the a posteriori as shallow? Why not instead regard it as also too deep to be analysed in more basic terms, and as fundamental to epistemology?

An initial difference between the two distinctions is that all human languages have a word translatable as ‘know’ (Nagel 2014: 6–7), whereas ‘a priori’ and ‘a posteriori’ are more specialized, technical expressions. If the category of knowledge is a human universal, it has survived far more severe and extensive testing than has the category of the a priori. Thus there is a stronger presumption in favour of the former category than of the latter. Of course, such a presumption is defeasible. Distinctions that come naturally to us can turn out to be shallow: distinctions in colour are an example. Distinctions that do not come naturally to us can turn out to be deep, like many introduced for theoretical purposes in natural science. For that reason, the difference in my treatment of the two distinctions results mainly from the kind of consideration to which I appeal in the case of the a priori, for which I can find no proper analogue in the case of knowledge.

If the a priori cannot be analysed in more theoretically fundamental terms, that indicates the failure of what I call in the paper the top-down approach to drawing the distinction between the a priori and the a posteriori. In my view, knowledge also cannot be analysed in more theoretically fundamental terms, which indicates the failure of the top-down approach to drawing the distinction between knowledge and ignorance. So far the two cases run in parallel.

However, I do not take the failure of the top-down approach to show that the distinction between the a priori and the a posteriori is shallow. Rather, in the 2013 paper I treat it as a preliminary to the main argument, which addresses the bottom-up approach to drawing the distinction, by means of examples which enable us to classify new cases with a fair degree of non-collusive agreement. I provide a pair of examples which, I argue, are deeply alike in epistemological respects, even though, by bottom-up standards, one of them is clearly a priori while the other is clearly not a priori. A parallel argument for the knowledge case would require a pair of examples which are deeply alike in epistemological respects, even though, by bottom-up standards, one of them is clearly knowledge while the other is clearly not knowledge. Melis and Wright make no attempt to offer such a pair of examples. In the literature, attempts have in effect been made to provide such a pair, for example by comparing various cases of common-sense knowledge to mere true belief that a given ticket will not win the lottery (see Hawthorne 2004 for discussion). In my view, the proposed cases of common-sense knowledge either are not clearly knowledge or are clearly different in epistemological structure from true belief in a lottery proposition, but arguing the point in detail would take us too far from Melis and Wright’s critique.

Of course, there are cases which are neither clearly knowledge nor clearly not knowledge, but the possibility of such borderline cases is just a corollary of the vagueness or imperfect precision of the term ‘knowledge’, and shows very little. Virtually all useful or deep distinctions turn out to be imperfectly precise and to have borderline cases. In arguing that the distinction between the a priori and the a posteriori is shallow, I did not appeal to borderline cases which are neither clearly a priori nor clearly not a priori, for they would only have shown the term ‘a priori’ to be vague or imperfectly precise, which would be quite consistent with the utility and depth of the distinction. Rather, I appealed to clear cases, one clearly on one side of the bottom-up boundary, the other clearly on the other side. It is there that the lack of a deep epistemological difference reveals that our classifications are not picking up a theoretically significant distinction. Similarly, the lack of a deep botanical difference between blue and yellow flowers of the same species would reveal that a classification of flowers by colour was not picking up a theoretically significant distinction.

Arguably, there is a further reason for ascribing more theoretical significance to the distinction between knowledge and ignorance than to the distinction between the a priori and the a posteriori, which is that the former distinction can do more heavy lifting than the latter in other epistemological theorizing. For instance, the category of knowledge is arguably needed to make good sense of evidential probabilities (Williamson 2000). Of course, the category of a priori knowledge has also been cast in various starring roles in past and present epistemological theories. It does indeed highlight a large but rough methodological difference between mathematics and, say, geology. However, attempts to make it a more load-bearing category in recent philosophy of mathematics and modal epistemology have arguably obscured more than they have clarified. Indeed, it was dissatisfaction with such attempts which led me to criticize the a priori–a posteriori distinction. I did not address such attempts directly in the 2013 paper, although its main argument constitutes a threat to them. They doubtless deserve detailed treatment in their own right, but since Melis and Wright do not argue by appeal to them, they can be left aside here.

Melis and Wright (at the end of section 3) suggest that, if the main argument of the 2013 paper works at all, it justifies a less modest conclusion than I drew from it; namely, it shows ‘that there is no real distinction: that, in our bottom-up judgements about the a priori and the a posteriori, while we largely march in step with each other, we track no objective divide’. I did not draw that immodest conclusion because my argument does not justify it. There is a striking difference between my two cases, but it is not primarily an epistemological one. Rather, it is modal: one case concerns knowledge of a manifestly metaphysically necessary truth, the other concerns knowledge of a manifestly contingent truth.1 When we classify the cases bottom-up, those similarities to respective prototypes of the a priori and the a posteriori tip the scales in opposite directions, given the other similarities and differences in play.

The bottom-up distinction is not stipulated to cut at an epistemological joint, for any such stipulation would be a top-down theoretical element. One can draw a line by the bottom-up method, however vaguely, even it does not coincide with any line drawn by the top-down method. If one defines ‘a priori’ and ‘a posteriori’ bottom-up, simply by examples on each side, one must be prepared for the line between things more similar to prototypes for ‘a priori’ and things more similar to prototypes for ‘a posteriori’ to run where it will—just as one must be prepared for the line between people more like lawyers and people more like lorry-drivers to run where it will, if one classifies everyone by those opposing prototypes. A shallow, meandering distinction is still a distinction.

Of course, a bottom-up distinction may be hard to apply in many cases because there is no obvious difference in degree of similarity to the rival prototypes. However, the more practice one has in applying a distinction, the easier one is likely to find it, and philosophers have had plenty of practice in applying the a priori–a posteriori distinction.

2. Examples

Melis and Wright’s major objection to the argument of my 2013 paper lies elsewhere. It concerns the treatment of the key examples. The truths at issue are these (with their nomenclature):

(Crimson)
All crimson things are red
(Who’s Who)
All recent volumes of Who’s Who are red

I describe a subject, Norman, who comes to know (Crimson) and (Who’s Who) for the first time by parallel imaginative exercises, using his recognitional capacities for the relevant properties offline, without reliance on any episodic memories or previously acquired rules connecting crimson or recent volumes of Who’s Who to red.

(Crimson) is of course an example of the sort of colour inclusion and exclusion principles often used as paradigms of truths knowable a priori. Melis and Wright question how imagining a shade of crimson and then making a judgement of red can achieve the requisite generality over all shades of crimson. In particular, they ask whether Norman has ever applied ‘crimson’ to something red but not crimson and been corrected. If not, how does he know the semantic difference between ‘crimson’ and ‘red’? So we may assume that Norman has received such a correction. In that case, Melis and Wright complain, the imaginative exercise is redundant: Norman can know (Crimson) independently of it because ‘he has been advised, by example and correction, that correct use of “crimson” is restricted to a band, vaguely demarcated no doubt, within the determinable colour red’ (section 6). But that description conflates two very different cases. It suggests that Norman was corrected by being told something like ‘It’s not crimson, though it is red; crimson is only one band of red’. Thus he is in effect told (Crimson). But in specifying the case I explicitly stipulated that Norman learnt ‘crimson’ and ‘red’ independently of each other, as could quite easily happen (2013: 295). The relevant correction is just ‘It’s not crimson’, with or without ‘It’s red’. He is given no general connection between crimson and red, such as ‘Crimson is only one band of red’. He must build the bridge from case-specific judgements to the universal generality of (Crimson) all by himself. That is why the imaginative exercise is not redundant.

But how can an imaginative exercise take one from the particular to the universal?2 Mathematicians often rely on visual imagination in reaching their results. Even if the proof is published without diagrams, other mathematicians may convince themselves of its correctness more by picture thinking than by checking every detail of the proof (see Giaquinto 2007 for more discussion). Famously, thinking about an imagined triangle may enable a mathematician to know something about all triangles, including those much more acute or obtuse than imagined.3 The key is mathematicians’ ability to avoid relying on imagined features not generalizable to all triangles. Exactly how they do it is still not fully understood, but that they do it is rather clear.4

In logic, the analogous move is the standard rule of Universal Generalization. Given a proof of FaFa from premises in which the name ‘aa’ does not occur, one can conclude ∀xFx\forall x\,Fx from the same premises. The proof works whatever ‘aa’ refers to, and varying its reference involves no change in the meaning of the premises; ‘aa’ is arbitrary. In a formal system, it is easy to keep track of the premises on which a conclusion rests, and whether they contain a given name. In ordinary human thinking, it is much harder to keep track of all the assumptions on which one’s current judgement rests. We may prejudge issues without realizing it. For example, the use of ‘she’ rather than ‘he’ (or vice versa) in generalizing about doctors and nurses may trigger gendered stereotypes and thereby distort our conclusions. Nevertheless, we sometimes need to use the informal analogue of Universal Generalization, so we need some ability to use the informal analogues of arbitrary names correctly. We do so in the imagination (Williamson 2016: 120–121).

At one point (section 8), Melis and Wright contemplate—but do not endorse—a need for an extra premise (or ‘lemma’) in Norman’s reasoning. Let us examine the case of (Crimson) in detail; analogous considerations apply to (Who’s Who). Their candidate extra premise is that the imagined shade is representative of crimson things with respect to red, or that crimson things are uniform with respect to red. Formalized (with ‘aa’ for the imagined shade), those potential premises and the conclusion are:

(Representative)
∀y(Crimson(y)→(Red(a)↔Red(y)))\forall y\,(\mathrm{Crimson}(y) \to (\mathrm{Red}(a) \leftrightarrow \mathrm{Red}(y)))
(Uniform)
∀x∀y((Crimson(x)&Crimson(y))→(Red(x)↔Red(y)))\forall x\,\forall y\,((\mathrm{Crimson}(x) \mathbin{\&} \mathrm{Crimson}(y)) \to (\mathrm{Red}(x) \leftrightarrow \mathrm{Red}(y)))
(Crimson)
∀y(Crimson(y)→Red(y))\forall y\,(\mathrm{Crimson}(y) \to \mathrm{Red}(y))

From (Representative) and Red(a)\mathrm{Red}(a) we can indeed derive (Crimson). We can also derive (Crimson) from (Uniform) and the conjunction Crimson(a)&Red(a)\mathrm{Crimson}(a) \mathbin{\&} \mathrm{Red}(a). The trouble is that both (Representative) and (Uniform) are universal generalizations themselves. Thus they merely postpone the crucial challenge: how to get from judgements that are at least particular in form, like FaFa, to explicit universal generalizations, like ∀xFx\forall x\,Fx.

Indeed, (Representative) and (Uniform) are much less simple and less psychologically natural than (Crimson) itself; they are more plausibly seen in the cognitive order as corollaries derived from (Crimson) than premises used in deriving it. (Uniform) follows from (Crimson) alone and (Representative) from (Crimson) combined with Red(a)\mathrm{Red}(a). Analogous points apply to the analogous extra premises for (Who’s Who). Thus Norman’s cognitive route to (Crimson) and (Who’s Who) should not be understood as involving an implicit auxiliary premise.5

For similar reasons, even if Norman were explicitly taught (Crimson), that too would only postpone the challenge. For how does the teacher know (Crimson)? It is implausible that explicit stipulations and dictionary definitions play an essential role in normal human understanding of colour terms.

Both logically and psychologically, the most natural way for Norman to learn (Crimson) as formalized above is this. First, he supposes Crimson(a)\mathrm{Crimson}(a). Then he knowledgeably judges Red(a)\mathrm{Red}(a) on that supposition. That puts him in a position knowledgeably to judge the conditional Crimson(a)→Red(a)\mathrm{Crimson}(a) \to \mathrm{Red}(a), by the standard rule for accepting a conditional, closely related to both the logical rule of Conditional Proof (→-Introduction) and the Ramsey Test for conditionals. By a final step of Universal Generalization, he can then knowledgeably judge (Crimson).6

Of course, the formalization of the natural language sentence ‘All crimson things are red’ by the universally quantified conditional, as above, is not fully faithful. A better approximation uses a dyadic quantifier: ∀x(Crimson(x),Red(x))\forall x\,(\mathrm{Crimson}(x), \mathrm{Red}(x)). In that case, the separate applications of the rule for the conditional and the rule for the monadic universal quantifier can be telescoped into a single application of the rule for the dyadic universal quantifier. That is psychologically even more natural.

All of this has to be implemented by natural human cognitive capacities. Instead of a whiteboard, we use our imagination. In particular, since the conclusion depends on the particular predicates in play, we use our cognitive capacities to apply ‘crimson’ and ‘red’. To a first approximation, they are recognitional capacities calibrated by positive and negative exemplars. What the recognitional capacity delivers is particular, at least in form: judgements, and in good cases knowledge, such as ‘This is red’, ‘This is not red’, ‘This is crimson’, ‘This is not crimson’. They are delivered online, in perception, but also offline, in imagination.

Of course, a recognitional capacity is implicitly general in a sense because it can operate across a range of circumstances. But still there is a gap between that sort of implicit, subpersonal-level generality and the explicit, personal-level generality of a verbalized judgement in the form of a universally quantified sentence such as (Crimson) or (Who’s Who). Humans have the capacity to use their imaginations to bridge that gap. Of course, they often fail in unfavourable circumstances, but that applies to all human cognitive capacities—perception, memory, reasoning . . . . By hypothesis, Norman uses his imagination in favourable circumstances. By doing so, he bridges the gap and comes to know both (Crimson) and (Who’s Who). For (Crimson), he applies offline his recognitional capacities for ‘crimson’ and ‘red’. For (Who’s Who), he applies offline his recognitional capacities for ‘recent volumes of Who’s Who’ and ‘red’.

In this imaginative process, we start with a supposition, perhaps expressed in a sentence such as ‘It is crimson’, ‘It is a recent volume of Who’s Who’, or ‘ABC is a triangle’, where the subject term (‘it’, ‘ABC’, ‘aa’) does not single out any particular object. We then treat the supposition as embodied in visual imagination. We visually imagine a crimson patch, a recent volume of Who’s Who, or a triangle, although there need be no particular crimson patch, recent volume of Who’s Who, or triangle which we imagine. The point of the exercise is to bring to bear our visual skills and recognitional capacities to draw out the consequences of the supposition. These consequences need not be purely logical; in general, they may be causal or constitutive, depending on our interests.

What we must refrain from doing is exploiting special features of the visually imagined embodiment in ways which illicitly go beyond the supposition. For example, if one imagines an equilateral triangle, it is illicit to draw the conclusion ‘ABC is equiangular’ if the original supposition was ‘ABC is a triangle’, but not if it was ‘ABC is equilateral’. Similarly, if one imagines a crimson patch of a specific shade S\mathrm{S}, it is illicit to draw the conclusion ‘It is shade S\mathrm{S}’ if the original supposition was ‘It is crimson’, but not if it was ‘It is a patch of shade S\mathrm{S}’. If one imagines a recent volume of Who’s Who on a shelf, it is illicit to draw the conclusion ‘It is on a shelf’ if the original supposition was ‘It is a recent volume of Who’s Who’, but not if it was ‘It is a recent volume of Who’s Who on a shelf’. The illicit cases may be described as compromising typicality (for the given supposition). They often yield false generalizations: ‘All triangles are equiangular’, ‘All crimsons things are shade S\mathrm{S}’, ‘All recent volumes of Who’s Who are on a shelf’. One maintains typicality by not compromising it.

Evidently, one needs cognitive skill to maintain typicality. Many people have that skill in spatial reasoning, as already noted for geometry. Like other skills, it is typically unreflective. Trained mathematicians may be able to explain what is going on, but much spatial reasoning is far less formal than that. It may involve some tacit monitoring of other ways of imagining the original supposition permitted by the recognitional capacities at issue. There is no reason to expect skill in maintaining typicality to be restricted to spatial reasoning. It is equally relevant to reasoning about colour relations, and the colours of books. In effect, my 2013 paper assumed that Norman skilfully maintained typicality in the cognitive processes through which he came to accept (Crimson) and (Who’s Who).

According to Melis and Wright (section 8), Norman cannot learn (Crimson) just from looking at a sample of crimson, which is also red, nor can he learn (Who’s Who) from looking at a recent volume of Who’s Who, which is also red. Naturally, then, they wonder how he can learn (Crimson) from visualizing a sample of crimson, or (Who’s Who) from visualizing a recent volume of Who’s Who. What cognitive magic do I attribute to imagination which perception lacks? My account makes the challenge especially apt, since it treats visual imagination as just the offline variant of visual perception.

However, the case of spatial reasoning suggests that in principle Norman can learn (Crimson) from looking at a sample of crimson, and can learn (Who’s Who) from looking at a recent volume of Who’s Who. For we can learn a spatial generalization through looking at a diagram on a whiteboard just as we can through visually imagining such a diagram. For example, we treat the supposition ‘ABC is a triangle’ as embodied in the visually presented roughly triangular shape on the whiteboard, rather than as embodied in the visually imagined triangle. For many purposes, the diagram drawn on the board may do better than the imagined diagram: it is steadier, more durable, more capable of complex elaboration, and it can be shared by several people. Of course, in using the diagram on the board, we must maintain typicality. But that is as feasible for a visually presented diagram as it is for a visually imagined one. In both cases, we must refrain from illicitly exploiting special features of the visual embodiment. In a geometry lesson, the teacher maintains typicality in using the diagram on the board. Norman can maintain typicality in drawing conclusions from a visually presented sample of crimson, treated as embodying the supposition ‘It is crimson’, just as he can in drawing conclusions from a visually imagined sample of crimson, treated as embodying the same supposition. Likewise, Norman can maintain typicality in drawing conclusions from a visually presented recent volume of Who’s Who, treated as embodying the supposition ‘It is a recent volume of Who’s Who’, just as he can in drawing conclusions from a visually imagined recent volume of Who’s Who, treated as embodying the same supposition. For example, he may tacitly monitor ways of visually imagining the original supposition permitted by the recognitional capacities at issue which contrast with what is visually presented.

In these respects, my account attributes no special privilege to imagination over perception. The chief advantage of the former over the latter is more straightforward: you can imagine things and events in their absence; you cannot perceive them in their absence.

Of course, in the long run, we need a well-developed and well-tested psychological account of how we manage to maintain typicality. But just about everyone faces some version of this challenge. As already seen, it arises for the rule of Universal Generalization, with its ban on auxiliary premises containing the arbitrary name ‘aa’ or free variable to be generalized on. Any exception to that proviso compromises typicality. There is a similar restriction on the standard rule for reasoning from a premise of the form ∃xFx\exists x\,Fx. One assumes a ‘typical instance’ FaFa and derives a conclusion CC from it and auxiliary premises. Then CC also follows from ∃xFx\exists x\,Fx and those auxiliary premises, provided that the arbitrary name ‘aa’ occurs in neither the auxiliary premises nor CC nor FxFx. Any exception to that proviso compromises typicality. Since normal humans can reason in similar ways with quantifiers like ‘all’ and ‘some’, we must in effect be sensitive to analogous restrictions, even though we are normally not conscious of them. An account which denied us that capacity would deprive us of some of our fundamental forms of reasoning. The capacities I attributed to Norman are informal analogues of our capacity to maintain typicality in quantificational reasoning, for the imagination as the natural human medium for suppositional reasoning.

3. Internalism and Externalism

How exactly does an imaginative exercise produce knowledge of (Crimson) and (Who’s Who), or justified belief? When I specified ways in which Norman’s circumstances are favourable, I stipulated that his recognitional capacities are reliable. Melis and Wright (sections 6 and 9) conjecture that my argument was led astray by implicit reliance on epistemological externalism, which I certainly had not advertised. But that is to mistake the dialectical structure of the argument.

Independently of disputes between internalism and externalism, it is common ground that principles of colour inclusion and exclusion make prime candidates for the a priori. To let one example stand for many, in an internalist-friendly defence of the a priori, Laurence Bonjour cites ‘No surface can be uniformly red and uniformly blue at the same time’ as something we have an obvious a priori reason to believe (2014: 180). For someone entertaining such a principle about colours for the first time, as opposed to simply remembering that red and blue are mutually exclusive colours, the natural way to evaluate it is by bringing one’s visual imagination to bear; it is unclear what else one should do. There is no special reason for internalists to reject that modest phenomenological observation. Their differences with externalists should be expected to emerge later. In specifying that Norman’s circumstances are favourable, I was simply eliminating irrelevant obstacles to his knowledge. I stipulated that his recognitional capacities are reliable to avoid one sort of externalist objection, and of course any mention of reliability is a red rag to an internalist bull, but if internalists regard the reliability of Norman’s recognitional capacities as irrelevant to his epistemic position, they are free to ignore it. The onus is on them to explain in their own terms how such an imaginative exercise can produce knowledge or justified belief.

For instance, an internalist might emphasize that imagining something crimson makes it seem true to Norman that all crimson things are red. But then imagining a recent volume of Who’s Who may make it seem true to him that all recent volumes of Who’s Who are red. Going internalist may even smooth the path for my argument because the conscious paraphernalia on which internalists put so much weight can quite easily be kept in parallel between the two cases, exactly as the argument requires. Perhaps some versions of internalism can simply accept that the difference between the a priori and the a posteriori is superficial.

Of course, some internalists may be tempted to invoke differences between the two cases derivative from the difference in metaphysical modality between (Crimson) and (Who’s Who), but externalists may be tempted to do the same thing. As I argued in the original paper, such a strategy will not vindicate a fundamentally epistemological distinction between the a priori and the a posteriori.

Whether internalists can provide a seriously defensible account of knowledge or justified belief from imaginative exercises is another matter. If they cannot, that is their problem. Denying the existence of such knowledge or justified belief is not a good option for them because it then becomes hard to prevent a slide into a more general scepticism, since so much of our cognition relies on imaginative thinking. Indeed, it is not clear that internalist constraints are compatible with a seriously defensible account of knowledge or justified belief in any human case at all, but my purpose here is not to argue for that, nor was it in the 2013 paper.

Naturally, my own account of knowing by imagining (Williamson 2016) is externalist in spirit. If internalists have trouble accounting for knowing by imagining, so much the better. But to use examples that turn out to make trouble for a philosophical theory is not to assume it to be false. Rather, it may be the starting point for an argument against the theory.
