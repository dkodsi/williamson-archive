On the paradox of knowability

Timothy Williamson

Mind 96 (1987): 256–261.

Dorothy Edgington has recently helped an argument of Fitch’s against verificationism to emerge from ill-merited obscurity.1 Roughly speaking, the argument shows that not obviously silly forms of verificationism entail obviously silly forms of it. Edgington treats the argument as a paradox because she holds that we must retain some form of verificationism in order to do justice to certain considerations about what it is to grasp a concept, considerations which seem to owe something to Michael Dummett. She ingeniously proposes a modification of the verificationist schema that blocks Fitch’s argument. I shall argue that if there is a way of reading the modified schema as a not obviously silly form of verificationism, it has not yet been revealed.

A not obviously silly form of verificationism says, roughly, that all truths are knowable. It can be formalized by the schema:

(1)
p→◇Kpp \to ◇ \mathrm{K}p,

where ‘◇◇’ and ‘K’ abbreviate ‘it is possible that’ and ‘it is at some time known that’ respectively.2 An obviously silly form of verificationism says that all truths are known. It can be formalized by the schema:

(2)
p→Kpp \to \mathrm{K}p.

By substituting ‘p&−Kpp \mathbin{\&} \mathord{-} \mathrm{K}p’ for ‘pp’, Fitch showed that schema (1) entails schema (2), given principles of classical modal and epistemic logic that neither Edgington nor I dispute. If it is an unknown truth that pp, it is an unknowable truth that it is an unknown truth that pp.

According to Edgington, the trouble with (1) is that it conflates the situation ss in which pp with the situation s+s\mathord{+} in which it is known that, in ss, pp. For the crucial substitution ‘p&−Kpp \mathbin{\&} \mathord{-} \mathrm{K}p’ for ‘pp’, the idea is that there is no reason why it should not be known in s+s\mathord{+} that, in ss, it is an unknown truth that pp. Contradiction results only if one puts s=s+s = s\mathord{+}, when it would be known in ss that, in ss, it is an unknown truth that pp.3 Thus, Edgington’s preferred form of verificationism can be expressed by the schema:

(3)
∀s((in s,p)→∃s+(in s+,K(in s,p)))\forall s\,((\text{in }s,p) \to \exists s\,\mathord{+} (\text{in }s\mathord{+},\mathrm{K}(\text{in }s,p)))

In (3), the quantifiers range over situations, which should be thought of as nonspecific possibilities; Edgington uses situations rather than possible worlds in order to avoid making the knowledge in (3) involve the infinite amount of detail that the specification of a possible world would require. One can make (3) comparable to (1) and (2) by restricting ‘ss’ to actual situations and using the ‘actually’ operator ‘A\mathrm{A}’, in the sense of ‘in some actual situation’. The result is:

(4)
Ap→◇KAp\mathrm{A}p \to ◇ \mathrm{K}\mathrm{A}p.

(4) is a sub-schema of (1).4 Fitch’s argument does not reduce (4) to any obviously silly form of verificationism.

In one respect, (4) is a surprisingly weak form of verificationism. For, as Edgington notes, ‘Ap\mathrm{A}p’ always entails ‘□Ap□ \mathrm{A}p’, where ‘□□’ abbreviates ‘it is necessary that’. Thus, the only knowledge that (4) requires is of necessary truths. One might expect a robust form of verificationism to insist that at least some contingent truths are knowable. Of course, although the truths that (4) requires to be knowable are all necessary, they need not all be a priori. Actually to know the necessary truth that it actually snowed in 1985 is just as a posteriori a business as actually to know the contingent truth that it snowed in 1985. However, Edgington’s defence of (4) depends on the possibility of non-actual knowledge about what is actually the case, and what could constitute such knowledge remains to be seen.

Indeed, it is hard enough to see what could constitute even non-actual thought about what is actually the case, let alone knowledge. For, as Edgington notes, if people had non-actually had thoughts which they could have expressed by saying something of the form ‘It is actually the case that pp’, they would not have been expressing thoughts of the requisite kind, since their use of ‘actually’ would refer rigidly to their own situation, not to ours. In the same way, at seven o’clock I shall not be able to express the thought that it is now (six o’clock) raining in the words ‘It is now raining’, since my use then of the word ‘now’ will refer rigidly to seven o’clock, not to six o’clock. This temporal analogy suggests a further problem. How is one to think at seven o’clock the thought that one could have expressed at six o’clock in the words ‘It is now raining’? It will not do to think at seven o’clock the thought that one could express at seven o’clock in the words ‘It is raining at six o’clock’ (‘is’ tenseless). For one can express the latter thought in the same words at six o’clock, when they notoriously express a different thought from that which the words ‘It is now raining’ express; at six o’clock, one may not know that it is six o’clock. The only apparent way in which one might think at seven o’clock the thought that one could have expressed at six o’clock in the words ‘It is now raining’ is by remembering six o’clock; given the right context, such a thought could be expressed in the words ‘It was then raining’. However, this analogy offers no comfort to Edgington, for the memory link between the later thought and the earlier time requires there to be a causal link between events at the two times; in contrast there can be no causal link between the non-actual and the actual. In this respect, non-actual thought about the actual differs crucially from non-present thought about the present.5

More generally, the problem concerns thought and knowledge about counterfactual situations. We can raise it without use of the ‘actually’ operator by focusing on the quantificational version of Edgington’s verificationism. In schema (3), the situation variable ‘ss’ occurs inside the knowledge operator ‘K’. The person who knows that, in ss, pp must somehow have specified the situation ss. Moreover, they must have done so in the right sort of way, for it cannot be assumed that every way of specifying ss will permit their knowledge to be described by means of the de re construction. Four ways of specifying a situation will be considered: (i) by necessary and sufficient conditions, (ii) by counterfactuals, (iii) by space-time co-ordinates, and (iv) by ostension. It will be argued that, on the one hand, whenever ss may be specified in way (i) or (ii) in the knowledge that, in ss, pp, this knowledge requires no more than knowledge of a trivial logical truth, while, on the other hand, (iii) and (iv) do not yield non-actual knowledge of the actual. Thus, none of (i)-(iv) yields nontrivial non-actual knowledge of the actual. In the absence of some further way of specifying situations, Edgington can differentiate her position from obviously silly forms of verificationism only by making it too weak to be a form of verificationism at all.

These claims may sound implausibly strong. Surely we can have knowledge of counterfactual situations in the form ‘If ss were to obtain, it would be the case that pp’; surely such knowledge requires more than knowledge of a trivial logical truth. Both points are correct, but they are no objection to the argument, which—following schema (3)—concerns knowledge in the form ‘In ss, pp’. The two forms are not equivalent, for ‘In ss, pp’, unlike ‘If ss were to obtain, it would be the case that pp’ (for most values of ‘ss’ and ‘pp’), is necessarily true, if true at all. This view needs to be established as a preliminary to consideration of (i)-(iv).

What is it for something to be the case in a situation? Edgington thinks of situations as like possible worlds, but as not completely specific. Thus, the truth value of a proposition in a given situation does not itself vary from situation to situation, just as the truth value of a proposition in a given possible world does not itself vary from world to world. ‘In ss, pp’ is therefore necessarily true, if true at all. Edgington also requires this result for her quantifier and operator formulations (such as (3) and (4) respectively) to mesh with each other. Her operator formulations concern knowledge only of propositions of the form ApAp, which are necessarily true, if true at all; since she takes them to be restatements of her quantifier formulations, the latter should also concern knowledge only of propositions with this property. There is a deeper reason for this result. Verificationists hold not just that all truths of a particular kind—those of the form ‘If ss were to obtain, it would be the case that pp’, for example—happen to be knowable; they hold that all truths are knowable, because of what truth is. Edgington seeks to vindicate this idea by arguing that a truth can be known ‘from’ a situation other than that (or those) in which it is located. (3) has that effect only if the truth which ‘pp’ expresses in ss is the same as the truth which ‘In ss, pp’ expresses in s+s\mathord{+}. The only way that I can see of making out such a claim is in terms of a trivial equivalence between ‘In ss, pp’ and ‘In s+s\mathord{+}, in ss, pp’, that is, in terms of the claim that ‘In s+s\mathord{+}’ is redundant as applied to ‘In ss, pp’—which can be so only if the latter holds no contingency for ‘In s+s\mathord{+}’ to fasten on. Thus Edgington’s project requires ‘In ss, pp’ to be necessarily true, if true at all. It is now easy to argue that what ‘In ss, pp’ amounts to is ‘Necessarily, if ss obtains then pp’ (in an operator formulation). The latter clearly entails the former. For the converse, note that ‘In ss, pp’ entails the material conditional ‘If ss obtains then pp’, whence ‘Necessarily, in ss, pp’ entails ‘Necessarily, if ss obtains then pp’; since ‘In ss, pp’ entails the former, it also entails the latter.

With the locution ‘In ss, pp’ thus clarified, the various ways of specifying situations can be considered. In what follows, ss is any situation in which pp; by the previous paragraph, necessarily, if ss obtains then pp. What (3) requires is a situation s+s\mathord{+} in which it is known that, in ss, pp.

Assume first that, in knowing that in ss, pp, one can specify ss in way (i). Thus, for some value of ‘qq’, necessarily, ss obtains if and only if qq: moreover, the knowledge that, necessarily, if qq then pp counts as knowledge that, in ss, pp. Now it is easy to show that, necessarily, ss obtains if and only if both pp and qq. Thus the condition that both pp and qq specifies ss in way (i) just as well as the condition that qq does. It would therefore be quite ad hoc not to permit its use in de re knowledge about ss, since the latter condition may be so used. Hence the knowledge that, necessarily, if both pp and qq then rr counts as knowledge that, in ss, rr. In particular, the knowledge that, necessarily, if both pp and qq then pp counts as knowledge that, in ss, pp. Thus, given the assumption about (i), the knowledge that, in ss, pp requires no more than knowledge of a trivial logical truth.

Similarly, assume that, in knowing that, in ss, pp, one can specify ss in way (ii). Part of what is meant by saying that ss is specified by a counterfactual is that, for some value of ‘qq’, ss would obtain if qq. However, that by itself would not specify ss uniquely, for whenever ss obtains, so do other situations which stand to it as genus to species; what is required is for ss to be the most specific situation which would obtain if qq. Thus the assumption is that, in some situation s+s\mathord{+}, ss is the most specific situation which would obtain if qq, and the knowledge that if qq then it would be the case that pp counts as the knowledge that, in ss, pp (note that ss may not be specifiable as the most specific situation which would obtain if qq in a situation other than s+s\mathord{+}). Now it is certainly true in s+s\mathord{+} that if qq then it would be the case that pp, for ss would obtain if qq and, necessarily, if ss obtains then pp. By standard counterfactual reasoning (which both the Stalnaker and Lewis semantics validate), it follows that what would be the case if qq is just what would be the case if both pp and qq; in other words, the counterfactual statements ‘It would be the case that rr if qq’ and ‘It would be the case that rr if both pp and qq’ have the same truth value at s+s\mathord{+}. Therefore, since ss is the most specific situation which would obtain if qq, it is the most specific situation which would obtain if both pp and qq. Thus, in s+s\mathord{+}, the condition that both pp and qq specifies ss in way (ii) just as well as the condition that qq does. It would therefore be quite ad hoc not to permit its use in de re knowledge about ss, since the latter condition may be so used. Hence the knowledge that if both pp and qq then it would be the case that rr, counts as knowledge that, in ss, rr. In particular, the knowledge that if both pp and qq then it would be the case that pp, counts as knowledge that, in ss, pp. Thus, given the assumption about (ii), the knowledge that, in ss, pp requires no more than knowledge of a trivial logical truth.

No such arguments can be made for ways (iii) and (iv), since they do not specify ss by a condition into which the conjunct that pp can be built. For the same reason, however, they do not permit knowledge of counterfactual situations. ‘The situation in the Balkans in 1913’ or ‘this situation’, as used in actual situations, refer (if at all) to actual situations. As Edgington admits, it is obvious that not all actual truths are actually known. It is thus wholly unclear that (3) can be interpreted as a not obviously silly form of verificationism. If it permits the use of either (i) or (ii) in the knowledge at issue, its demands are too weak to be verificationist. If it permits the use only of (iii) and (iv), its demands are too strong not to be obviously silly. The onus is on anyone who defends (3) as a not obviously silly form of verificationism to give some account, which avoids these twin dangers, of how situations may and may not be specified in knowledge about them. Until that has been done, (3) should be treated as uninterpreted formalism.

Given these problems in reading Edgington’s quantified formulations, it might be better to concentrate on those, such as (4), which use operators instead. However, (4) is equally problematic, and for the same sort of reason. If (4) is not to collapse into the obviously silly schema Ap→AKAp\mathrm{A}p \to \mathrm{A}\mathrm{K}\mathrm{A}p, non-actual knowledge that actually pp is required. A fortiori, non-actual thought that actually pp is required. How would such thought be conceptualized? The proponent of (4) faces a dilemma. Either non-actual thinkers of the thought that actually pp have the concept which we express by ‘actually’, or they do not (in the sense in which the English word ‘actually’ expresses different concepts in different worlds). If they do, they can think about an absolutely specific counterfactual world—the actual one—and that is quite implausible; Edgington herself says ‘Knowledge of counterfactual situations is never of one specific possible world’ (p. 564). If they do not, what relation is required between their thinking and our sentence ‘Actually pp’ for the latter to characterize the former (as by hypothesis it does)? For, on this horn of the dilemma, it is possible to think that it is actually the case that pp without having the concept of what is actually the case. It is certainly not enough that their thought should be necessarily equivalent to the thought that actually pp, for the latter thought is a necessary truth (given that it is true), and thus necessarily equivalent to any tautology; (4) is not a form of verificationism if its truth requires only the possibility that someone knows a tautology.

It might be thought that Edgington’s replacement of possible worlds by actual situations would allow her to accept the first horn of the dilemma. If our concept of what is actually the case refers to a non-specific actual situation, might it not be available to the non-actual thinker? This objection rests on a technical mistake. The move from worlds to situations affects the semantics of modal language, not its logic. The same inferences are validated, but in a different way. Now, in a standard logic for the actuality operator A\mathrm{A}, the inference from −Ap\mathord{-} \mathrm{A}p to A−p\mathrm{A}\mathord{-} p and its converse are valid; moreover, if qq follows from p1,…,pnp_1,\ldots,p_n, then Aq\mathrm{A}q follows from Ap1,…,Apn\mathrm{A}p_1,\ldots,\mathrm{A}p_n. Suppose that non-actual thinkers have the concept A\mathrm{A} (which they of course cannot express by ‘actually’); then they can hypothesize a situation at which all instances of the schema ‘p↔App \leftrightarrow \mathrm{A}p’ are true. It follows from what has been said about the logic of A\mathrm{A} that they have specified a consistent and complete counterfactual situation—the actual one. In other words, the notion of a complete world is built into the technical concept of actuality to which Edgington appeals. Nor is it clear how a different concept of actuality, more apt to her purposes, could be introduced. For ‘the actual world’ means this world; the ostension succeeds because it takes place in a single world. In contrast, one could not say ‘Let “the actual situation” mean this situation’, for the ostension takes place in many situations of varying degrees of specificity simultaneously, and therefore fails. Situations differ from worlds precisely in not being mutually exclusive. The onus is on anyone who defends (4) as a not obviously silly form of verificationism to give some account of the nonstandard sense in which they are using the ‘actually’ operator. Until that has been done, (4) should be treated as uninterpreted formalism.

How else might a verificationist escape from Fitch’s argument? One way would be to substitute intuitionistic for classical logic.6 It may even be the only way. If so, that would confirm the connection between the logical and metaphysical issues which Michael Dummett has argued to exist. It is also a blow to the hope, or fear, that verificationism might come at no significant cost to logic. Conversely, to the extent that we are more likely to be mistaken in our theory of meaning than in our logic, we have further reason to reject any form of verificationism so far proposed.
