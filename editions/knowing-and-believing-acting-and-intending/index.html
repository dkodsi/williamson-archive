Knowing and believing, acting and intending

Timothy Williamson

Journal of Chinese Philosophy 42 (S1) (2015): 525–539.

A frequent theme in encounters between Chinese and Western philosophy is the relation between the theoretical and the practical, or between knowledge and action. The aim of this paper is to present one approach to this relation, from the perspective of contemporary analytic philosophy, as a possible basis for further discussion. The presentation derives from some of my encounters with Chinese philosophers.1

Section I works on the theoretical side, noting some basic points about the relation between knowing and believing, and some problems to which they give rise. Section II switches to the practical side, noting analogous basic points about the relation between acting and intending, and analogous problems to which they give rise. Section III sketches a contrast between internalist and externalist orders of explanation of mental phenomena, where internalist explanations start with states of the agent such as believing and intending, conceived as purely internal to the agent in contrast with the external environment, while externalist explanations typically start with relations of the agent to the external environment, such as knowing and acting. The externalist order of explanation provides an alternative to the degenerating research programme of analysing knowledge and action in terms of the internalist starting point, thereby resolving the problems noted in sections I and II. Section IV explains why not even believing and intending really fit internalists’ conception of the purely internal starting point, for the external environment plays a role in constituting their content. Section V describes how knowing and acting are linked by the nexus of practical reasoning, when we act on what we know, the paradigm of mental functioning. This connection enables us to understand believing in terms of knowing and intending in terms of acting. Of course, in an article of this size, one cannot hope to substantiate such large claims in adequate detail, but even a simple preliminary sketch may serve a useful purpose.

I. Knowing and Believing

The most obvious difference between knowledge and belief is that knowledge is factive while belief is not. In other words, if one knows that something is so, then it is so, whereas one may believe that something is so even if it is not so. For example, if I know that you are in China, it follows that you are in China, whereas if I believe that you are in China, it does not follow that you are in China; you may be somewhere else. We may put this by saying that knowledge entails truth whereas belief does not, using Aristotle’s account of truth and falsity in his Metaphysics: “To say of what is that it is not, or of what is not that it is, is false, while to say of what it is that it is, or of what is not that it is not, is true.”2

Of course, you may trick me into falsely believing that you are in China, in which case I may also believe that I know that you are in China, but then that is just another false belief of mine. I do not really know that you are in China.

Since knowledge entails truth and belief does not, it follows that belief does not entail knowledge, otherwise it would have to entail truth too. But it is very plausible that the converse does hold: knowledge entails belief. If I know that you are in China, it follows that I believe that you are in China. One might get the contrary impression because in some situations it is misleading to attribute belief but not misleading to attribute knowledge. For example, if you know that I know that Mary is in China, it would be misleading for you to say “TW believes that Mary is in China,” because doing so might cause your hearers falsely to believe that you do not know that I know that Mary is in China. For they might reason thus: if you did know that I knew that Mary was in China, you would have made the more informative and useful statement “TW knows that Mary is in China” instead; therefore, since you didn’t make the latter statement, you didn’t know that I knew that Mary was in China. Thus, on the assumption that knowledge entails belief, we can still explain (following the philosopher of language Paul Grice) why saying “TW believes that Mary is in China” is true but conversationally misleading when saying “TW knows that Mary is in China” is true and not conversationally misleading. Thus such cases pose no threat to the proposed entailment from knowledge to belief. To argue the point from the opposite direction, suppose that NN has no belief that Mary is in China. Surely we can conclude that NN does not know that Mary is in China. In what follows, we assume that knowledge entails belief.

Since knowledge entails belief and also (as already noted) truth, knowledge entails true belief. But the converse fails: not even true belief entails knowledge. For example, imagine someone who falsely believes that Athens is in Israel and falsely believes that Israel is part of Europe. He concludes that Athens is in Europe, which is true. On that basis alone, he believes that Athens is in Europe. He truly believes that Athens is in Europe. But he does not know that Athens is in Europe. Schematically, we might say that in such cases knowledge is lacking because belief and truth are not related appropriately. But what is an appropriate relation between belief and truth?

Since both belief and truth are necessary conditions for knowledge, many Western philosophers from Plato onwards have wondered what other necessary conditions for knowledge must be added to them to make a condition that is sufficient as well as necessary for knowledge. Such a condition, presented in terms of those individually necessary and jointly sufficient conditions, might be regarded as a definition of knowledge, even an analysis of knowing into its component parts. If you like, that project is the attempt to solve the following equation for the variable “X\mathrm{X}”:

knowledge = belief + truth + X\mathrm{X}

This is slightly metaphorical, because “+” here does not literally mean arithmetical addition, but it will do for present purposes.

An important constraint on solving the equation is that one must specify X\mathrm{X} in terms independent of “knowledge,” otherwise the definition or analysis would be circular. For example, one would get an almost trivially true equation by substituting the word “knowledge” itself for the variable “X\mathrm{X},” but it would not constitute a definition or analysis of the kind that so many philosophers have sought.

Why expect the equation to have such a solution? One might think that since belief and truth are parts of knowledge, it is just a matter of identifying the remaining part. But such thinking relies on a naïve application of the part-whole distinction. An analogy will make this clearer. Being coloured is a necessary condition for being red. One might therefore wonder what other necessary conditions for being red must be added to being coloured to make a condition that is sufficient as well as necessary for being red. Such a condition, presented in terms of those individually necessary and jointly sufficient conditions, might be regarded as a definition of being red, an analysis of being red into its component parts. This would correspond to the attempt to solve the following equation for the variable “Y\mathrm{Y}”:

red = coloured + Y\mathrm{Y}

An important constraint on solving the equation is that one must specify Y\mathrm{Y} in terms independent of “red,” otherwise the definition or analysis would be circular. But what could Y\mathrm{Y} be? One would get an almost trivially true equation by substituting “red” itself for the variable “Y\mathrm{Y},” or “red if coloured,” but it would not constitute a definition or analysis of the desired kind. Even if being red can be reduced to a property specified in physical terms, that would not fit the displayed pattern, since it would not have “coloured” as a component. The moral is: do not assume that a necessary condition for something is a component of a non-circular definition or analysis of that thing. Thus, even though knowledge entails both belief and truth, we should not assume that the displayed equation for knowledge has a solution in the intended sense.

In practice, all attempts so far to solve the equation, and thereby define or analyse knowledge, have either suffered from counterexamples or been circular.3 This bad track record itself constitutes inductive evidence for the insolubility of the equation for knowledge. Although the history of such attempts goes back to Plato, for instance in his dialogue Theaetetus, its most intense phase has been quite recent, in the half-century since the publication of Edmund Gettier’s short note (1963), in which he demonstrated that justification in the relevant sense does not satisfy the equation as a value of “X\mathrm{X},” because justified true belief is not always knowledge (where “justified” is not itself explained in terms of “knowledge”). After all, as a result of an elaborate hoax someone could have massive evidence that Athens is in Israel and that Israel is part of Europe.

For present purposes, a more interesting proposal is that X\mathrm{X} in the equation is the holding of a causal relation between the belief and its truth. The simplest and most natural-seeming version of this idea is the suggestion that one knows something if and only if its truth causes one to believe it. For instance, I know that it is raining when the rain causes me to believe that it is raining. But one main obstacle to causal theories of knowledge is the problem of deviant causal chains: truth can cause belief “in the wrong way,” in which case knowledge does not result. Here is a fanciful example. A very small meteorite falls on my head. The impact rearranges the molecules in my brain so as to cause me to forget the incident but to have some random new beliefs. One of them happens to be the belief that a meteorite has fallen on my head. The truth (that a meteorite fell on my head) caused my belief that a meteorite fell on my head, but I do not know that a meteorite fell on my head. All attempts to fix this problem, and others that afflict causal theories of knowledge, have failed.4 The same applies more generally to the project of solving the above equation for knowledge. In a characteristic pattern, which resembles the history of astronomers’ attempts to save geocentric theories in the face of recalcitrant data by the addition of epicycles, each counterexample leads to the revision of the theory by the addition of further qualifications, the more complicated new theory is then refuted by a more complicated new counterexample, and so on. Such a history warrants pessimism about the prospects for final success.

II. Acting and Intending

The issues about knowledge in section I have analogues for action. The words “action” and “acting” here will be reserved for what an agent does intentionally. For example, eating one’s food counts as an action, because one eats it intentionally, but digesting one’s food does not count as an action, because one does not digest it intentionally. Digesting, unlike eating, happens independently of one’s intentions. Some things can be done intentionally or unintentionally. For instance, one can drop a vase intentionally (to annoy someone, for instance), which counts as acting, but one can also drop it unintentionally (when it slips out of one’s hands), which does not count as acting.

The central difference between knowledge and action can be understood in terms of opposite directions of fit, at least to a first approximation. In knowledge, mind is fitted to world. In action, world is fitted to mind. For instance, when you shut the door, you make the state of the door fit what you had in mind. When I see that the door is shut, what I have in mind is made to fit the state of the door. Of course, real life situations involve complex interactions between the two directions of fit, because they involve complex interactions between knowledge and action.5

What stands to acting as believing stands to knowing? A natural answer is: intending. We can substantiate that answer by developing points about the relation between acting and intending analogous to the points about the relation between knowing and believing in section I.

As already noted, the most obvious difference between knowledge and belief is that knowing that something is so entails that it is so, whereas one can believe that something is so even if it is not so. Similarly, the most obvious difference between action and intention is that intentionally doing something entails doing it, whereas one can intend to do something (such as climbing Mount Everest) even if one never does it. In that sense, knowledge and action involve success, while belief and intention do not.

The relevant sense of “success” is strictly limited. An archer may act by intentionally shooting an arrow, intending to hit the target, but miss, and so fail in his further purpose. The present point is just that when he intentionally shot the arrow, he succeeded in his immediate purpose of shooting the arrow.

The overall analogy looks like this:

knowledge action
belief intention
truth success
falsity failure
fitting mind to world fitting world to mind

Since action entails success and intention does not, it follows that intention does not entail action, otherwise it would have to entail success too. But it is very plausible that the converse does hold: intentionally doing something entails intending to do it, just as knowledge entails belief. In what follows, we assume that action entails intention.

Since action entails intention and also (as already noted) success, action entails successful intention, just as knowledge entails true belief. But the converse fails: not even successful intention entails action, just as not even true belief entails knowledge. For example, imagine someone who intends to go to Sardinia. Before he even buys his air ticket, he is kidnapped and taken in a box to Sardinia. He intended to go to Sardinia, and he did go to Sardinia; in that sense, he had a successful intention to go to Sardinia. But he did not intentionally go to Sardinia. Schematically, we might say that in such cases action is lacking because intention and success are not related appropriately. But what is an appropriate relation between intention and success?

Since both intention and success are necessary conditions for action, philosophers have wondered what other necessary conditions for action must be added to them to make a condition that is sufficient as well as necessary for action. Such a condition, presented in terms of those individually necessary and jointly sufficient conditions, might be regarded as a definition of action, even an analysis of acting into its component parts. If you like, that project is the attempt to solve the following equation for the variable “Z\mathrm{Z}”:

action = intention + success + Z\mathrm{Z}

This is obviously analogous to the equation for knowledge in section I. It too is slightly metaphorical, because “+” does not literally mean arithmetical addition, but it will do for present purposes.

Analogously, an important constraint on solving the equation is that one must specify Z\mathrm{Z} in terms independent of “action,” otherwise the definition or analysis would be circular. For example, one would get an almost trivially true equation by substituting the word “action” itself for the variable “Z\mathrm{Z},” but it would not constitute a definition or analysis of the kind that many philosophers have sought. Why expect the equation to have such a solution? One might think that since intention and success are parts of action, it is just a matter of identifying the remaining part. But such thinking relies on a naïve application of the part-whole distinction. As before, the analogy with the equation for “red” in terms of “coloured” shows that there are alternatives.

In practice, all attempts so far to solve the equation, and thereby define or analyse action, have either suffered from counterexamples or been circular. This bad track record itself constitutes inductive evidence for the insolubility of the equation for action. The period of most intensive activity in trying to solve that equation was roughly the same as the period of most intensive activity in trying to solve the corresponding equation for knowledge.

For present purposes, the most interesting proposal is that Z\mathrm{Z} in the equation is the holding of a causal relation between the intention and its success: a causal theory of action analogous to a causal theory of knowledge. The simplest and most natural-seeming version of this idea is the suggestion that one intentionally does something if and only if one’s intention to do it causes one to do it. For instance, I intentionally drop the vase when my intention to drop it causes me to drop it. But one main obstacle to causal theories of action is the problem of deviant causal chains: the intention can cause success “in the wrong way,” just as for causal theories of knowledge truth can cause the belief “in the wrong way.” For instance, my intention to drop the vase may cause me to become nervous and my hands to sweat, which causes the vase to slip from my hands before I am ready to drop it intentionally. All attempts to fix this problem for causal theories of action have failed.6 In the same characteristic pattern as before, each counterexample leads to the revision of the theory by the addition of further qualifications, the more complicated new theory is then refuted by a more complicated new counterexample, and so on. As before, such a history warrants pessimism about the prospects for final success.

A. Internalist and Externalist Orders of Explanation

Both the project of explaining the nature of knowledge in terms of belief and the project of explaining the nature of action in terms of intention look like degenerating research programmes, to borrow a phrase from the philosopher of science Imre Lakatos. The two projects have much in common. Each of them starts with a state that appears to be purely internal to the mind: belief or intention. Each then tries to use it, together with other elements (truth, success; causation; . . .), to explain the nature of something that clearly involves the external environment too: knowledge or action. We may call this the internalist order of explanation. Instead of blindly persisting with this internalist order of explanation, we should pause to ask what reason we have to assume that it is the appropriate order.

Of course, the natural sciences have made great progress by explaining the behaviour of macroscopic objects in terms of the interaction of their microscopic parts. But that order of explanation is not optimal for all purposes. Consider, for example, the social science of economics. Someone who tried to develop economic theory by analysing the chemical composition of credit cards would be missing the point. Their economic significance has to be explained by their role in a whole system of social relations, not by their internal composition. It might be more relevant to analyse the chemical composition of the brains of the agents who rely on credit cards in their financial transactions. But even that line of research would miss the level of generality appropriate to economics. For a similar economic system could in principle be instantiated by beings whose internal composition, including that of their brains, was very different from that of humans, but whose behaviour exhibited similar overall patterns.

Even in the natural sciences, not all explanation is by decomposition. In mechanics, basic principles about the motion of a pendulum abstract from the internal composition of the pendulum. In biology, fruitful models of the evolution of populations of predators and prey abstract from the internal composition of the predators and prey. Although internal composition may become relevant when more specific phenomena are to be explained, the more general phenomena are often better explained at a corresponding level of generality, without reference to internal composition.

The same applies to knowledge and action. Although every particular case of knowing or acting has an underlying physical constitution, which could in principle be analysed, doing that would involve a drastic loss of generality. For example, the state of knowing that it is raining, or the action of intentionally putting up an umbrella, can be physically realized in vastly many different ways, the differences between which may be irrelevant to our explanatory purposes. The point is that there may be a level of generality at which one can classify in terms of knowledge or action even though they have no internalist analysis at that level of generality.

The internalist order of explanation also draws no support from considerations of linguistic understanding or conceptual grasp. Native speakers of a natural language typically understand its common words not by dictionary definitions in simpler terms but by paradigm examples of its applications. Nor can they grasp all concepts by analysis into simpler concepts, on pain of an infinite regress. Even “know” and “act” may be understood by paradigm examples. There is no reason to expect paradigms of believing and of intending to be prior to paradigms of knowing and of acting. If anything, one would expect the opposite, since the external aspects of knowing and of acting make them more palpable than are mere believing and mere intending.

We should therefore consider the opposite, externalist order of explanation, which starts with relations between the agent and the external environment, such as knowing and acting, and in terms of them explains more internal properties, such as believing and intending. The underlying picture is that in knowing and acting the agent to that extent functions well in its environment. When all goes well cognitively, one believes something only if one knows that thing. When one merely believes something without knowing that thing, something has gone wrong cognitively. Knowing is the normative standard for believing. Cognitive malfunctioning is to be understood as a deviation from good cognitive functioning. Similarly, when all goes well practically, one intends to do something only if (sooner or later) one intentionally does that thing. When one merely intends to do something without ever intentionally doing that thing, something has gone wrong practically. Intentional doing is the normative standard for intending. Practical malfunctioning is to be understood as a deviation from good practical functioning.

B. External Aspects of Believing and Intending

So far, we have left unchallenged the conception of believing and intending as purely internal. But that conception is false. For both believing and intending have a content—what the agent believes or intends—and a thought experiment devised by Hilary Putnam, as reinterpreted by Tyler Burge, shows that the content depends on the internal environment.7 Thus not even starting with believing and intending really fulfils the internalist aim.

Here is a version of Putnam’s twin earth thought experiment. XYZ is a liquid with the same appearance as H2O but a completely different chemical structure. XYZ is not water because it does not contain H2O. Twin Earth is a planet just like Earth except that where Earth has H2O, Twin Earth has XYZ. The thought experiment is set centuries ago, when the inhabitants of Earth and Twin Earth have no idea about the chemical structure of their liquids. Corresponding to a man Oscar on Earth there is Twin Oscar on Twin Earth. We may assume that Oscar and Twin Oscar have all the same relevant internal properties. On Earth, Oscar is holding a glass of H2O. On Twin Earth, Twin Oscar is holding a glass of XYZ. Oscar believes himself to be holding a glass of water and intends to drink water. He succeeds.

Do Oscar and Twin Oscar have the same beliefs about themselves (they have no beliefs about each other, because they have never heard of each other)? If so, Twin Oscar believes that he is holding a glass of water. Then his belief is false, because he is not holding a glass of water (here we use Aristotle’s principle about falsity, mentioned in section I). His glass contains XYZ, not H2O, and as already noted XYZ is not water. But there is no more reason to attribute error to Twin Oscar than to Oscar; their situations are symmetrical. So Twin Oscar does not believe that he is holding a glass of water. Thus Oscar and Twin Oscar have different beliefs. To state what Twin Oscar does believe, we can coin the term “twater,” which stands to XYZ as “water” stands to H2O. Then Twin Oscar believes that he is holding a glass of twater.

Do Oscar and Twin Oscar have the same intentions? If so, Twin Oscar intends to drink water. Then his intention fails, because he does not drink water. He drinks XYZ, not H2O, and XYZ is not water. But there is no more reason to attribute failure to Twin Oscar than to Oscar; their situations are symmetrical. So Twin Oscar does not intend to drink water. Thus Oscar and Twin Oscar have different intentions. To state what Twin Oscar does intend, we can use the term “twater” again. Twin Oscar intends to drink twater.

Some philosophers have tried to get round the argument by constructing a technical notion of “narrow content” that remains constant between Oscar and Twin Oscar: Oscar’s beliefs, intentions, and other mental states are supposed to have the same narrow contents as Twin Oscar’s, because narrow contents are supposed to be determined by purely internal states. But the construction has never been properly carried out, and there is no good reason to think that it could be. The more one reflects on the notion of narrow content, the more problematic it turns out to be. For example, let NC be the narrow content corresponding both to Oscar’s belief that he is holding a glass of water and to Twin Oscar’s belief that he is holding a glass of twater. But NC should not be confined to those in exactly the same internal physical state as Oscar and Twin Oscar, for then it would be hopelessly lacking in generality. One can be in any one of a vast range of internal physical states and believe that one is holding a glass of water (or twater). To achieve adequate generality, NC should be the narrow content of any belief that one is holding a glass of water. For the same reason, NC should also be the narrow content of any belief that one is holding a glass of twater. But now suppose that someone travels between Earth and Twin Earth, and now has both a belief that he is holding a glass of water and a belief that he is holding a glass of twater (he is holding a glass in each hand). But then we have the single narrow content NC for both beliefs, which is clearly inadequate, since he has two logically independent beliefs (is NC true or false if both glasses contain water?). Although friends of narrow content will continue to manoeuvre on their behalf, we are faced with another degenerating research programme. In general, having a given belief and having a given intention just are not internal properties.

The water/twater example is not an isolated curiosity. Such cases are ubiquitous. Many involve thoughts about particular objects. For instance, let G be the glass that Oscar is holding and TG be the glass that Twin Oscar is holding. Oscar believes that G is a glass. Twin Oscar does not believe that G is a glass; he has never seen or heard of G, and has no beliefs about G. Rather, Twin Oscar believes that TG is a glass. Oscar intends to drink from G. Twin Oscar does not intend to drink from G; he has no intentions about G. Rather, Twin Oscar intends to drink from TG.

Presumably, the gross evolutionary advantage of having a mind is that it enables one to interact with the environment in more flexible and complex ways than can mindless things like plants. From this perspective, what is basic to mind is not a bunch of monadic “qualitative” properties making up an inner world, but a network of relations between the agent and the environment: seeing water and G; loving water and G; imagining water and G; referring to water and G. Oscar and Twin Oscar have such relations to different things. It is an illusion that the way to pure mind is by abstracting from such relations, as wrong-headed as the idea that the way to pure soccer is by abstracting from the other team. To eliminate the other terms of the relations is to eliminate what matters about mind.

The point of mental content is to involve the world. That applies just as much to the content of beliefs and intentions as to the content of knowledge and action, what we know and what we intentionally do. But knowledge and action involve the world even more closely than do belief and intention, because they also involve them in a second way that does not depend on the externalist nature of mental content, but instead on the externalist nature of the mental relation to that content: the truth condition on knowing and the success condition on intentional doing. On the picture of mind just sketched, such forms of involvement with the world are not impurities in the mental: rather, they bring out the nature of the mental more clearly than it appears in their absence. Thus, to understand the cognitive aspect of mental life, the appropriate starting point is the case of knowing something rather than that of merely believing it; in brief, knowledge before belief. To understand the practical aspect of mental life, the appropriate starting point is the case of intentionally doing something rather than that of merely intending to do it; in brief, action before intention. Obviously, the priority to which “before” there refer is not that of earlier to later in time, but rather the priority of what explains to what is explained, and in turn the type of explanation here is not causal explanation, but explanation of the most general nature of these phenomena.

C. Acting on Knowledge

The focus so far has been on the analogy between the cognitive and practical sides of intelligent life, each taken in isolation from the other. But of course the two sides are sides of the same life: knowing and believing combine with acting and intending to form a single system, which we also need to consider. That in turn will enhance our understanding of each side separately, of both the relation of believing to knowing and the relation of intending to acting.

The central way in which knowledge and action combine is this: we act on what we know. If we know where we can get food, we may (intentionally) go there when we are hungry. Since knowledge entails truth, in acting on what we know we act on true rather than false beliefs, which is of course conducive to success in fulfilling our intentions. Acting on knowledge is the paradigm of a well-functioning mental life. Indeed, it is plausible and can be argued in detail that there is a corresponding norm of practical reasoning: act only on what you know.8 If you act on the belief that there is a deer behind the bush (as opposed to the weaker belief that it is probable that there is a deer behind the bush) without knowing that there is a deer behind the bush, the basis of your action is defective, even if you get lucky because there is in fact a deer behind the bush.

Of course, the interaction between knowledge and action is two-way. Not only can knowledge generate action, action can generate knowledge. For example, we gain knowledge through actions of looking and listening. Nevertheless, acting on knowledge is surely the most central way in which knowledge and action should combine.

When one acts on what one knows, a more local aspect of the process is that one’s beliefs help cause one’s intention. For instance, your belief that food is available over there (together with your hunger) helps cause you to intend to go over there. Internalists emphasize that the local aspect of practical reasoning may be present even if the global process is not. The belief-intention link may hold even if the belief fails to constitute knowledge (perhaps the belief is false) or the intention fails to be consummated in action (perhaps something prevents you from reaching the food). Indeed, it is characteristic of belief to play the same local role in practical reasoning as knowledge does, and characteristic of intention to play the same local role in practical reasoning as action does. But that is no reason to regard belief as more fundamental than knowledge, or intention as more fundamental than action. Rather, it sharpens our understanding of belief in terms of knowledge, and of action in terms of intention. Belief is what plays the same local role in practical reasoning as knowledge (the premises are believed), and intention is what plays the same local role in practical reasoning as action (the conclusion is intended). The reasoning here must be genuinely practical, involving genuine dispositions to action (even if, as a result of interfering factors, those dispositions are never manifested). For instance, if I say “My team will win, so I’ll bet on them,” but doing so in no way disposes me to bet on them, the reasoning is not genuinely practical, and provides no basis for classifying me as genuinely believing that my team will win or genuinely intending to bet on them.

Of course, it can happen that the content of a particular belief is never in fact a premise in practical reasoning, or that the content of a particular intention is never in fact a conclusion in practical reasoning (the intention might form through some non-rational process). Nevertheless, belief is the sort of thing that is disposed to give its content to the premises of practical reasoning, and intention is the sort of thing that is disposed to take its content from the conclusion of practical reasoning, even if those dispositions are not always manifested.9 In these ways too, both believing and intending must be understood in terms of relations to the wider environment.

Intermediate between intending and acting is trying. At least in normal cases, intentionally doing something involves trying to do that thing, and trying to do something involves intending to do that thing, but often one tries to do something but never does that thing (one’s attempt fails), and often one intends to do something but never tries to do that thing (one drops the intention, or never gets the opportunity to carry it out). Trying to do something resembles intentionally doing it not just in the local context of practical reasoning, but more extensively, although the resemblance does not always extend as far as actually doing the thing itself. What must we add to the knowing/believing pair to make a trio analogous to the acting/trying/intending trio?10 The analogy requires something intermediate between knowing and believing that resembles knowing not just in the local context of practical reasoning, but more extensively, although the resemblance does not always extend as far as actually knowing. An approximate answer is: believing reasonably. A capricious, utterly irrational belief is not enough. The agent’s cognitive faculties should not be malfunctioning too badly. This loose characterization encourages us to expect a wide range of cases, just as there is a wide range of cases of trying. Sometimes people try their hardest, and do their best, even though they finally fail; at other times they try only feebly, and could easily have done much better. Similarly, some cognitive processes are much more strongly disposed to produce knowledge than others are (even if on a particular occasion something interferes and they fail to produce it), while other processes are not disposed at all to produce knowledge.

The picture just sketched makes acting on what one knows the central feature of intelligent life. In that sense, it gives both acting and knowing explanatory priority. But if we ask what makes the action intelligent, the answer lies in its connection with knowledge. That makes sense of the slogan “Knowledge first,” not only in epistemology, but also in philosophy of mind and action.11

Notes

This paper originates in a public discussion I had with Professor Chung-ying Cheng at Oriel College Oxford in November 2013, loosely conceived as a dialogue, if not between Chinese and Western philosophy, then at least between one Chinese philosopher and one Western philosopher. It began with Professor Cheng’s presentation of some main points of his position on knowledge and mind (Chung-ying Cheng, “Some Points of My Position on Knowledge and Mind,” [typescript] 2013). My response followed. As I noted, Oxford University has a history of interaction with Chinese philosophers. The Chinese logician Hao Wang gave the prestigious John Locke lectures at Oxford in 1955, and then became Reader in the Philosophy of Mathematics at Oxford from 1956 to 1961. As an undergraduate in about 1974, I warmed to a remark in his book From Mathematics to Philosophy: “We know more about what we know than how we know what we know,” which still strikes me as true and important (Hao Wang, From Mathematics to Philosophy [London: Routledge & Kegan Paul, 1974], 1; I assure cynics that I read much further). One feature that Professor Cheng shares with Wang is having a Ph.D. in philosophy from Harvard, so they both straddle Chinese and Western analytic philosophy in a way I cannot pretend to do. Nor could I speak as a representative of Western analytic philosophy in the sense of articulating its shared assumptions or theories, since it is too internally diverse for that. I am speaking for myself, as one Western philosopher in the broad analytic tradition. An encouraging feature of my exchange with Professor Cheng was how much common ground turned out to unite us, especially in the direction of what might be characterized as anti-reductionist realism (many more themes came up in our encounter than can be discussed here, on pain of excessive fragmentation). Nothing like a generic or essential difference emerged between Chinese and Western viewpoints. A later version of my contribution was presented at Xiamen University in June 2014.
