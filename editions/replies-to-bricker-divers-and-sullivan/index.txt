# Replies to Bricker, Divers, and Sullivan

Timothy Williamson

Philosophy and Phenomenological Research 88 (3) (2014): 744–764.

The thoughtful and probing comments by Phillip Bricker, John Divers, and Meghan Sullivan on Modal Logic as Metaphysics raise a number of interlocking issues, both detailed ones about the relation of my arguments to David Lewis’s modal realism and more general ones about methodology in metaphysics. I will respond to each author separately, mentioning interconnections as they arise, and expanding some points made very briefly in the book.

# Reply to John Divers

Lewis’s modal realism plays a role in the book that some readers may find surprisingly marginal. I argue that Lewis really accepts the key necessitist thesis, properly understood, that necessarily everything is necessarily something, so on that crucial point my quarrel is not with him (pp. 16–17).1 Divers (2014) agrees with my characterization of Lewis as a necessitist, and recognizes that the book does not attempt a full-scale critique of modal realism. However, he takes issue with two remarks about modal realism in the book, which he finds too quickly dismissive. I will consider them in turn, using Divers’s labels.

(2a) The first remark is a very simple objection to modal realism (p. xii; see also p. 17 n. 23):

Since cosmological theories in physics are naturally understood as embodying no restriction of their purview to exclude Lewis’s multiple spatiotemporal systems, many of which are supposed to violate their laws, his cosmology is inconsistent with physicists’, and so in competition with them as a theory of total spatiotemporal reality. On such matters, physicists may be felt to speak with more authority than metaphysicians.

Divers replies that, once Lewis’s pluriverse hypothesis is on the table, the ‘level-headed physicist’ will restrict her generalizations to our maximal spatiotemporal region and ‘leave it to others to ruminate on whether this is all that there is’. Presumably, those others will be metaphysicians.

Physicists are not all as meek as Divers implies they should be. The structure of space and time is a central topic for physical theory. Lewis’s postulate of infinitely many spatiotemporal regions spatiotemporally disconnected from each other is a hypothesis about the structure of space and time if ever there was one, so it impinges on the subject matter of physics. Of course, physicists in our spatiotemporal system will never observe events in any other spatiotemporal system, even by means of instruments, but it is not therefore illegitimate for them as physicists to theorize about disconnected spatiotemporal systems. After all, it is obviously legitimate for physicists to theorize about whether our universe will end, even though they will never observe it. Cosmologists take seriously scenarios on which the universe accessible to our observation is a bubble within a much larger physical reality, and theorize as physicists about that larger reality.2 Another obvious example is the many worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics of Everett and others. Of course, none of these theories entails Lewis’s own hypothesis about the disconnected structure of space-time; that is part of my argument. Rather, they show how implausible it is to put Lewis’s hypothesis outside the proper domain of natural science. The sort of abductive reasoning that is the stock in trade of natural science can legitimately be applied beyond the limits of observation. Since multiverse hypotheses of various sorts are already on the physicists’ table, we are not entitled to understand their theories as tacitly restricted to our own spatiotemporal region. Indeed, adding such restrictions to cosmological theories merely in order to achieve consistency with modal realism has serious costs from an abductive perspective: it subtracts simplicity and strength from the physical theories, for no compensating gains most physicists would recognize.

Lewis’s own case for postulating a pluriverse is itself a broadly abductive one. The reason for believing in modal realism is supposed to be its explanatory power (1986, pp. 3–69). In that respect he is arguing like a physicist, minus the precise observations and the mathematics. It is Lewis’s abduction about the structure of spatiotemporal reality against the physicists’. I know which side my money is on.

Divers mentions models of the general theory of relativity in which there are disjoint spacetimes. He suggests that what ‘a perfectly competent, but cautious, physicist might do in that case’ is to restrict all her putative statements of natural law to her own spacetime. He envisages an exception for putative laws that supervene on the laws of general relativity, since by hypothesis they all hold in all the spacetimes in the model, but that exception already involves a role for the abductive reasoning behind general relativity in application to spacetimes other than our own. Moreover, it is quite unclear why the physicist should refuse to countenance abductive reasoning to unrestricted natural laws beyond (but compatible with) those of general relativity. That refusal is not just caution; it looks more like a mild dose of abductive or even inductive scepticism. Why cold feet at that point and not some other?

Divers cites the ‘familiar thought that the metaphysical hypothesis of physicalism is not a thesis of physics’. By ‘the metaphysical hypothesis of physicalism’ he presumably means the absolutely unrestricted universal generalization that everything is physical. I agree that physicalism in that sense does not have the authority of physics behind it. But that is because it is not suitably related to the principles that do the explanatory work in physics. After all, standard formulations of putative physical laws involve quantification over real numbers or other mathematical entities, which are not thereby treated as physical objects. Thus we should in any case not read all the quantifiers in physical principles as implicitly restricted to physical objects.

The conflict between Lewis’s cosmological theory and physicists’ is less easily resolved than Divers claims.

(2b) I say that Lewis ‘trivializes necessitism’ (p. 17). My reason is that, on the modal realist reading of the key necessitist principle ‘Necessarily everything is necessarily something’ that best approximates its intent, the modal operators are redundant, so the principle reduces to ‘Everything is something’, an elementary theorem of first-order non-modal logic with identity; it is even neutral between free and unfree logic. Divers reads my use of ‘trivializes’ as ‘pejorative’ towards modal realism, and challenges me ‘to explain more fully why Lewisian necessitism is trivial or trivializing (in a way that is to its discredit)’. This mistakes my intention. I was not using the alleged trivialization as an argument against modal realism. The main argument against modal realism in the book is just its inconsistency with physical theories, discussed under (2a). The negative tone of ‘trivial’ was directed towards the interest (though not the plausibility) of necessitism, under the hypothesis of modal realism: on the best modal realist account of the truth condition of the key necessitist principle, that condition holds for an uninteresting, uncontroversial reason. Of course, someone might try to use that result as an objection to modal realist semantics, on the grounds that it does not preserve cognitive significance, because the original necessitist principle is interesting and controversial. However, the book offers no such objection to modal realism: presumably, the modal realist semantics is not intended to preserve fine-grained cognitive significance, but only to capture truth conditions in some more coarse-grained sense. Thus I do not endorse that objection to modal realism from cognitive significance; I have no need of it.

I agree with Divers that, on my view, not all logical truths are trivial. In particular, the book counts the formalization of the key necessitist principle as a logical truth, and it is highly non-trivial in the absence of a reduction such as the modal realist offers. Unsurprisingly, the book’s defence of necessitism takes place mainly in a setting where necessitism is non-trivial. Since the book puts much less weight on the distinction between triviality and non-triviality than Divers assumes, there is no need of an elaborate theoretical account of the distinction.

In the final section of his piece, Divers voices the worry that the account in the book may be rather uninformative about modal metaphysics, by comparison with modal realism: ‘The Williamsonian conception of the metaphysically universal formulas as those that are true on the intended model structure is one that may lack force in the absence of an appropriately general and informative characterization of which model structure is intended’.

Before addressing the underlying worry, I should correct a misimpression about the order of explanation in the book. What it is for a formula of the quantified modal language to be metaphysically universal is defined in terms of an intended modal interpretation of the language, without reference to model structures, intended or otherwise. Construct the universal generalization of a formula of the quantified modal language by replacing the non-logical constants in it by free variables of the same grammatical type (occurrences of the same constant by occurrences of the same variable, and occurrences of different constants by occurrences of different variables), and prefixing with universal quantifiers on all free variables. Treat the modal and truth-functional operators, quantifiers, and identity sign as logical constants. The intended interpretation treats the modal operators as expressing metaphysical necessity and possibility and the quantifiers as unrestricted; otherwise it is standard. A formula is metaphysically universal if and only if its universal generalization is true on the intended interpretation (p. 93). The link between metaphysical universality and an intended model structure comes later, as a necessary condition on the latter in terms of the former, in order to connect the purely mathematical model theory of Kripke structures for modal logic with the philosophically important standard of metaphysical universality. A model structure is like a Kripke model with a designated point but no specified assignment of values to the non-logical constants over that structure. A model based on the model structure adds such an assignment. A formula of the quantified modal language is valid on a given model structure if and only if it is true at the designated point in every model based on the model structure. The constraint on an intended model structure is that the formulas valid on it should be all and only the metaphysically universal ones (p. 105). This is envisaged as a necessary but insufficient condition for a model structure to be intended, since any model structure isomorphic to an intended one will also satisfy the constraint but is typically not itself intended. The hope is to find a model structure for us to intend that not only satisfies the constraint but has a set of points that can be understood as worlds in some natural sense, a designated point that can be understood as the actual world in a corresponding sense, an accessibility relation between points that can be understood in terms of metaphysical possibility, and so on. One needs various non-trivial metaphysical assumptions to argue that there is something like an intended model structure, and even then it will have some limitations because the set-theoretic nature of model structures imposes unwanted cardinality restrictions. Still, the upshot is a link between the model theory and the metaphysics strong enough to explain the usefulness of the model theory for metaphysics. That is good for metaphysics, since from a technical point of view the model theory is by far the most successful part of modal logic. One striking metaphysical corollary of the connection is that, if there is an intended model structure, then the (unnecessitated) Barcan formula is metaphysically universal (p. 135). That formula takes us most of the way to necessitism. However, since the contingentist may deny that there is an intended model structure, the main argument in the book for a strongly necessitist system of higher-order modal logic with a strong comprehension principle based on the strong propositional modal logic S5 is not model-theoretic; instead, it is an abductive argument that draws on a range of considerations in the modal object-language. Given the contentiousness in metaphysics of these principles on their intended interpretation, the modal logic defended in the book can hardly be accused of uninformativeness about modal metaphysics.

Naturally, the information that a higher-order modal logic supplies about modal metaphysics is of a somewhat abstract, general nature. It is supplemented by further conclusions about the best form of necessitist metaphysics that would count as metaphysically universal only if one stretched the domain of logic unreasonably far, for instance by counting the predicate ‘concrete’ as a logical constant. An example is the postulate that there are contingently non-concrete objects, which I endorse (pp. 5–9) even though it is not metaphysically universal by the standard employed in the book, on which ‘concrete’ is not a logical constant: if ‘There are contingently non-concrete objects’ were metaphysically universal, it would be true on every reinterpretation of ‘concrete’; but it is false on the reinterpretation of ‘concrete’ to mean self-identical. Some truths of modal metaphysics are not metaphysically universal in the defined sense. A broadly abductive scientific methodology is applicable to those further truths too. It is just that the metaphysically universal truths form a central logical core of modal metaphysics, whose generality and precision enables it to impose valuable discipline on the rest of modal metaphysics.

Of course, the book leaves many questions in modal metaphysics unanswered. The obvious examples are questions whose answers are not metaphysically universal. For instance, the book does not say whether a member of one species could have been a member of another species instead (‘species’ is not a logical constant). Less obviously, the book leaves unanswered infinitely many questions whose answers are metaphysically universal. For higher-order logic is incomplete, and the system of principles endorsed in the book inherits that incompleteness. No consistent recursively axiomatizable extension of it has all the metaphysically universal formulas of the language as theorems.3 Unsurprisingly, answering all or even most questions in modal metaphysics is not a reasonable aim for any book.

None of the limitations above comes from a putative ‘metaphilosophical moral’ of the book floated by Divers, that ‘any hope of an appropriately scientific approach to the metaphysics of modality depends on our abandoning the aspiration to find answers to the (speculative?) questions that we have normally taken to be characteristic of the project (and as per Lewis 1986)’. I have no wish to ban attempts to answer speculative questions about the metaphysics of modality, nor do I despair of answering them in a scientifically respectable way, by broadly abductive considerations. But it is a task on which we must expect progress to be slow and never complete.

# Reply to Meghan Sullivan

Sullivan contrasts my methodology for metaphysics with David Lewis’s, which she describes as ‘intuition-driven’ (2014). As she interprets his methodology, its ‘goal is to identify the theory that carries the balance of intuitive support’. My reading of Lewis is different. In the passage she quotes, he speaks of ‘acceptably counterintuitive’ consequences of a philosophical theory, and in the next paragraph he goes on to deny that intuitions play a privileged methodological role: ‘Our “intuitions” are simply opinions; our philosophical theories are the same’ (1983, p. x). He then sketches a methodology aimed at reflective equilibrium rather than intuitive support. I have criticized the coherentist commitments of such accounts of philosophical methodology elsewhere (2007, pp. 244–6). But that difference between me and Lewis may be less in our practice than in our theories of that practice. In any case, I agree with Bricker’s description of the overall methodological similarities between my defence of necessitism and Lewis’s defence of modal realism (2014):

Both employ a broadly Quinean methodology that evaluates metaphysical theories of modality in part by applying pragmatic criteria such as fruitfulness, simplicity, and elegance. Both hold that the evaluations must be made by appropriately trained philosophers, not by laymen—or untrained philosophers—consulting their untutored judgments. And both hold that theoretical benefits may outweigh the costs of accepting a controversial ontology.

The methodological differences between Lewis and me that Bricker then identifies concern more specific issues about the relation of modal metaphysics to modal logic.

According to Sullivan, ‘If we use the intuition-driven methodology, then necessitists fight an uphill battle’. Presumably, by the same token, if we use the intuition-driven methodology, Lewis’s own, then modal realists fight an uphill battle too. But just as Lewis would argue that the belief that the life-sized butter statue of Elvis Presley at the 1997 Iowa State Fair could have melted before the Fair opened is consistent with his modal realism, via a reading of ‘could have’ in terms of counterpart relations, so I argue, contrary to Sullivan, that the belief that the statue could have failed to exist is consistent with my necessitism, via a reading of ‘exists’ in terms of restricted quantification. Obviously, that does not mean that modal realism and necessitism stand or fall together. But it does suggest that my methodological differences with Lewis are not as great as Sullivan claims.

Once Sullivan comes to her specific critique of my methodology, ‘intuitions’ fade into the background. She gives us a general methodological maxim, Evidence Inclusion: ‘Do not arbitrarily exclude potential evidence for or against a theory’s truth’. Few philosophers will deny the maxim (if someone excludes potential evidence for a reason, the exclusion is presumably not arbitrary). I myself have endorsed Carnap’s requirement of total evidence (Williamson 2000, p. 189). When Sullivan introduces intuition-driven methodology, what she says about evidence is just this: ‘intuitions are cited as evidence for or against particular claims of the theories’. She does not specify whether what are cited are the things we intuit or the intuitings of them. However, when she applies Evidence Inclusion in arguing against my methodology, the alleged arbitrary exclusion of evidence turns out not to involve anything that could naturally be described as an ‘intuition’. Rather, her point is that a strong and simple physicalist principle (HP)—that necessarily everything is physical—is omitted from the abductive comparison between necessitist and contingentist logics because even by physicalist lights it is not metaphysically universal (‘physical’ is not a logical constant). Sullivan does not say that her physicalist intuits, or claims to intuit, his physicalist principle. Rather, she has him accept physicalism on broadly abductive theoretical grounds. Here is how she states his case for the principle:

hardcore physicalism is a strong and elegant hypothesis in metaphysics. Hardcore physicalism confers a structural benefit on theories in which it appears. It also unites investigations in metaphysics directly with those of our most successful natural sciences. And it avoids the epistemological difficulties of theories which postulate non-physical objects.

Of course, a physicalist who took his evidence to include the physicalist principle itself could argue that his evidence is simply inconsistent with my style of necessitism, on which I could have been something non-physical. But Sullivan’s physicalist shows no sign of regarding denials of his physicalist principle as inconsistent with his evidence.

Sullivan’s underlying concern seems rather to be this: The methodology of the book involves an abductive comparison between modal logics. The preferred logic is then taken as the structural core of a richer modal theory. The modal theory, unlike the modal logic, is allowed to include principles that are not metaphysically universal—for instance, the physicalist principle, or its negation. But even if the bare necessitist modal logic does better abductively than the bare contingentist modal logic, might not the full contingentist modal theory do better abductively than the full necessitist modal theory? Physicalism and other principles excluded from the logic but included in the theory might tip the balance. In that case, shouldn’t our all things considered preference go to the full contingentist modal theory, and so in particular to the contingentist modal logic at its core, rather than to the full necessitist modal theory and in particular the necessitist modal logic at its core? Even if the physicalist contingentism is not abductively better than the corresponding necessitist theory, but is not clearly abductively worse either, that already challenges the abductive case for necessitism.

Such problems about where the abductive comparison should be made are endemic to an abductive methodology. More generally, non-deductive reasoning is typically non-monotonic, in the sense that sometimes a conclusion is appropriately drawn from a set of premises, but not from its extension by further premises. For example, even if Sullivan’s physicalist contingentism were abductively better than the corresponding necessitist theory, what would stop the advantage swinging back to necessitism again when a still wider domain of inquiry were taken into account? Such oscillations might occur in any branch of science with an abductive methodology. In principle, we might respond by insisting that only total theories should be compared, so that there is no wider domain of inquiry to destabilize the ranking. In practice, however, that response is unworkable: human scientists are not capable of taking everything into consideration at once. Our theories are not integrated to that degree.

Such general methodological reflections show that the form of Sullivan’s concern is unsurprising, but that does not licence us to ignore its content. A less generic reply is needed.

At this point, some differences between logico-mathematical theories and other theories become relevant—not differences of kind, but differences of degree. When the predictions of a natural scientific theory clash with observations, only very rarely indeed do scientists try to save the theory by revising logic or mathematics. Of course, they look for logical or mathematical errors in the derivations of the predictions from the theory, but what they seek are normally errors in how standard logical or mathematical principles were applied, not errors in those principles themselves. Although the underlying case for the first principles of logic and mathematics is plausibly abductive, scientists do not normally treat that case as hostage to new developments in natural science.4 That is not to say that they are barred in principle from doing so.

The most celebrated test case is quantum logic. Quine (1951) invoked proposals to revise the law of excluded middle in order to simplify quantum mechanics, and Putnam (1969) proposed rejecting one of the distributive laws of classical propositional logic in order to understand what is physically happening in two-slit experiments. In both cases, the suggestion was that the abductive advantages of classical logic over a non-classical alternative are trumped by the abductive advantages of the non-classical logic plus quantum mechanics over classical logic plus quantum mechanics. Such abductive challenges to classical logic cannot be mechanically dismissed by some transcendental argument or all-purpose methodological maxim. However, they emerged in response to puzzles in physics of an atypically radical sort. Moreover, both Quine (1970, pp. 85-6) and Putnam (2012) later came to a negative verdict on quantum logic. In practice, rejecting classical logic just does not help us understand the nature of quantum reality. Quantum logic is a discouraging precedent for introducing considerations of physical theory into the abductive comparison of logics.

An important typical feature of first principles of logic and mathematics is their extremely wide applicability. Any theory needs logic to draw out its consequences; very many need mathematics. Consequently, restricting first principles of logic or mathematics tends to have knock-on effects in any well-developed branch of science. Weakening logic or mathematics typically weakens the derivable consequences of a given scientific theory and so its abductive power. Thus the global abductive loss implicit in such a weakening over the totality of our theories promises to outweigh any local gains made with respect to a particular branch of inquiry.

Deviant logicians who propose rejecting a principle of classical logic typically reassure us that the classical principle will still hold under ordinary or favourable conditions (for instance, when applied to finite totalities or to precise expressions). However, the resultant need for inserting complicated restrictions into simple classical principles and invoking extra auxiliary assumptions that the given restricting conditions hold in the cases at hand itself constitutes a major loss of abductive power. Proponents of non-classical logics often ignore or grossly underestimate the abductive costs of their proposals.

But does the abductive case for retaining classical non-modal logic, however compelling, parallel an abductive case for necessitist modal logic over its contingentist rivals? Several arguments suggest a negative answer. (i) There is no question of retaining necessitist modal logic, since it does not represent the status quo. If anything, contingentist modal logic is currently more popular. (ii) Both necessitist modal logic and its main contingentist rivals are classical, in the sense that their non-modal propositional fragment is just standard classical propositional logic. (iii) Modal logics are much less widely applicable than their non-modal fragments, because modal operators are much rarer in theoretical discourse than are the ubiquitous quantifiers, Boolean operators, and identity sign of non-modal logic. For example, the core language of mathematics contains no modal operators.

All three arguments (i)-(iii) raise pertinent issues, but as a defence of contingentist modal logic against the charge of abductive weakness they fail, both individually and together, because they all depend on the same underlying misconception. I will explain.

The first point to notice is that virtually all inquiries into contingent matters are riddled with modal considerations. Even if causation lacks strictly necessary and sufficient conditions in terms of counterfactual conditionals, in practice systematic causal thought requires continual attention to counterfactual issues. Would the effect still have occurred even if the alleged cause had not? Could the effect have easily not occurred even if the alleged cause had still occurred? Such questions are asked over the spectrum of inquiries from history to physics. In reasoning about such counterfactual possibilities and drawing conclusions therefrom about actual causal connections, we reason modally. Moreover, even mathematical arguments in science are often being applied to something implicitly modal. For instance, talk of state spaces is widespread in the physical sciences, and is a typical framework for the application of mathematical arguments. A state space is a space of possible specific states of a system. A mathematical argument about the state space will involve quantification over the states, which is relevantly like quantification over possible worlds, and so corresponds to the use of modal operators (perhaps for physical rather than metaphysical modalities). The mathematical argument may also involve quantification over entities implicated by those states, such as the particles there would be if the system were in that state. In effect, the mathematics handles the modal dimension of the system under study by analysis of something like an intended Kripke model structure in extensional terms, rather than by overt use of modal operators.

Necessitism poses no obstacle to such a procedure. For necessitists, it is plausible that under reasonable idealizations there is an intended model structure (pp. 139–47). The idealizations concern cardinality restrictions imposed by the mathematically convenient definition of model structures as set-theoretic complexes: in reality, the relevant entities will be too many to form a set. But the idealizing assumption that they do form a set is not really needed to legitimize the extensional style of the mathematical reasoning, which would remain legitimate even if the usual set-theoretic form of model theory were replaced by a cardinality-neutral higher-order form (pp. 236–40). By contrast, from a contingentist point of view, the extensional style of the mathematical reasoning is deeply problematic. For, as already noted in my reply to Divers, the assumption that there is an intended model structure for quantified modal logic can be shown to validate the (unnecessitated) Barcan formula, which suffices for many of the most metaphysically provocative consequences of necessitism (p. 135). In particular, it implies that there could not have been anything other than the actual things. Moreover, this consequence does not depend on the definition of model structures as set-theoretic entities; it holds even if the set-theoretic terms are replaced by cardinality-neutral higher-order ones. On the face of it, therefore, the unqualified use of mathematical reasoning about state spaces embodies an implicit commitment to necessitism. Thus the absence of modal operators from such scientific reasoning manifests not neutrality on modal matters but a specific theoretical stance towards them. A rough analogy: if the interviewing committee for an academic post does not consider the colour of the candidates’ eyes in deciding which of them to appoint, that does not show that the committee is neutral on whether their eye colour is an important qualification; more likely, it shows that the committee is implicitly committed to the positive view that their eye colour is not an important qualification.5

Where do these considerations leave arguments (i)-(iii) against a parallelism between the abductive case for classical logic and an abductive case for necessitist modal logic? They undermine point (i) because they suggest that necessitism is the status quo after all in much scientific reasoning, albeit as an implicit commitment rather than an explicit claim. They undermine point (iii) because they show how commitment to modal assumptions can be widespread even where no modal operators appear. Even point (ii) becomes vulnerable, since contingentists typically tell us to use free logic in reasoning about counterfactual situations; free logic is already a deviation from classical first-order logic, albeit a rather mild deviation. For example, if contingentists applied standard unfree first-order classical logic in reasoning about a counterfactual situation in which Napoleon’s parents never met, from the uncontentious logical truth ‘Everything is something’ they would deduce ‘Napoleon is something’, which by their lights is not true in that situation.

The argument so far can be reinforced by results in the book. For instance, it is shown that if comprehension principles in second-order modal logic are weakened in ways consonant with contingentism, then we can no longer derive a modal analogue of a standard form of second-order mathematical reasoning about ordering relations (pp. 286–8). By contrast, the modal analogue is straightforwardly derivable in a necessitist setting. Moreover, if we work in the setting of ordinary second-order non-modal mathematics applied to a state space, the modal argument reduces to a special case of the non-modal argument. Thus the standard state space reasoning assumes something stronger than those contingentist comprehension principles.

Some potential contingentist replies to the foregoing argument are worth considering.

First, in order to avoid states individuated in terms of counterfactual objects, contingentists might individuate states in purely qualitative terms available in the actual world. For instance, instead of postulating many distinct non-qualitative states differing from each other only by permutations of indistinguishable non-actual particles, they might lump them all together into a single coarse-grained qualitative state. But this will sometimes obstruct the purposes of science. For example, we may need to count the fine-grained states rather than the coarse-grained ones in order to derive the correct probabilities (such as Fermi-Dirac statistics rather than Bose-Einstein statistics), just as when two coins are tossed simultaneously we risk getting the wrong probability of 1/3 for one head, one tails if we count it as a single state. The fine-grained states may be needed for scientific purposes, not just for metaphysical ones.

Second, contingentists may note that the modality at issue in scientific contexts is typically physical rather than metaphysical. They could in principle be necessitist for physical modality but contingentist for metaphysical modality. The picture would be of ontology constant through the inner sphere of physical possibility but variable beyond it in the outer sphere of mere metaphysical possibility. But this picture ill fits the usual motivations for contingentism. Presumably, it was physically possible for Napoleon’s parents never to have met, which most contingentists regard as a paradigm possibility in which there would have been no such thing as Napoleon.

Third, contingentists may hope to gain the benefits of necessitist ways of talking without paying the ontological costs, by simulating necessitist talk in contingentist terms. But an extended argument reveals severe limitations to such a strategy (pp. 305–75).6

Fourth, some contingentists may confine their contingentism to first-order logic, and go necessitist about all higher orders. In particular, they may adopt strong necessitist comprehension principles for higher-order modal logic. Full-blooded contingentists such as Robert Stalnaker (2012) reject this hybrid position, but it can at least recover many of the technical benefits of necessitism through simulating necessitist ways of talking with second-order quantification over necessarily existent but contingently instantiated haecceities in place of first-order quantification over necessarily existent individuals. In effect, the hybrid strategy implements Alvin Plantinga’s treatment of quantified modal logic in a higher-order setting (1974, pp. 70–87). The book criticizes the hybrid strategy (pp. 267–77). Attempts to provide a metaphysical rationale for the hybrid view get contingentists into worse trouble. The safest defence is a minimalist one. But the hybrid view, minimalist or not, still has major abductive costs. The mixture of contingentism and necessitism at different orders is inelegant, and the difference in treatment between individuals and their haecceities looks to be an ad hoc fix. Moreover, in first-order reasoning, which is where the hybrid view differs from necessitism, it still has some of the significant abductive costs of other forms of contingentism. For example, in first-order reasoning about counterfactual situations, some restriction to free logic is still needed, on pain of first-order necessitist conclusions such as ‘Napoleon is something’.7 Overall, first-order contingentist modal logic is weaker than first-order necessitist modal logic in the sort of reasoning it permits about any counterfactual situation. Such widespread weakness is a major abductive cost.8

To summarize the argument so far: The general applicability of logic gives special importance to abductively relevant differences between logics, since they have repercussions for the abductive quality of a wide range of theories based on one of the rival logics in a wide range of applications. That rule of thumb holds in particular for the abductively relevant differences between contingentist modal logic and necessitist modal logic.

Does Sullivan’s physicalist principle (HP) (‘Necessarily everything is physical’) have enough abductive power to buck the general trend, by outweighing the global abductive advantage of necessitist modal logic over contingentist modal logic? (HP) has the virtues of simplicity and local strength. But it lacks the global applications that principles of first-order modal logic have. Whereas they are tacitly used in a vast range of ordinary and scientific first-order reasoning about counterfactual situations, (HP) has a narrower range of applications. As Divers observes, theories in physics do not commit one to having a purely physicalist ontology. The premise that the objects one is reasoning about are physical (if anything at all) in an actual or counterfactual situation tells one little by itself; one needs more information about what physical kinds they belong to. After all, both a single particle and the whole of spacetime are presumably physical in the intended sense, but if one does not know which x is, one cannot conclude much about its behaviour, granted only that x is physical. The predicate ‘physical’ is not integrated with physical theories in any useful way. Indeed, given that mathematical objects are non-physical, (HP) is in danger of being inconsistent with physical theories, through excluding the mathematical objects they assume. (HP) lacks the abductive power to outweigh the global abductive advantage of necessitist modal logic over contingentist modal logic.

The case of (HP) suggests a more general methodological moral. In abductively comparing two logics, it is quite appropriate in principle to take into account abductive comparisons between corresponding non-logical theories based on the rival logics. In practice, however, doing so will tend not to tip the balance, because the general applicability of logical principles gives them special abductive weight. Of course, a logic should be consistent with our evidence, in the sense that not everything should follow from the evidence according to the logic.9 But Sullivan gives no reason to doubt the consistency of necessitist modal logic with our evidence.

# Reply to Phillip Bricker

Divers and I interpret Lewis as a necessitist. By contrast, Bricker (2014) interprets him as a contingentist. The difference has ramifications beyond Lewis scholarship and the semantics of ‘contingentist’. In my case for necessitism, I argue that what is at issue between it and contingentism should be formulated in a quantified modal language, out of fairness to contingentism, since formulating it in the non-modal language Lewis and other anti-modalists take as more fundamental, with quantifiers over worlds in place of modal operators, trivializes the issue in favour of necessitism (pp. 334–5). On Bricker’s view, my argument neglects a major option, anti-modalist contingentism, and in doing so forces the abductive comparison between necessitism and contingentism to be carried out from the perspective of a modal language, when it might have a very different outcome if carried out from the perspective of a non-modal language. In reply, I will first criticize Bricker’s interpretation of Lewis as a contingentist, and then generalize to anti-modalist contingentism.

According to Bricker, Lewis took himself to be a contingentist. For Lewis, the default interpretation of sentences of natural language with quantification into modal contexts is by counterpart theory. The English sentence ‘Necessarily everything is necessarily something’ can be used to express a distinctively necessitist claim, and his counterpart theory maps that sentence to something like ‘Everything in every world has a counterpart in every world’, which for Lewis express a false proposition in any ordinary context: many worlds contain nothing similar enough to me to be my counterpart on any ordinary counterpart relation. Thus Lewis can naturally interpret an ordinary person who says ‘I could have been nothing’ as speaking truly. So much is agreed. But the metaphysical dispute between contingentism and necessitism is not about the truth-values of sentences of natural language as uttered in everyday contexts. If we have to put it metalinguistically, it is about the truth-values of the relevant sentences when the quantifiers are interpreted as absolutely unrestricted (and the modal operators are interpreted as expressing metaphysical modalities). We can put it more accurately by taking those clarifications on board and then putting what the dispute is about non-metalinguistically: whether necessarily everything is necessarily something. The book is explicit that this is the metaphysical issue (p. 14). Lewis’s default counterpart-theoretic interpretations treat the individual quantifiers as restricted to the inhabitants of a world, and therefore have no direct bearing on the metaphysical dispute. After all, even hard-line necessitists restrict their quantifiers in ordinary contexts, for instance to concrete things, and make statements using sentences that in more metaphysical contexts could be used to make distinctively contingentist claims. According to Bricker, ‘It is only the counterpart-theoretic reading, Lewis would hold, that is relevant to the debate between necessitists and contingentists’. Perhaps Lewis had some other debate in mind. Perhaps he was a ‘contingentist’ in some other sense of the term. But that does not make him a contingentist in the sense of the book.

Bricker does not deny that Lewis also permits a reading on which the quantifiers are quite unrestricted; a knock-on effect of the lack of restriction is that the modal operators become redundant (p. 16). Pace Bricker, that modal realism permits this trivial reading is to the point, because the dispute between necessitism and contingentism is stipulated to be the one expressed on the unrestricted reading of the quantifiers. Nor is the stipulation arbitrary. Restricted quantifiers are explanatorily posterior to the unrestricted quantifiers they restrict. The generalizations of most interest to metaphysics are unrestricted. In particular, the sense of ‘being’ that matters most in metaphysics is being something without restriction on ‘something’, for then the denial of being is an unrestricted generalization: one cannot fail to be something simply by being in some other domain. Restricting the quantifiers in the definitions of ‘necessitism’ and ‘contingentism’ would yield a dispute explanatorily downstream from the dispute with which the book is concerned. In the relevant sense, Lewis is a necessitist, not an anti-modalist contingentist.

Of course, Lewis’s modal realism is not the only form of anti-modalism. Someone may reduce the modal operators to quantifiers over worlds without equating those worlds with concrete spatiotemporal systems. Instead, they may be equated with more abstract objects, such as maximally specific ways things could have been. The argument against modal realism discussed in my reply to Divers, from its inconsistency with standard physical theories, leaves such alternative forms of anti-modalism untouched; they cannot be eliminated that way. However, the reason for interpreting Lewis as a necessitist was also independent of his equation of worlds with concrete spatiotemporal systems. It just depended on the redundancy of the modal operators in ‘Necessarily everything is necessarily something’ once the quantifiers are read unrestrictedly, as Lewis permits. Thus we have a more general reason for interpreting anti-modalists as necessitists. But is the reason general enough to apply to all anti-modalists?

The main support for anti-modalism comes from possible world semantics, which overtly interprets the modal operators in terms of quantification over worlds. But the standard forms of possible world semantics that invalidate the Barcan formula and its converse do so by interpreting the overt object-language quantifiers as restricted to the domain of the world of evaluation, which may differ from the domains of other worlds. Lifting those restrictions validates the Barcan formula, its converse, and the formalization of ‘Necessarily everything is necessarily something’. Thus if one treats the language of possible worlds semantics as explanatorily prior to the modal object-language, as anti-modalists do, and interprets the object-language quantifiers as really unrestricted, as faithfulness to the intended senses of ‘necessitism’ and ‘contingentism’ requires, one is committed to necessitism. Anti-modalist contingentism would sacrifice the main support for anti-modalism. It is hard to see a genuine option there.

One complication is this. Bricker follows Kit Fine in defining modalism as the view that ‘the ordinary modal idioms (necessarily, possibly) are primitive’ (Fine 2005, p. 133). The book takes seriously a view that does not treat ‘necessarily’ and ‘possibly’ as primitive, but instead analyses them as ‘for every world w, at w:’ and ‘for some world w, at w:’ respectively, where ‘at w’ is itself a modal operator, not to be reduced to an extra world parameter tacked on to the primitive predicates, and a restriction on the quantifiers (pp. 419–20). That view opposes modalism in Fine and Bricker’s sense, since it denies that the ordinary modal idioms are primitive, and explains them in terms of quantification over worlds. Bricker explicitly allows an anti-modalist to accept ‘at w’ as a primitive modal operator. In the book, the explanations of ‘modalism’ and ‘anti-modalism’ embody no restriction to ordinary modal operators (p. 333), so the view under consideration would not count as anti-modalist, but for present purposes we can treat it as a non-standard form of anti-modalism. Can such a non-standard anti-modalist be a contingentist?

On the envisaged view, the Barcan and Converse Barcan formulas for an unrestricted quantifier EVERYTHING together amount to the equivalence of ‘for every world w, at w: for EVERYTHING x, A(x)’ and ‘for EVERYTHING x, for every world w, at w: A(x)’. The latter is trivially equivalent to ‘for every world w, for EVERYTHING x, at w: A(x)’, since any two universal quantifiers commute. Thus the target equivalence would hold if ‘at w: for EVERYTHING x, A(x)’ and ‘for EVERYTHING x, A(x), at w: A(x)’ were equivalent, but for contingentists there is no obvious reason why the modal operator ‘at w’ should commute with ‘for EVERYTHING x’, however unrestricted the quantifier; in the absence of such a reason, there is no obvious reason why the original equivalence should hold. Indeed, contingentists will presumably deny the original equivalence. The argument that standard forms of anti-modalism make necessitism trivially true does not extend to this non-standard form.

Can contingentists better resist the arguments of the book by adopting non-standard anti-modalism rather than modalism? It does not seem to help. If it helped anywhere, it would help with the allegation that contingentism lacks what Bricker calls expressive power. But, as Bricker notes, I allow contingentists a wide range of modal operators with which to simulate the effect of quantification over worlds, including cross-reference between different variables of quantification, just the advantage they might have hoped to gain from the ‘at w’ operator (pp. 369–71). As Bricker says, ‘If contingentists do indeed lack the means to interpret necessitist discourse, as Williamson thinks, it is not for lack of logical resources; it is due to a deficient ontology’. But what the ‘at w’ operator contributes is another logical resource, not more ontology.

The non-standard form of anti-modalism also lacks what Bricker takes to be a characteristic benefit of anti-modalism, the ability to explain familiar patterns of modal inference by reducing them to standard patterns of quantificational inference. As a typical example, take the inference from ‘Necessarily if A then B’ and ‘Necessarily A’ to ‘Necessarily B’, which is valid in any normal modal logic. A standard anti-modalist will ‘translate’ it to an inference from ‘For every world w if $(A)^{w}$ then $(B)^{w}$’ and ‘For every world w $(A)^{w}$’ to ‘For every world w $(B)^{w}$’, where $(A)^{w}$ and $(B)^{w}$ are the standard anti-modalist’s ‘translations’ of the sentences A and B respectively.10 That inference is valid in first-order non-modal logic, so the reduction reaches the explanatory goal Bricker sets it. By contrast, the non-standard anti-modalist ‘translates’ the modal inference to another modal inference, from ‘For every world w, at w: if $\mathopen{<}A\mathclose{>}^{w}$ then $\mathopen{<}B\mathclose{>}^{w}$’ and ‘For every world w, at w: $\mathopen{<}A\mathclose{>}^{w}$’ to ‘For every world w, at w: $\mathopen{<}B\mathclose{>}^{w}$’. But that inference is not valid in first-order non-modal logic. To validate it, we also need the validity of the inference from ‘at w: if $\mathopen{<}A\mathclose{>}^{w}$ then $\mathopen{<}B\mathclose{>}^{w}$’ and ‘at w: $\mathopen{<}A\mathclose{>}^{w}$’ to ‘at w: $\mathopen{<}B\mathclose{>}^{w}$’, where $\mathopen{<}A\mathclose{>}^{w}$ and $\mathopen{<}B\mathclose{>}^{w}$ are the non-standard anti-modalist’s ‘translations’ of A and B. But the latter inference is of the same general sort of the one whose validity was to be explained, since both are cases of modus ponens within the scope of a suitable modal operator. Analogous problems would arise in trying to explain the validity of other standard inferences in modal logic. This failure of ‘non-standard anti-modalism’ to replicate the explanatory successes of standard anti-modalism suggests that the term ‘anti-modalist’ is better redefined as in the book, so that anti-modalism requires treating all modal operators, ordinary or not, as reducible to the non-modal language.

In short, Bricker’s comments do not point to any consistent theory that would constitute a genuinely anti-modalist form of contingentism. Consequently, the abductive comparison between necessitist and contingentist modal logics must be carried out with respect to a modal object-language, as in the book, because the only available alternative begs the question in favour of necessitism.

Bricker raises a more general concern about the book’s methodology. He writes:

When we evaluate necessitist and contingentist modal logics, we are asking in part which logic allows of a simpler and more elegant semantics, with simpler and more elegant definitions and rules. But often such semantical considerations do not seem at all relevant to the truth or falsity of the corresponding metaphysical theories. They raise issues as to how best to formulate the theory, not issues with the theory’s content.

Of course, it is not always easy to separate issues as to how best to formulate a theory from issues with the theory’s content. Nor is it clear how semantical considerations about what a theory means can be irrelevant to whether it is true or false. But Bricker clarifies his concern when he writes later: ‘the simplicity of a metaphysical theory’s semantics, by itself, should not count at all in evaluating the theory for truth or falsity’. Note that he does not say ‘the simplicity of a metaphysical theory, by itself, should not count at all in evaluating the theory for truth or falsity’. He is not challenging the abductive methodology itself. Rather, his worry is that an abductive comparison between the semantics of two metaphysical theories may be playing an improper role in the abductive comparison between the two metaphysical theories themselves.

Bricker is right that metaphysical theories are not semantic theories, and that comparing the former is not comparing the latter. Given my interpretation of modal logics as metaphysical theories, they are not semantic theories. The metaphysically universal principles that constitute the target modal logic are not metalinguistic, and a fortiori not semantic; none of the logical constants at issue has a metalinguistic meaning (p. 93). Comparing modal logics is not comparing semantic theories. The difference comes out very clearly in the comparison between necessitist and contingentist comprehension principles in higher-order modal logic, which is carried out with respect to the higher-order modal object-language (pp. 262–304). The role of possible world model theory here is instrumental, to check the relative strength of various comprehension principles, not to provide an intended interpretation. It plays a similar role in the subsequent argument that contingentism cannot simulate higher-order necessitist theorizing (pp. 305–75).

I do argue that necessitists can take possible world model theory more seriously than contingentists can, because the assumption that there is an intended model structure implies that the Barcan formula is metaphysically universal. I nowhere suggest that this is a devastating result for contingentists. They are entitled to take a less realist attitude to possible world semantics than necessitists may do; neither side will take a perfectly realist attitude to it. Nevertheless, the asymmetry is not wholly irrelevant to an abductive comparison between necessitist and contingentist modal logics. For in abductively comparing two theories about one domain, it is reasonable to take into account how well they integrate with successful theories about other domains. For example, in abductively comparing two political theories, it is reasonable to take into account how well they integrate with successful economic theories. Since possible world semantics is a highly successful theory (or research programme) in semantics, when abductively comparing necessitist and contingentist modal logics it is reasonable to take into account how well they integrate with possible world semantics. In that respect, necessitist modal logic outscores its contingentist rival. Although that point is far from decisive in favour of necessitism, it should not simply be omitted from the comparison.

The upshot is in line with a more general conclusion about the role of linguistic considerations in philosophy, for which I have argued elsewhere (Williamson 2007). Although most philosophical questions are about a largely non-linguistic reality, in answering them we cannot afford to ignore the language in which we ask them and argue about their answers, because reflection on the language is often the best means of alerting ourselves to subtle patterns in reality itself.
