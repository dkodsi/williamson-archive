Two Incomplete Anti-Realist Modal Epistemic Logics1

Timothy Williamson

The Journal of Symbolic Logic 55 (1) (1990): 297–314.

Many phrases have been used to express what are sometimes called anti-realist conceptions of truth: ‘verifiability’, ‘knowability’, ‘rational acceptability’, ‘warranted assertability’. In spite of their obvious differences, all four of these phrases have a common form; each is a cognitive attitude modified by ‘-ability’. They speak of the possibility of verification, knowledge, rational acceptance or warranted assertion. Schematically, it seems to be claimed that it is true that AA if and only if it is possible that it is EE’d that AA, where ‘EE’ is to be replaced by some cognitive verb and ‘AA’ by any indicative sentence of the class to which the anti-realist conception is being claimed to apply. Since truth is redundant as a sentential operator, this boils down to the following thesis, where ‘pp’ is a propositional variable and ‘MM’ expresses the appropriate kind of possibility:

p↔MEp.p \leftrightarrow MEp.(*)

(*) also formalizes views such as Putnam’s: ‘To claim a statement is true is to claim it could be justified’ [11, p. 56]. It is no doubt a crude model for anti-realism, but one has to start somewhere; by seeing how and why more sophisticated versions of anti-realism differ from (*) one should be able to understand them better too. Moreover, if an anti-realist rejects the equation of truth with, say, warranted assertibility, arguing that truth is rather to be identified with the possibility of getting into a position in which one’s warrant to assert somehow cannot be overturned, the form of (*) is preserved, for truth is still being identified with the possibility of something. The arguments below require no more; nothing else will be assumed about EE. The aim of this paper is not primarily to evaluate the philosophical plausibility of (*) under some interpretation, but to discuss systems of propositional modal logic with an added operator EE in which it is a thesis, although some results about the latter will naturally provide material for the former task.2 The area also has mathematical interest; in particular, (*) seems to say that the possibility operator MM has a right inverse.

Consider a language 𝕃\mathbb{L} whose primitive vocabulary consists of a denumerable set Atom\mathrm{Atom} of propositional variables p,q,r,…p,q,r,\ldots, three monadic sentential operators ¬\neg (negation), MM and EE, the dyadic &\mathbin{\&} (conjunction) and parentheses. LL (necessity) abbreviates ¬M¬\neg M\neg in the metalanguage; ∨\vee, →\to and ↔\leftrightarrow (disjunction and the material conditional and biconditional) are used similarly. The set Wff\mathrm{Wff} of well-formed formulae of 𝕃\mathbb{L} is defined in the usual way. The concept of a normal modal logic (cf. [9]) has an obvious extension to 𝕃\mathbb{L}: a subset SS of Wff\mathrm{Wff} is a normal EE-logic iff SS contains L(p→q)→(Lp→Lq)L(p\to q)\to(Lp\to Lq) and all truth-functional tautologies and is closed under the rules of modus ponens (MP), uniform substitution (US) and necessitation (Nec). By analogy with modal logic, “K”\text{“}K\text{”} is the smallest normal EE-logic, “KT”\text{“}KT\text{”} is the smallest normal EE-logic containing the TT axiom Lp→pLp\to p, “KT4”\text{“}KT4\text{”} (or “S4”\text{“}S4\text{”}) is the smallest normal EE-logic containing it and the S4S4 axiom Lp→LLpLp\to LLp, and so on.3 A normal EE-logic collapses iff it contains Mp↔pMp\leftrightarrow p.

The philosophical motivation for interest in (*) requires MM to be interpreted as some kind of genuine possibility, and therefore LL as some kind of genuine necessity, although perhaps of a restricted sort. It seems reasonable to assume that a necessary consequence of something necessary is itself necessary and that the necessity of a logical truth is itself a logical truth, which suggests that attention should be confined to normal EE-logics. US is of course justified by the treatment of p,q,r,…p,q,r,\ldots as variables, a wff AA of 𝕃\mathbb{L} being valid on the intended interpretation iff every substitution instance of AA in an interpreted language of some kind has an appropriate kind of logical truth, for every substitution instance of a substitution instance of AA is itself a substitution instance of AA. The requirement that the underlying propositional logic be classical is a substantive one, for some anti-realists reject classical in favour of intuitionist logic; however, the attempt to combine anti-realism with classical logic is sufficiently common for investigation of its consequences to be worthwhile.4 Thus the concern is with normal EE-logics containing (*). Note that (*) is thereby subject to Nec; this seems reasonable, for anti-realists advance readings of (*) in saying what it is for a statement to be true, not just which statements happen to be true. The focus will further be narrowed to normal EE-logics containing the TT axiom Lp→pLp\to p, and therefore, equivalently, p→Mpp\to Mp: what is necessary is actual, and what is actual is possible. That is scarcely controversial. Some versions of anti-realism may seem to violate this requirement, for example by claiming that it is true that AA iff it is possible to verify that AA under epistemically ideal conditions, which suggests a limitation of the possible to the epistemically ideal, thereby excluding the actual. However, these versions can be regimented into line with TT by building the restriction into the interpretation of EE rather than of MM. Again, it might be suggested that ‘It is possible to EE that AA’ is not equivalent to ‘It is possible that it is EE’d that AA’, and that the notion of possibility involved in the former is the ‘can’ of ability, which, Anthony Kenny has argued, obeys neither p→Mpp\to Mp nor the thesis M(p∨q)→(Mp∨Mq)M(p\vee q)\to(Mp\vee Mq) of all normal logics ([10, pp. 122–144], and [2]). In both of these cases the problem can be traced to the intuition that one can XX only if one has a reliable ability to XX. But this condition too can be built into the interpretation of EE rather than of MM: to EE that AA might be (in some appropriate sense) to verify that AA in a reliable or locally reproducible way. Thus the use of a normal logic with the TT axiom does not beg this particular philosophical question. Other interpretations for which the TT axiom fails are some on which LALA says that AA is provable in a given formal system, as in the Gödelian modal logic KWKW (= GG; cf. [1]); however, even granted a formal system in which EE is expressible, (*) then equates the truth of a proposition with the formal consistency of the claim that it is cognized, not a principle likely to attract many anti-realists. In any case, if (*) holds for an operator MM which obeys “K”\text{“}K\text{”} and EE satisfies the principle Ep→pEp\to p, then (*) holds for an operator M′M' which obeys “KT”\text{“}KT\text{”}, since we can put M′p=p∨MpM'p=p\vee Mp.

Finally, collapsing systems should be excluded, for no interesting kind of possibility is logically equivalent to actuality; moreover, if p↔Mpp\leftrightarrow Mp is a thesis of a normal EE-logic containing (*), so by US is Ep↔MEpEp\leftrightarrow MEp, and therefore by (*) Ep↔pEp\leftrightarrow p, which would trivialize (*). Thus, since only extensions of “T”\text{“}T\text{”} are under consideration, Mp→pMp\to p must not be a thesis.

Given the desiderata of the previous paragraph, an anti-realist system may be defined as a normal EE-logic containing (*) and TT, and a nontrivial anti-realist system as a noncollapsing one. There are exactly two trivial anti-realist systems: the inconsistent system and the system of classical propositional logic with MM and EE as redundant operators. If “KT”*\text{“}KT\text{”*} (“KT”\text{“}KT\text{”} plus (*)) does not collapse, it is the smallest nontrivial anti-realist system. If it does collapse, there are no nontrivial anti-realist systems. The main result of this paper is the existence of nontrivial anti-realist systems. However, some facts about all nontrivial anti-realist systems will first be listed and briefly discussed.

If SS is any nontrivial anti-realist system then:

However, one factive, surprisingly often ignored by anti-realists, is undoubtedly applicable to empirical language: the ordinary concept of knowledge. The obvious next step is to attempt a treatment of anti-realist systems in terms of something like possible worlds semantics. Even if no philosophical significance is attached to these structures, they represent a mathematically powerful technique for investigating modal logics. In the present case they will provide a proof that nontrivial anti-realist systems exist.

The usual semantic notions must first be adapted to the language 𝕃\mathbb{L} with the new operator EE. Now if a nontrivial anti-realist system SS were characterized by a class CC of frames in the manner sketched above, SS would be valid on every frame in CC, so that every frame in CC would be suitable for SS, and thus by Theorem 1 Mp→pMp\to p would be valid in every frame in CC, whence Mp→p∈SMp\to p\in S by the definition of characterization; but Mp→p∉SMp\to p\notin S. It is thus necessary to study anti-realist systems in terms of models, not just of frames.

A point of some philosophical significance can be extracted from the proof of Theorem 1. Intuitively, the feature of frames which it exploits to make trouble for anti-realist logic is that they permit world-propositions—propositions true at exactly one world. Now if world-propositions are problematic for the anti-realist, so presumably are worlds, and the anti-realist will wish to put the point in terms which do not presuppose the possible worlds framework. That, however, can be done, since a world-proposition can be recharacterized as a maximally specific consistent proposition. The argument can then be reproduced in an object language with propositional quantifiers, concluding that for the anti-realist no maximally specific propositions are contingently false, as follows. Let AA be a maximally specific proposition. Thus for any proposition BB, either AA entails BB or AA entails ¬B\neg B. In particular, either AA entails EAEA or AA entails ¬EA\neg EA. But if AA entails ¬EA\neg EA then EAEA is inconsistent (since it entails AA), so MEAMEA is inconsistent, so by (*) AA is inconsistent. Thus if AA is consistent, it entails EAEA. But then MAMA entails MEAMEA, and so AA. Thus if AA is possibly true, it is actually true. So no maximally specific propositions are contingently false. The argument does not immediately rule out contingently true maximally specific propositions, since although the fact that MAMA entails AA means that M¬AM\neg A entails M¬MAM\neg MA in a normal system, the anti-realist has already been seen to reject the Brouwerian inference from M¬MAM\neg MA to ¬A\neg A. In any case, the argument gives a sort of formal vindication to the widespread sense that the notion of a nonactual possible world is anti-realistically suspect. It does not follow, of course, that anti-realists should reject the use of formal possible worlds model theory in proving results about modal logic, for they can regard it as a purely algebraic device; a model ⟨W,R,V⟩\langle W,R,V\rangle does not really assign truth-values to all propositions at each member of WW, but only to those propositions expressible in the language (relative to an assignment of propositions to the propositional variables), and the set of all sentences of the object language true at a given world may not itself be equivalent to a single sentence of that language. What the anti-realist should not do is to regard the possible worlds semantics as having philosophically explanatory force—an independently plausible conclusion.

A trivial model of an anti-realist system SS is a model of SS throughout which MA→AMA\to A is true for every A∈WffA\in\mathrm{Wff}. Trivial models are evidently of little use in proving the existence of nontrivial anti-realist systems. The obvious method of proof is by construction of a nontrivial model—and any nontrivial anti-realist system has a nontrivial model, its canonical model.

Theorems 3 and 5 yield a choice between hyperintensional and merely intensional nontrivial anti-realist systems. This in turn suggests a choice between two kinds of explanation for the failure of E(p&q)→EpE(p\mathbin{\&}q)\to Ep from an anti-realist standpoint. On the hyperintensional view, exemplified by “KT”*\text{“}KT\text{”*}, the principle fails because EE is ‘too intensional’. For instance, its failure might be traced to the point that an answer to the question ‘Is it the case that pp and qq?’ is not explicitly an answer to the question ‘Is it the case that pp?’ Here the emphasis is on occurrent linguistic or intellectual acts. On the alternative, merely intensional view, exemplified by “KT”*C\text{“}KT\text{”*}C, E(p&q)→EpE(p\mathbin{\&}q)\to Ep fails because EE is ‘not intensional enough’. For instance, its failure might be traced to the possibility of a situation in which the information that pp and qq is accessible even though the information that pp is not accessible, because the information that pp and qq is accessible only in a form in which it is not articulated as a conjunction, or as that conjunction; the consequence pp becomes buried. Both the fine-grained and the coarse-grained alternatives seem to merit further investigation.

A particularly interesting version of the fine-grained explanation goes as follows (its fine-grainedness will emerge in the discussion); I owe it to Kit Fine. Suppose that cognition of whatever kind EE represents comes in levels of increasing self-reflectiveness; such a hierarchy might be independently motivated by reference to epistemic cousins of the semantic paradoxes (cf. e.g. Burge [3] and [4]). What is cognized at a given level includes both what is cognized at a lower level and the results of reflection on that lower-level cognition. Cognition at a level ii may be represented as EiE_i. It does satisfy the conjunction principle: we have Ei(p&q)→EipE_i(p\mathbin{\&}q)\to E_ip for every level ii. As just noted, we also have Eip→EjpE_ip\to E_jp whenever i≤ji\leq j. Moreover, we have an anti-realist axiom schema A↔MEiAA\leftrightarrow ME_iA at each level, where ii is higher than the level of any operator which appears in AA. The unsubscripted use of EE can then be explained as context-relative, much as in some treatments of the epistemic paradoxes: the implicit level of EE in the context EAEA is the lowest one higher than the level of any operator in AA. An explanation for the failure of E(A&B)→EAE(A\mathbin{\&}B)\to EA is then forthcoming, since BB may have operators of a higher level than any in AA, so that A&BA\mathbin{\&}B also does. Just such a substitution in E(p&q)→EpE(p\mathbin{\&}q)\to Ep was used at (b) above to show its failure in nontrivial anti-realist systems.8

The use of propositional variables complicates the picture, since there is no limit to the level of the propositions which may be substituted for them. If they are assigned fixed numerical levels at all, then some propositional variable pp will be assigned a level ii no lower than that of some other propositional variable qq, in which case E(p&q)→EpE(p\mathbin{\&}q)\to Ep will be read as Ei(p&q)→EipE_i(p\mathbin{\&}q)\to E_ip and thus come out valid. Nor can a left inverse of |M|\lvert M\rvert be constructed in the Lindenbaum algebra, for the same example shows that |M|\lvert M\rvert is not 1-1. One might try to gerrymander an equivalence relation on wff which was congruence with respect to the operations in EE and on the equivalence classes of which the functions induced by MM and EE were mutually inverse, but the point of the exercise would be obscure. These facts about (**) can also be put in the framework of modal logic. In place of the operator EE, consider any modal function E(p)E(p); in a normal modal logic it obeys substitution of equivalents. Thus any normal modal logic which contains both TT and a thesis of the form p↔E(Mp)p\leftrightarrow E(Mp) collapses.

The use of the term ‘anti-realism’ for an epistemic conception of truth is loosely based on that of Michael Dummett (although the framework of classical logic is not Dummettian); his anti-realist with respect to the past claims, for example, that “The only notion of truth for past-tense statements which we could have acquired … is that which coincides with the justifiability of assertions of such statements” [5, p. 363]. The interpretation of (*) as an account of truth exploits the redundant truth operator, but this is not a problematic move for Dummett’s anti-realist [5, p. 234]. Of course, if EE is interpreted as a nonepistemic operator then (*) does not express a form of anti-realism: a realist who thought that the actual world was the only possible one might assert (*), reading EE as LL or MM. Such unintended readings can hardly be ruled out by formal means alone. Just as modal provability logics are ones which it is interesting but not compulsory to interpret in terms of provability, so anti-realist logics are ones which it is interesting but not compulsory to interpret in terms of anti-realism.

A weaker philosophical doctrine, perhaps associated with the verification principle of the logical positivists, characterizes what it is to have some truth value or other, rather than what it is to have a given truth value, in epistemic terms.
