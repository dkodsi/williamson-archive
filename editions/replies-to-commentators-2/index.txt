# Replies to commentators

Timothy Williamson

Philosophical Books 45 (2004): 313–323.

# Reply to Owens

In ‘Williamson on Scepticism and Rationality’, David Owens argues that the critique of scepticism in Knowledge and Its Limits attributes an unnecessarily strong premise to the sceptic: the sameness of evidence thesis, that the subject in the good case (a common sense scenario, in which appearances are veridical) and the subject in the bad case (a corresponding sceptical scenario, in which appearances are not veridical) have exactly the same evidence. According to Owens, the sceptic can get by just as well with the weaker equal rationality thesis, that the subject in the good case and the subject in the bad case are equally rational in holding any given belief. For the sceptic is supposed to use the sameness of evidence thesis by arguing that since our evidence is not strong enough for knowledge in the bad case, it is not strong enough for knowledge in the good case either. Thus the sceptic uses sameness of evidence only to infer the corollary that the evidence in the good case and the evidence in the bad case provide equally strong support for any given belief. That corollary is equivalent to the equal rationality thesis on an evidentialist conception of rational belief (such as sceptics typically assume). The sceptic could appeal directly to equal rationality without deriving it from sameness of evidence.

Owens’s reason for seeking a weaker premise for the sceptic is to avoid my argument against the sameness of evidence thesis. He is right that one cannot deduce sameness of evidence from equal rationality without auxiliary assumptions. We can put the point in probabilistic terms. Let $e(s)$ be the subject’s evidence in case $s$; let $g$ and $b$ be the good and bad cases respectively. Suppose that the degree to which the evidence in a case $s$ supports a proposition $p$ is the probability of $p$ conditional on the evidence in $s$, $\mathrm{P}(p\mid e(s))$ (p. 212).1 We can now state the two theses formally:

SAMENESS OF EVIDENCE
$e(g) = e(b)$
EQUAL RATIONALITY
For every proposition $p$,
$\mathrm{P}(p\mid e(g)) = \mathrm{P}(p\mid e(b))$

Clearly, SAMENESS OF EVIDENCE entails EQUAL RATIONALITY. The latter is equivalent to the claim that the evidence in the good case and the evidence in the bad case support each other to the maximal degree:

MUTUAL SUPPORT
$\mathrm{P}(e(b)\mid e(g)) = \mathrm{P}(e(g)\mid e(b)) = 1$

EQUAL RATIONALITY entails MUTUAL SUPPORT because, trivially, $\mathrm{P}(e(g)\mid e(g)) = \mathrm{P}(e(b)\mid e(b)) = 1$ and, by EQUAL RATIONALITY, $\mathrm{P}(e(g)\mid e(g)) = \mathrm{P}(e(g)\mid e(b))$ and $\mathrm{P}(e(b)\mid e(g)) = \mathrm{P}(e(b)\mid e(b))$. Conversely, MUTUAL SUPPORT entails EQUAL RATIONALITY because, given the former, $\mathrm{P}(p\mid e(g)) = \mathrm{P}(p\mid e(b) \mathbin{\&} e(g))$ since $\mathrm{P}(e(b)\mid e(g)) = 1$ and $\mathrm{P}(p\mid e(b)) = \mathrm{P}(p\mid e(b) \mathbin{\&} e(g))$ since $\mathrm{P}(e(g)\mid e(b)) = 1$, so $\mathrm{P}(p\mid e(g)) = \mathrm{P}(p\mid e(b))$. But MUTUAL SUPPORT does not entail SAMENESS OF EVIDENCE, because $e(g)$ and $e(b)$ might differ in truth-value in some worlds even if the prior probability of a difference in truth-value is 0. If there is an uncountable infinity of worlds, they cannot all have non-zero probabilities; if there is only a countable infinity of worlds, they cannot all have the same non-zero probability; even if there are only finitely many worlds, some distributions give some of them zero probability.2 Alternatively, on a fine-grained conception, $e(g)$ and $e(b)$ might be distinct but logically equivalent propositions. Consequently, since MUTUAL SUPPORT entails EQUAL RATIONALITY, EQUAL RATIONALITY does not entail SAMENESS OF EVIDENCE. Nevertheless, the abstruseness of the cases in which EQUAL RATIONALITY holds without SAMENESS OF EVIDENCE raises the question: how significant is the difference between them?

Owens suggests another justification for equal rationality: that appearances to the subject are the same in the good and bad cases. According to him:

The claim on which Williamson insists—that one party has a further piece of evidence available to him—makes no odds in our assessment of the rationality of these beliefs. The fact that our subject perceives that p is, so far as his rationality goes, screened off by the fact that it appears to him that p. (p. 309)

But this is wrong. Owens is conceding, at least for the sake of argument, that the subject’s evidence in the good case includes the proposition $p$ about the external world; the subject perceives that it is the case, thereby knowing that it is the case, which on the conception of evidence defended in the book makes it part of the subject’s total evidence. Thus $e(g)$ entails $p$; consequently $\mathrm{P}(p\mid e(g)) = 1$. By contrast, $e(b)$ does not entail $p$; it merely contains the proposition that $p$ appears to be the case and others of a similar sort. They may make $p$ probable but surely do not make it certain; hence $\mathrm{P}(p\mid e(b)) < 1$. Thus, in practice, the failure of sameness of evidence induces a failure of the equal rationality thesis.3

For maximum generality, let us continue the discussion without assuming a probabilistic reading of the theses at issue. Owens’s strategy for shielding the sceptic from my argument still fails, for there is a critique of the equal rationality thesis exactly analogous to my critique of sameness of evidence. In fact, as I emphasised in the book, the critique of sameness of evidence is quite schematic: it works on just about any understanding of ‘evidence’ (p. 178). Thus it works even if one reinterprets the subject’s ‘evidence’ in a given case as the function taking each proposition to the degree to which it is rational to believe it in that case, under which reinterpretation the words originally used to express the sameness of evidence thesis now express the equal rationality thesis. The argument about scepticism goes through as before.

Let me explain a little more fully. I suggest that the sceptic’s basis for accepting sameness of evidence is a conception of rationality on which one must always be in a position to know what rationality demands, and a corresponding conception of evidence on which one must always be in a position to know what one’s evidence is. I show how that conception implies that one has exactly the same evidence in the good and bad cases.4 However, by an argument of the same form, it also implies that one has exactly the same evidence in successive members of a sorites series of cases, and therefore the same evidence in the first and last members of the series, which is absurd, for they differ manifestly from each other.5 That refutes the conception on which one must always be in a position to know what one’s evidence is, and thereby removes the sceptic’s justification for the sameness of evidence thesis. Roughly speaking: evidence must sometimes differ between cases that are indiscriminable to the subject because indiscriminability (in the relevant sense) is a non-transitive relation. If the sceptic falls back on the equal rationality thesis, as Owens recommends, then the justification for accepting it will be a conception on which one must always be in a position to know how rational it is for one to believe a given proposition. That conception implies that it is equally rational to believe a given proposition in the good and bad cases.6 However, by an argument of the same form, it also implies that it is equally rational to believe a given proposition in successive members of a sorites series of cases, and therefore equally rational to believe it in the first and last members of the series, which is absurd, for they differ manifestly from each other.7 That refutes the conception on which one must always be in a position to know how rational it is for one to believe a given proposition, and thereby removes the sceptic’s justification for the equal rationality thesis. Roughly speaking: the rationality of believing a given proposition must sometimes differ between cases that are indiscriminable to the subject because indiscriminability is a non-transitive relation. Thus, after the retreat that Owens recommends, the sceptic is just as vulnerable as before to the underlying critique.

Similar problems face Owens’s emphasis on appearances as allegedly playing some privileged role in the theory of rational belief. If what is supposed to be special about appearances is that one is always in a position to know how things appear to one, that claim is refuted by yet another argument of the same form. Appearances must sometimes differ between cases that are indiscriminable to the subject because indiscriminability is a non-transitive relation. One is not always in a position to know how things appear to one. Consequently, there is no obvious reason to assign appearances a privileged role in an account of rationality or evidence.

Owens and I agree that in many cases what matters is causal sensitivity to one’s evidence rather than knowledge of one’s evidence. However, this point will not do the work that Owens requires of it. For causal sensitivity differs between the good case and the bad case. In the good case, I am causally sensitive to whether I have hands; it would be a misunderstanding of the notion of causal sensitivity to insist that I am causally sensitive only to whether I appear to myself to have hands. In the bad case, by contrast, I am not causally sensitive to whether I have hands; I am causally sensitive only to whether I appear to myself to have hands. Thus considerations of causal sensitivity do not support the equal rationality thesis.

Whether stated in terms of rationality or evidence, the diagnosis of scepticism stands.

# Reply to Joyce

In ‘Williamson on Evidence and Knowledge’, Jim Joyce contests my account of evidence, as Owens does, but from a different starting point. The focus of Joyce’s critique is my equation E = K of the subject’s total evidence with the totality of the subject’s knowledge. He agrees that all evidence is propositional, and grants that all knowledge is evidence, but rejects the converse claim that all evidence is knowledge.

The first part of Joyce’s case is that evidence comes in degrees, rather than being all or nothing. The issue here is not whether the evidence supports different hypotheses to different degrees; everyone in the debate acknowledges that it does; in the book, those degrees are measured as probabilities.8 Joyce is claiming something more: that the property of belonging to the total body of evidence that does the supporting comes in degrees, so that, for a given subject at a given time, a proposition p may be evidence to a higher degree than a proposition q is, while q is evidence to a higher degree than a third proposition r is. Of course, if knowledge also came in degrees, one could consistently combine the gradational conception of evidence with the equation E = K, read as claiming that each proposition is evidence for a given subject at a given time to exactly the degree to which it is known by that subject at that time. However, Joyce assumes that knowledge does not come in degrees; I happily accept that assumption.

It is not entirely clear how far Joyce is using the word ‘evidence’ to express the concept we do have and how far he is using it to express the concept we ought to have. However, I will not press that concern, for it would be a hollow victory for me if it turned out that my account fitted the concept we do have only in virtue of features that differentiated it from the concept we ought to have.

The gradationalist account of evidence also needs to be reconciled with the agreed point that one’s evidence is a proposition or set of propositions. The gradationalist appears to be identifying one’s evidence with a function from propositions to degrees of evidentiality. But such a function is not itself a proposition or set of propositions. For example, it is obscure how one could apply truth-functions such as conjunction and disjunction to functions from propositions to degrees of evidentiality (as I read him, Joyce is not advocating a form of fuzzy logic). However, we might interpret Joyce as proposing that the concept of evidence divides into an infinite family of concepts of evidence to (at least) degree $d$, for all degrees $d$ of evidentiality. For each such degree $d$, one’s evidence to degree $d$ would be a proposition or set of propositions. At a given time, each proposition would then be associated with a possibly infinite family of probabilities, its probability on one’s evidence to degree $d$ at that time for each degree $d$. Which of those probabilities should one attempt to match one’s degree of confidence in the proposition to? Joyce does not say. If the proposition has probability 0.9 on one’s evidence of at least some high degree $d$ but probability 0.1 on one’s evidence of at least some lower degree d*, how confident should one be? No developed gradational account of evidence has been put on the table for assessment.

A further unclarity concerns what exactly the degrees of evidentiality are degrees of. Joyce writes:

While I have no theory of evidential status to offer, I do claim that, minimally, e’s status as evidence improves with the subject’s level of confidence in its truth and its resilience in the face of learning experiences that add true beliefs to her evidence. The best sort of evidence must be believed with certainty and be completely resilient under the learning of truths. (p. 299f)

Joyce explains ‘resilience’ by saying:

A belief that $h$ is resilient with respect to e* to the extent that learning e* does not greatly alter the believer’s level of confidence in $h$. (p. 299, fn.)

Thus his two conditions for the best sort of evidence might in principle be met as a result of extreme dogmatism. For example, suppose that Terry believes with complete certainty, in a way that most of us would count as highly irrational, that Elvis lives. Terry’s belief that Elvis lives is also highly resilient under the learning of truths, because that does not greatly alter Terry’s level of confidence that Elvis lives. Even if Terry learns the truth that his belief that Elvis lives is highly irrational, he may still remain completely confident that, even so, Elvis does live. What if he learns the truth that Elvis is dead? But imagine that, funnily enough, Elvis does live, although that fact has no influence on Terry’s confidence that Elvis lives. Thus Terry’s belief that Elvis lives would count as the best sort of evidence by the criteria that Joyce specifies. Even granted that Elvis is dead, Terrry’s belief will still count as a very good sort of evidence, since it is highly resilient under the learning of truths. Terry can then use the proposition that Elvis lives to support his theories about the life-enhancing power of ice-cream and so on. Surely this is not the notion of evidence that we want. Whatever Terry thinks, he has very little evidence for his theories. The epistemic dimension is exactly what is missing: Terry does not know that Elvis lives. Resilience under the learning of truths is an inadequate subjective Bayesian substitute for knowledge. Like knowledge, quality of evidence is not a subjective matter. The gradational conception of evidence is not yet a promising alternative to the equation E = K.

Joyce goes on to argue that false propositions can constitute evidence. He considers a juror who believes that the accused was innocent because she accepts the false testimony of a convincing but lying witness who said that the accused was asleep at the time of the murder. The juror knows that the witness said that the accused was asleep at the time; she believes but does not know that the accused was asleep then. On my view, the proposition that the witness said that the accused was asleep is part of the juror’s evidence; the proposition that the accused was asleep is not part of her evidence. Joyce objects:

The witness’s testimony, taken by itself, provides no reason at all for the juror to believe this [that the accused is innocent]. Barring recherché cases, a juror will only regard the witness’s assertion as evidentially relevant to the guilt of the accused if she holds the further (false) belief that the witness is reliable. Thus, if we want to present the reasons that a juror might have for believing the accused innocent then we have no option but to specify some false beliefs that she holds. (p. 304)

Joyce’s description of the case seems wrong in an important respect. Without being sure that the witness is reliable, the juror may believe and even know that there is a general tendency for people to speak truly at least somewhat more often than they speak falsely. That is enough to make her knowledge that the witness said that the accused was asleep evidentially relevant to the guilt of the accused. Thus we have reason to reject the further claim that Joyce offers me:

A person speaks falsely when he aims to rationalise the juror’s decision by saying (as we ordinarily do) that she had evidence that the defendant was asleep. (p. 302)

Of course, if we want to present what the juror believes about her evidence for the innocence of the accused, we shall need to present some false beliefs. She treats as part of her evidence propositions that are not in fact part of her evidence. Similarly, if we want to present what the juror believes about her knowledge, we may need to present some false beliefs. She may believe falsely that she knows that the accused was asleep at the time of the murder. But all that is just what one would expect given the equation E = K.

To allow the content of one’s mere beliefs to be part of one’s evidence seems to make evidence too easy to manufacture, as we saw in the case of Terry’s belief that Elvis lives. Joyce replies to this charge. He supposes that I go to hear a sermon by Monsignor Menteur, believing in advance that he is a liar and that there have been no miracles, but also that the occurrence of miracles would provide excellent evidence for theism and that if I hear his sermon he will convince me that there have been many miracles. The sermon has the expected effect. According to Joyce, an equation of evidence with belief (E = B) has the reasonable consequence that I violate the requirement to proportion my beliefs to the evidence when I decide to go to the sermon, but not after Menteur has convinced me that there have been many miracles.

It is not clear whether Joyce means that, at the earlier stage, I violate the proportionality requirement directly, by having a degree of confidence in some proposition (which one?) disproportionate to my evidence then, or only indirectly, by doing something that on my evidence at the time was likely to bring about false beliefs later.

At any rate, consider my later belief in theism. If mere beliefs count as part of my evidence, my belief that there have been many miracles is part of my evidence. I believe it with certainty and, since I burn with the zeal of the convert, the belief is highly resilient under the learning of truths (perhaps not perfectly resilient). Thus, by Joyce’s criteria, my later belief that there have been many miracles counts as evidence of a very good sort. Moreover, we may assume that the notion of a miracle at issue is such that my belief that there have been many miracles strongly supports my belief in theism. Thus, on Joyce’s account, I have excellent evidence that there is a god, with respect both to its standing as evidence and to its evidential support for the proposition that there is a god. But that is the wrong result. I do not have good evidence that there is a god; my belief that there is a god is in terrible evidential shape. All I have in its support is credulous rubbish that survives only because I protect it with religious fervour from the implications of critical questioning (if you need to, make that a stipulation about the example). The objection to E = B stands: it makes evidence too easy to acquire. By contrast, E = K has no problem in handling the case. Monsignor Menteur’s sermon does not give me knowledge that there have been many miracles. Thus the proposition that there have been many miracles is not part of my evidence. Whatever I believe to the contrary, my belief that there is a god is very poorly supported by my evidence.

# Reply to Heathcote

In section I of ‘KT and the Diamond of Knowledge’, Adrian Heathcote challenges the basis of epistemic logic, the idea of substituting for the □ of modal logic an operator ‘K’ for knowledge. His challenge depends on a radical claim:

whenever I don’t know that p is false I also don’t know that it is true. (p. 287)

As he notes, this claim appears to collapse into universal scepticism. For, by the factivity of knowledge, if I know that p is true then p is true. If p is true, then it is not false. But, by the factivity of knowledge again, if I know that p is false then p is false, so by contraposition, if p is not false then I do not know that p is false. Chaining those conditionals together by transitivity, we deduce the platitude that if I know that p is true then I do not know that p is false. But if we combine that platitude with Heathcote’s quoted claim, we deduce that if I know that p is true then I do not know that p is true. Since that is a reductio ad absurdum of the antecedent, we can conclude unconditionally that I do not know p, whatever p is. Heathcote is no universal sceptic. He accepts that we know things and that the argument is therefore unsound. He also agrees that knowledge is factive. His diagnosis is that the error was to treat knowledge as modal. But it is obscure what ‘treating knowledge as modal’ has to do with the problem. I have just stated the argument in ordinary English, without assuming that it can be correctly formalised in modal logic. Construing ‘knows’ as a simple predicate rather than a modal operator, as Heathcote recommends, makes no obvious difference to the validity of the argument. Every case in which I know that p is true is a counterexample to his quoted claim.

One might conjecture that Heathcote has confused ‘know that’ with ‘know whether’. One knows whether P if and only if one either knows that P or knows that not P. Therefore, one knows whether P if and only if one knows whether not P (given that one knows that not not P if and only if one knows that P). However, in a footnote Heathcote distinguishes between ‘know that’ and ‘know whether’ when ‘know’ is not in the scope of negation. His view appears to be that although ‘S knows that P’ and ‘S knows that not P’ are not (logically) equivalent, their negations are equivalent. But, in classical logic, if A is equivalent to B, then the negation of A is equivalent to the negation of B. Thus, given Heathcote’s claim, the double negation of ‘S knows that P’ should be equivalent to the double negation of ‘S knows that not P’. Consequently, given that double negation behaves classically, ‘S knows that P’ should be equivalent to ‘S knows that not P’ after all. Heathcote therefore appears to be committed to a highly non-classical logic for sentences involving ‘knows’. Perhaps he would also reject the inference by contraposition in the argument for universal scepticism from ‘If I know that p is false then p is false’ to ‘If p is not false then I do not know that p is false’. At any rate, he gives the reader no idea as to what sort of non-classical logic he has in mind.

‘S does not know that P’ is sometimes said to presuppose ‘P’. If so, and the presuppositions of ‘I don’t know that p is false’ are met, then p is false, and therefore not true, so ‘I know that p is true’ is not true. But that would not justify Heathcote’s claim ‘whenever I don’t know that p is false I also don’t know that it is true’. For on the presuppositional view, the presuppositions of ‘I don’t know that p is false’ are inconsistent with the presuppositions of ‘I don’t know that p is true’. In any case, even if ‘S does not know that P’ does sometimes presuppose ‘P’ in some sense, the presupposition is easily cancelled. For example, one can say, ‘George thinks that he knows that his car is outside, but he is wrong; he doesn’t know that his car is outside, because it has been stolen’. In so saying, one is obviously not presupposing that George’s car is outside.

Consider a dialogue between two speakers discussing a conversation between a husband and wife:

Speaker 1: She knows that he is not telling the truth.

Speaker 2: She doesn’t know that he is not telling the truth, and no more do we. If she knew that he was not telling the truth, she would interrupt him. Very probably she knows that he is telling the truth.

There is no logical or linguistic impropriety in speaker 2’s speech. Yet on Heathcote’s view, speaker 2’s first claim is tantamount to ‘She doesn’t know that he is telling the truth’, so that speaker 2 is committed to a version of Moore’s paradox, ‘She doesn’t know that he is telling the truth, and very probably she knows that he is telling the truth’. On both logical and linguistic grounds, Heathcote’s claim about the negations of knowledge ascriptions should be dismissed.

In section II of his comments, Heathcote criticises my anti-luminosity argument. A condition is luminous if and only if in every case in which it obtains one is in a position to know that it obtains (p. 95).9 Roughly speaking, I argued that only trivial conditions are luminous by showing how to construct, for any non-trivial condition, a process at some point in which the condition obtains although one is not in a position to know that it obtains. Unfortunately, Heathcote’s criticisms depend on a pervasive misreading of the argument. He treats descriptions of a specific process as if they were intended as general principles about knowledge, despite my explicit warnings to the contrary.10 For my purpose in constructing a counterexample, one process is enough.

The specific process is such, I argue, that one knows at a time during it that the condition obtains (in this case, that one feels cold) only if the condition does in fact obtain at a slightly later time. Heathcote asks:

why is it necessary for me to feel cold in the future for me to know that I feel cold now? (p. 289)

The answer is that it is not necessary, nor does the argument imply otherwise. The claim linking knowledge at the earlier time to feeling cold at the later time is contingent on the description of the example, in which relations are specified between one’s states at the different times. In particular, it is part of the description of the example that one does not die in mid-process, the possibility to which Heathcote appeals. He has misread my material conditional as a strict conditional. When this mistake is corrected, Heathcote’s arguments in his section II dissolve.

Heathcote makes the same mistake several times in section III, when he criticises my application of the anti-luminosity argument to show that one can know without being in a position to know that one knows. In my example, if a tree is 666 inches tall then the subject (whose eyesight is terrible) does not know that it isn’t 665 inches tall. Since the subject knows that the tree is 666 inches tall only if it is 666 inches tall, it follows trivially that if he knows that the tree is 666 inches tall then he does not know that it isn’t 665 inches tall. “This is false,” according to Heathcote. Presumably he has in mind that if the subject did know that the tree was 666 inches tall, he could know by deduction that it was not 665 inches tall. But Heathcote has forgotten what he noted a few paragraphs earlier, that the conditionals in my argument are stipulated to be material. In the example at issue, the subject certainly does not know that the tree is 666 inches tall; his eyesight is far too bad for that. Therefore, on the material reading of ‘if’, the conditional ‘If he knows that the tree is 666 inches tall then he does not know that it isn’t 665 inches tall’ is true.

The description of the example involves a subject who has some knowledge of his own epistemic limitations. Heathcote treats this as if it were the claim that one’s epistemic limitations always enable one to know those limitations, which is indeed false. But the claim is not being made. One’s knowledge of one’s epistemic limitations is a specific feature of the example as constructed, not an absurd claim about knowledge in general. When these mistakes are corrected, Heathcote’s arguments in section III dissolve too.11

In section IV, Heathcote comments on my discussion of various axioms of epistemic logic. His remarks are based on various technical errors.

Of the B axiom (‘if a proposition is true then I know that I don’t know that it is not true’), Heathcote says:

This fails for all those propositions that are true but not known to be true. (p. 293)

That is incorrect. For example, suppose that the number of hairs on my head is even. I surely know that I do not know whether the number of hairs on my head is even (I do not even have a belief as to whether it is even). Thus I do not know that the number of hairs on my head is even, but I also know that I do not know that the number of hairs on my head is not even. There is no counterexample to the B axiom here, even though the relevant proposition is true but not known to be true. In fact, the B axiom follows easily from the S5 axiom (‘if I don’t know that a proposition is not true then I know that I don’t know that it is not true’) by factivity, for if a proposition is true then I don’t know that it is not true. An S5 epistemic logic is adequate for many simple situations of ignorance, even though it is far too strong for a logic of knowledge in general. S5 is used in many fruitful applications of epistemic logic to computer science and economics where the possibility of ‘propositions that are true but not known to be true’ is crucial.

Heathcote comments on the Lemmon-Scott rule of disjunction. An epistemic logic provides the rule just in case, whenever it has a theorem of the form $\mathrm{K}\mathrm{A}_1 \vee \ldots \vee \mathrm{K}\mathrm{A}_n$, some $\mathrm{A}_i$ is also a theorem. According to Heathcote:

the Lemmon-Scott rule of disjunction is a very plausible principle for an account of knowledge to satisfy, since it really just generalises the facticity [TW: he presumably means factivity] of knowledge. Thus any principle that is in conflict with it must suffer the gravest suspicions. (p. 292)

But the Lemmon-Scott rule has very little connection with factivity. It is provided by many weak epistemic logics such as K, KD, K4 and KD4 which lack factivity, because $\mathrm{K}p \to p$ is not a theorem. It is even provided by GL, the Gödel-Löb logic of formal provability, whose characteristic axiom is $\mathrm{K}(\mathrm{K}p \to p) \to \mathrm{K}p$, which on the provability reading of ‘K’ says that provability is provably factive for $p$ only in the special case in which $p$ is already provable. In GL one can prove that provability is not both factive and provably factive for some formulas, for GL has some theorems of the form $\neg((\mathrm{K}\mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{A}) \mathbin{\&} \mathrm{K}(\mathrm{K}\mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{A}))$. Equally, the Lemmon-Scott rule is not provided by the epistemic logics KTB and S5, even though they have the theorem $\mathrm{K}p \to p$ and do not seem to violate even the spirit of factivity in any intelligible sense. One certainly cannot maintain that every logic that fails to provide the Lemmon-Scott rule has a theorem that violates factivity in spirit if one concedes that the logic KT (which both has the axiom $\mathrm{K}p \to p$ and provides the rule) has no theorem that violates factivity in spirit, for some sublogics of KT fail to provide the rule. For example, the sublogic whose characteristic axiom is $\mathrm{K}(\mathrm{K}p \to p)$ violates the rule, precisely because $\mathrm{K}p \to p$ is not a theorem. The significance of the Lemmon-Scott rule of disjunction and other such rules depends on a subtle interplay, ignored by Heathcote’s remarks, between the notion of theoremhood represented in the metalanguage by the turnstile and the notion of knowledge represented by the ‘K’ operator in the object language.12

In Heathcote’s final section V, he briefly objects to my account of knowing as a mental state. The bottom line of his objection is “Factive states are not in the head” (p. 294). That is merely to assume without argument an internalist conception of the mental against which I and many proponents of externalism before me have been at pains to argue. Heathcote makes no attempt to address those arguments.13

It is unnecessary because a tautologous condition is luminous even though a person in it, and in a position to know that they are in it (as one always is), may fail to know that they are in it, because they do not reflect on the matter. It is also insufficient, because it holds vacuously if one can be in C but can never be in a position to know that one is in C; such a condition is not luminous. Perhaps Heathcote mistakenly treated a stipulation about the example in the anti-luminosity argument which implied that, in those particular cases, one knew that one was in C whenever one was in a position to know that one was in C (p. 97) as if it were part of the definition of ‘luminous’.

[11] Another mistake is Heathcote’s claim that I “assimilate reliability to the notion of stability: so that to know I must have knowledge under perturbation of the conditions” (p. **). Such a constraint is liable to generate a sorites paradox. My claim is rather that to know one must have truth under perturbation of the conditions.

[12] I can make nothing of Heathcote’s claim that “the condition $\mathrm{A} \to K\mathrm{A}$ says something far stronger than that trivial conditions are luminous, it says that all provable and refutable statements are known to be true” (p. 293). The displayed formula is not about provability or refutability ($K$ is an operator for knowledge, not for provability and refutability), still less does it imply the grotesque claim that all refutable statements are known to be true.

[13] Heathcote’s objection about the conjunction ‘knowing p and p is true’ depends on ignoring the distinction in the book between mental states and mental concepts of states (p. 28): ‘knowing p and p is true’ may express a concept that is not purely mental (in virtue of the second conjunct) of the very same mental state of which ‘knowing p’ expresses a purely mental concept. The book explicitly allows for such possibilities (p. 29).
