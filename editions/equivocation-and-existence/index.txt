# Equivocation and existence

Timothy Williamson

Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society 88 (1988): 109–127.

In this paper I want to connect two much-disputed questions:

(1)
Is it possible for two groups to employ unequivocally conflicting logics?
(2)
Is it possible to have properties without existing?

The connection is roughly as follows. Two groups who answer ‘Yes’ and ‘No’ respectively to question (2) may at least seem to employ conflicting logics. For one group may say ‘It is a basic principle of logic that having properties entails existence, just as it is a basic principle of logic that identity entails having the same properties’, while the other group says ‘Since Madame Bovary and Lady Jane Grey have the property of being pitied, not to mention that of being self-identical, in spite of not (now) existing, it is not a principle of logic, basic or otherwise, that having properties entails existence’. In view of the controversy around question (1), we are obliged to ask whether this is really an unequivocal conflict: do both groups mean the same by the word ‘existence’? In a more cautious formulation: are they using it with the same extension but conflicting beliefs about what that extension contains, or are they using it with different extensions and perhaps no conflicting beliefs?

In section I a style of argument is sketched to show that at least some apparent conflicts of logic are real ones. This style of argument does not show the apparent conflict of logic over (2) to be real, and section III gives prima facie reasons to think that it is indeed merely apparent, so that there are senses of ‘existence’ in which it obeys the proposed logical principle and senses in which it does not. A pragmatic question then arises as to which of these ‘existence’ concepts is the most useful. If we use ‘existence’ in a sense obedient to the proposed logical principle, the style of argument from section I secures us from threats of further equivocation (section II), whereas if we use ‘existence’ in a sense not so obedient we lose that security. Section IV argues that we therefore have good reason to use ‘existence’ in the former sense, which allows us to raise basic questions of ontology in an unequivocal way. Some of the resultant disputes will be transpositions of disputes about (2)—these will not therefore have been anodynely dissolved—for purported counter-examples to the principle about ‘existence’ become purported examples of ‘existence’ in a sense which undisputedly obeys that principle (section V). Nevertheless, an unequivocal conflict of ontology may be an improvement on an equivocal conflict of logic.

# I

If some principle strikes one as so compellingly obvious that one cannot imagine how it could reasonably be denied, and one interprets another person’s utterance as a denial of it, then one has a prima facie reason to believe that one’s interpretation may be wrong. Moreover, one’s basic principles of logic are likely to strike one as compellingly obvious in the relevant sense. So much is scarcely in dispute. More has been claimed, however: that the reason to doubt one’s interpretation is indefeasible, even that it is conclusive. Is it possible to have good reason to believe, all things considered, that someone is denying some of one’s basic logical principles? Even if it is not, is it possible that someone is denying some of one’s basic logical principles?1 This paper is concerned with the epistemological problem, not the ontological one. It is not concerned to ask what constitutes our convergent or divergent interpretations of the terms at issue (in virtue of what do we mean this rather than that by ‘exist’?). Rather it asks: what reason have the parties to an apparent conflict of logic to believe that they are not merely talking past each other, because of some equivocation, given their prima facie evidence to the contrary?

The case of negation can be used to illustrate a technique for arguing that an apparent conflict is a real one. Consider the difference between classical and intuitionist logic. The classical logician accepts the law of double negation elimination (DNE, that the negation of the negation of $A$ entails $A$); the intuitionist logician rejects it. This dispute has often been said to turn on a divergence between their understandings of negation.2 The suggestion is that each is correct in their use of their own ‘negation’, so that one could have a language with two ‘negations’, one behaving classically and one intuitionistically (‘a fusion of horizons’?). It is not as well known as it should be that this suggestion is provably false. For two operators in the same language which obey the laws common to both classical and intuitionist ‘negation’ are provably equivalent, so that one obeys double negation elimination if and only if the other does; the dispute between classical and intuitionist logic is real, not merely verbal.3

It is worth going into a little more detail. The parties to the dispute need to know whether they are talking past each other when they seem to disagree about DNE. Can they agree on a characterization of negation ($\mathord{-}$) in terms which they are not equally likely to understand in different senses? For example, it would not help to define $\mathord{-}A$ as $A\rightarrow\bot$ (where $\bot$ is an absurd proposition), since classical and intuitionist logicians are just as likely to diverge over their understandings of $\rightarrow$ as they are over their understandings of $\mathord{-}$. However, in both classical and intuitionist logic ‘negation’ obeys the laws of Reductio Ad Absurdum (RAA) and Ex Falso Quodlibet (EFQ). Why should they not agree to characterize negation as the operator which obeys RAA and EFQ? The immediate problem concerns uniqueness; perhaps several non-equivalent operators obey RAA and EFQ. That apparent possibility can be ruled out as follows. A monadic sentential operator $\mathrm{O}$ obeys RAA if and only if the following schema is valid, where $X$ and $Y$ are sets of assumptions and $\vdash$ is deducibility:

$\displaystyle\begin{array}{lc}\text{If} & \underline{X,A\vdash B\quad\text{and}\quad Y,A\vdash \mathrm{O}B} \\ \text{then} & X,Y\vdash \mathrm{O}A\end{array}$

It obeys EFQ if and only if this schema is valid:

$$A,\mathrm{O}A\vdash B$$

Now suppose that the operators $\mathrm{O}_1$ and $\mathrm{O}_2$ both obey RAA and EFQ. By EFQ for $\mathrm{O}_1$ we have $A,\mathrm{O}_1A\vdash A$ and $A,\mathrm{O}_1A\vdash \mathrm{O}_2A$, so by RAA for $\mathrm{O}_2$ we have $\mathrm{O}_1A\vdash \mathrm{O}_2A$; similarly, we have $\mathrm{O}_2A\vdash \mathrm{O}_1A$. Thus $\mathrm{O}_1$ and $\mathrm{O}_2$ are equivalent (as a corollary, DNE for $\mathrm{O}_1$ is equivalent to DNE for $\mathrm{O}_2$). By agreeing to characterize negation (up to logical equivalence) as the operator which obeys RAA and EFQ, classical and intuitionist logicians can come to a principled rejection of the idea that they are talking past each other.

Of course, a sceptic might wonder whether both sides mean the same by the deducibility symbol $\vdash$, and indeed not every characterization that one of them made of it would be acceptable to the other. Strictly speaking, the arguments below do not require this doubt to be resolved, but it does seem to be expressive of desperation rather than insight. As a matter of fact, both classical and intuitionist logicians treat $X\vdash A$ as meaning that you are committed to $A$ in making the set of assumptions $X$. It would otherwise be unclear that they could recognize each other as engaged in reasoning at all; to speak of classical and intuitionist logic would be to equivocate on the world ‘logic’. Suppose that there were distinct but equally legitimate ‘deducibility’ relations, one classical and one intuitionist, and that you discovered your beliefs to have a certain consequence in the sense of one but not in the sense of the other; should you accept that consequence or not? Or if the classical ‘deducibility’ relation were applicable only to ‘classical beliefs’ and the intuitionist one only to ‘intuitionist beliefs’, which would a conjunction of a ‘classical belief’ and an ‘intuitionist belief’ be? These are not promising lines of thought, and will be pursued no further here; $\vdash$ will provisionally be treated as unequivocal.

Two points do need to be noted.

(a) Even if we know just which inferences in a given language its speakers are prepared to accept, we cannot thereby conclusively determine just which inference schemata they are committed to, in the relevant sense of ‘schemata’. For a schema also has instances in extensions of their language; commitment to the schema requires them to accept these further instances. This notion of a schema was at work in the uniqueness proof; for example, EFQ for classical negation $\mathrm{O}_1$ was taken to have the instance $A,\mathrm{O}_1A\vdash \mathrm{O}_2A$, where $\mathrm{O}_2$ is intuitionist negation, so that $\mathrm{O}_2A$ is not a sentence of the original classical language. We certainly have dynamic commitments of this kind to inference schemata: my commitment to Modus Ponens is not simply a belief that the expressive powers of English are too limited to allow the formulation of a genuine counter-example. What does follow is that even if we come across speakers whose reasoning in their own language fits the schemata standardly associated with intuitionism, it remains at least a theoretical possibility that they are not committed to those schemata. However, this possibility is by no means untestable: behavioural dispositions bear on it just as they do on commitment to the infinitely many instances of a schema within a single language; it is just that the relevant exercises of them are displayed only when the language is enlarged. We can examine the dispositions of purported intuitionists and classical logicians: the uniqueness result is applicable to those who manifest commitment to EFQ and RAA.4

(b) What if classical logicians are mistaken in supposing EFQ and RAA to apply to their ‘negation’, or if intuitionists are making a similar mistake? The possibility seems a remote one, but two remarks on it are worth making. First, by definition it fails to rescue the claim that, properly interpreted, both parties are in the right. Second, it does not arise if the parties define negation (up to logical equivalence) as that operator which obeys EFQ and RAA—unless there is no such operator, which seems an even remoter possibility. Such stipulations will be at issue below.

The argument for the unequivocality of negation can be extended (given the use of classical or intuitionist logic) to the other standard logical constants, conjunction, disjunction, the material conditional, the quantifiers (indeed, one might regard the possibility of giving such an argument as a criterion of a logical constant). For present purposes the most relevant case is that of identity. Consider a dispute over, say, the identity of indiscernibles. Suppose that both parties accept the standard logical laws for identity: Reflexivity and Leibniz’s Law (the indiscernibility of identicals). Are they talking past each other, or do they mean the same by $=$ or ‘is identical with’? They can agree to characterize identity (up to logical equivalence) as the (binary) reflexive relation which obeys Leibniz’s Law, if they can show that all reflexive relations which satisfy Leibniz’s Law are logically equivalent; but they can show this. For suppose that $R_1$ and $R_2$ are such relations. Thus we have $\vdash \forall x\,R_2xx$; Leibniz’s Law for $R_1$ gives $\vdash \forall x\,\forall y\,(R_1xy\rightarrow(R_2xx\rightarrow R_2xy))$, whence uncontroversial logic gives $\vdash \forall x\,\forall y\,(R_1xy\rightarrow R_2xy)$. We have $\vdash \forall x\,\forall y\,(R_2xy\rightarrow R_1xy)$ similarly, and thus $\vdash \forall x\,\forall y\,(R_1xy\leftrightarrow R_2xy)$ (so the indiscernibility of $x$ and $y$ is sufficient for $R_1xy$ if and only if it is sufficient for $R_2xy$).5 Given classical logic, identity disputes do not turn on equivocation in the sign for identity. Remarks parallel to (a) and (b) above again apply. Note how strained it would be in this case to claim that the dispute turns on an equivocation in $\vdash$.

# II

Does the form of argument just exhibited apply to the concept of existence? On some views, I have already assumed a positive answer (for classical or intuitionist logic) by assuming that it applies to the quantifiers, for these views treat existence as a second-level concept to be identified with the existential quantifier. On the other views, there is a first-level concept of existence, but it is to be defined in terms of the existential quantifier by the formula $\exists y\,x=y$. However, it is unhelpful to take such views for granted when handling a dispute (over (2)) one party to which may hold that some things do not exist; for on that view, the quantifier ‘some’ has existential force only when implicitly or explicitly restricted to existing things. To be fair to such a view we can use quantifier-free formulae and a primitive existence predicate $E!$.6

What quantifier-free logical principles might govern $E!$? The obvious candidate is the principle queried in (2), that having properties entails existence. However, the concept of a property is too sensitive to metaphysical pressure to bear much weight in logic. Consider the similar case of Leibniz’s Law, which is sometimes formulated as the principle that identity entails having the same properties. On some views, a predicate does not stand for a property unless it ‘cuts nature at its joints’. Thus someone might claim not to be violating Leibniz’s Law in asserting that Cicero was Tully but Cicero was eloquent and Tully wasn’t, on the grounds that the line between eloquence and non-eloquence is not one of nature’s joints. Since we do not want Leibniz’s Law to be as toothless as that, we formulate it in terms of predicates rather than properties (where of course not every sentence involving ‘Cicero’ ascribes a predicate to him, as at least quotational contexts show). For similar reasons, what I shall call the Existence Principle (EP) is formulated in terms of predicates. It is the schema

$$Ft\vdash E!t$$

where ‘$t$’ may be replaced by any singular term (closed or open) and ‘$Ft$’ by any subject-predicate formula with the corresponding subject term.7 If the existence predicate were defined in terms of the existential quantifier, EP would be equivalent (given the standard laws for identity) to the classical rule of Existential Introduction, a rule that some ‘Free Logics’ accept and others reject.8 Now if terms denoting no existent are allowed then $E!t$ is not always a tautology (consider ‘God exists’), but if EP is not to make it one, the negation of $E!t$ must not itself be of the form $Ft$, so that a distinction is required between sentential negation and predicate negation; $t$ is not the subject term of every formula in which it appears. Such a distinction has been worked out in detail by Robert Stalnaker.9

Proponents of EP will deny that it is possible to have properties without existing; they will also deny that there are things which don’t exist, since they will infer ‘$x$ exists’ from ‘$x$ is a thing’. Conversely, opponents of EP hold that at least in some cases it is logically possible for $t$ to be $F$ without existing. Those who are prepared to quantify over a domain of things which is large enough to include $t$ will allow a good (non-substitutional) sense in which something can be $F$ without existing. Those who are prepared to quantify over a domain of properties which is large enough to include that of being $F$ will allow a good (non-substitutional) sense in which $t$ can have a property without existing. Those who are prepared to do both will allow a good sense in which something can have a property without existing.

Now consider an ontological dispute between two proponents of EP, in particular, an ontological dispute of the kind which can be expressed by means of a singular term. For example, one of them says ‘The number 7 exists’ or ‘God exists’, the other ‘It is not the case that the number 7 exists’ or ‘It is not the case that God exists’. Have they good reason to believe that they are not talking past each other, by equivocating on the word ‘exists’? Following the pattern of the previous section, one can argue that they have. For let the ‘existence’ predicates of the two be $F_1$ and $F_2$, and suppose that each is right in supposing his or her own ‘existence’ predicate to obey EP. Then since $F_1$ obeys EP, we have $F_2t\vdash F_1t$, and since $F_2$ obeys it, we have $F_1t\vdash F_2t$; thus the two ‘existence’ predicates are logically equivalent.10 Furthermore the argument shows that (up to logical equivalence) at most one predicate obeys EP; thus both parties can agree to define the ‘existence’ predicate as that which obeys EP, so neither need doubt that the other’s ‘existence’ predicate as so defined obeys EP. Note how strained it would be in this case, as in the earlier ones, to claim that there must be an equivocation on $\vdash$. In brief, for its proponents EP grounds a common understanding of ‘existence’ and permits genuine disagreement about what exists, just as Leibniz’s Law (with Reflexivity) grounds a common understanding of ‘identity’ and permits genuine disagreement about what is identical with what.

# III

Consider a dispute between a proponent and an opponent of EP, either about EP itself or about some specific issue of ontology. Have they good reason to believe that they are not talking past each other, by equivocating on the word ‘exists’? They certainly do not have the reason that the two proponents of EP had, for they cannot agree to define the ‘existence’ predicate as that which obeys EP. At the same time, they have a stronger reason than any in the previous case to believe that they are equivocating, since their beliefs about the logic of ‘exists’ are surely more fundamental to their understanding of it than are their beliefs about its extension (at least so far as concerns non-paradigmatic cases such as God and the number 7—oneself and the earth may be another matter).

It need hardly be added that there are indeed both proponents and opponents of EP, whether or not it is really with respect to the same predicate. Speaking for myself, I maintain that my ‘existence’ predicate obeys EP. Terence Parsons and Nathan Salmon, for example, would maintain that their ‘existence’ predicate or predicates does or do not obey EP.11 Moreover, enough technical work has been done on both sides to make it clear that both views can be made formally self-consistent. At least the appearance, if not the reality, of a dispute is real.

Is what one party asserts really what the other denies? Both are likely to claim that they are using their words—in particular, the word ‘exist’—in their everyday senses; but even if no unconscious philosophically motivated semantic change has occurred, the word ‘exist’ may have more than one everyday sense; these senses may vary in relative salience from one idiolect to another. It is certainly not clear that ordinary usage returns unequivocal answers to questions such as (2). For part of what needs to be explained is how some intelligent people can find it a blindingly obvious piece of common sense that what doesn’t exist just isn’t there to have properties, while others can find it a blindingly obvious piece of common sense that the dead have the property of being dead and therefore don’t exist, or that fictional characters have the property of being fictional characters and therefore don’t exist. We can be sure that ‘exist’ will not turn out to have two wholly unrelated senses, in the way that ‘bank’ has, but may it not turn out to have senses as different as those of ‘true’ in ‘true statement’ and ‘true friend’?

It would be implausible as well as depressing to suppose that it is the rule rather than the exception for a philosophical dispute to turn on mutual misunderstanding. If the misunderstandings were accidental, philosophy would be less difficult than it actually is; if essential, it would satisfy a kind of incommensurability thesis whose applications elsewhere are increasingly discredited. However, we have already seen that there is a widely applicable method of arguing that a philosophical dispute does not turn on mutual misunderstanding; the rules of the game have not been rigged in advance to favour the opposite claim. The question is what, if anything, can replace that method in cases, such as the present one, where it does not apply. If we can find no replacement, any remaining belief in the non-equivocality of the dispute over EP would be little better than blind faith: for although there may well be an initial presumption that we mean the same by same-sounding words, a given instance of such a presumption hardly deserves to survive the failure to find evidence in its favour, if we have looked in earnest.

It should be remembered, in assessing evidence for and against the homophonic translation of ‘exist’, that our situation is not that of the radical interpreter, who initially lacks all semantic knowledge of the alien language with which he or she is faced. Philosophical disputes are conducted against the background of a shared natural language, or at least, this one is. We have been given, not a general reason for doubting that we ever understand what others say, but a particular reason for doubting that our present interpretation of certain key words in a philosophical dispute is that which our interlocutor intends. To resolve such a doubt, we need a way of fixing the interpretation of those words in terms of that very large part of the background language whose interpretation is not, for present purposes, in doubt; there is no question of trying to rule out the sceptical possibility that our interlocutor’s interpretation of every expression of our apparently common language is wholly other than ours. At the same time, it will not suffice merely to define a key word in terms of an accepted synonym (‘to exist’ means to be) or other expressions so closely tied to the key word that any divergence in its interpretation is likely to extend to a divergence in theirs. This criterion is clearly not a precise one, but that is only to be expected, for the task is to assess the plausibility of a perfectly genuine doubt (do we mean the same by ‘exist’?) -not to exclude definitively the hyperbolical doubts of an hypothetical sceptic—and plausibility is not a precise matter.

It does not seem likely that the proponent and the opponent of EP will share other assumptions about the logic of ‘exist’ of a kind that would allow them to agree on a unique characterization of it (up to logical equivalence) in terms of its logical properties. In particular, it has already been seen that an attempt to define ‘exists’ in terms of the existential quantifier would be unlikely to help, since the opponent of EP would probably need to use ‘exists’ or the equivalent in specifying the appropriate domain of quantification. Nor does semantic ascent seem to help: for example, few opponents of EP will be prepared to accept that ‘$t$ exists’ is true if and only if ‘$t$’ designates, unless ‘designates’ is understood as ‘designates an existent’. The two parties might try to test for equivocation in the way that one does for non-logical predicates. For instance, they might try to give each other examples of things that satisfy the predicate ‘exists’ (‘To exist is to do what that stone is doing’). However, even if this method does not fail through fortuitous differences in ontology, the opponents of EP will be compelled also to give examples of things that do not satisfy the predicate ‘exists’, in order to distinguish it from what they conceive to be less restrictive predicates such as ‘is a thing’ and ‘is self-identical’; but the proponents of EP will view this attempt to point out particular things that do not satisfy ‘exists’ precisely as good evidence that the term is being used in a different sense.

For both parties, the doubt as to whether the dispute might turn on an equivocation must remain a somewhat abstract one, until they can find a non-homophonic translation of the other’s use of ‘exists’ into their own terms, a translation that makes better sense of what the other is saying and perhaps even wholly dispels the appearance of disagreement. Such translations would both confirm the doubt and enable something to be done about it. For instance, the opponent of EP may well be able to translate the proponent’s use of ‘exists’ by ‘is an object’ or by ‘is self-identical’.12 On such a translation, EP says that a subject of predicates is an object, or that it is self-identical, or something of the kind: principles whose acceptance by the proponent of EP its original opponent need have no difficulty in understanding and may even share. The converse translation is harder, since the diversity of ways of rejecting EP produces a corresponding diversity of translations. In some cases, the proponent of EP may well be able to translate the opponent’s use of ‘exists’ by ‘is causally efficacious’ or ‘has spatio-temporal location’. On such a translation, EP says that a subject of predicates is causally efficacious, or that it has spatio-temporal location, or something of the kind: principles whose rejection by the opponent of EP its original proponent need have no difficulty in understanding and may even share. There will be subtler cases too: an opponent of EP may hold that while both the null set and the person who would have resulted from the union of this sperm and this egg have the property of self-identity, the former ‘exists’ but the latter does not, perhaps because the latter contingently lacks causal efficacy or spatio-temporal location. Further exploration of such a philosopher’s position might suggest a more complex translation scheme for this case. In general, which scheme works, if any, will depend on which proponents and which opponents of EP are in question. Nevertheless, even in their crude state, the non-homophonic schemes promise to enable each party to make more sense of what the other is saying than do their homophonic alternatives. In so doing, they already constitute an argument in favour of the view that what seemed to be a stark clash of intuitions was not what it seemed.

In the worst case, we have a proponent and an opponent of EP at least one of whom cannot find a satisfactory non-homophonic translation of the other’s use of ‘exists’. It still does not follow that the homophonic translation is correct, nor even that it should provisionally be assumed to be correct. For it is perfectly possible that the proponent does not mean by any expression what the opponent means by ‘exists’, and/or that the opponent does not mean by any expression what the proponent means by ‘exists’. Moreover, in such circumstances it could still be fruitful—more fruitful than to persist with the homophonic scheme—for both parties to think of the opponent’s use of ‘exists’ as more restrictive than the proponent’s. That is, if $E!_p$ is the proponent’s ‘existence’ predicate and $E!_o$ is the opponent’s, it might be agreed that the truth of $E!_pt$ is necessary but not sufficient for the truth of $E!_ot$. It would not, of course, follow that the proponent’s acceptance of $E!_pt$ is necessary but not sufficient for the opponent’s acceptance of $E!_ot$. For the proponent might reject $E!_pt$, and therefore $E!_ot$, in a particular case where the opponent accepted $E!_ot$, and therefore $E!_pt$, because of some adventitious ontological dispute between them, independent of EP (for example, ‘$t$’ might be ‘God’, the proponent an atheist and the opponent a theist). To return to the argument, if we have $E!_ot\vdash E!_pt$ without $E!_pt\vdash E!_ot$, it is at least a live possibility that we have $Ft\vdash E!_pt$ without $Ft\vdash E!_ot$, and thus that the proponents are right that $E!_p$ obeys EP while the opponents are right that $E!_o$ does not. In any case, the argument for the equivocality thesis need not show both parties to be right: it need only show them both to be intelligible.

In this section I have sketched a case for the claim that parties to a dispute over EP are characteristically using ‘exists’ in different and non-equivalent senses. I take the case to be not decisive, but promising.13

# IV

Suppose that the argument of the previous section is correct in the most satisfying way: not only does the sense in which proponents of EP use ‘exists’ differ from the sense in which opponents use it, but the former sense makes EP valid and the latter makes it invalid. Is the dispute over EP therefore a purely verbal one, to be determined by considerations of stylistic convenience? It must be remembered that the situation is not perfectly symmetrical. We have already seen that two proponents of EP (for their own use of ‘exists’) have good reason to believe themselves to mean the same by ‘exists’; in contrast, two opponents of EP (for their own use of ‘exists’) have no such reason to believe themselves to mean the same by ‘exists’. Roughly speaking, there is only one sense of ‘exists’ in which it obeys EP, but many in which it does not. Thus agreement to use ‘exists’ in a sense obedient to EP, unlike agreement to use it in a sense disobedient to EP, provides a background against which parties to the agreement can go on to discuss their disagreements on fundamental issues of ontology without threat of equivocation on the word ‘exists’. The latter agreement cannot defuse the threat of equivocation constituted by such fundamental disagreements. Call those who accept EP for their own use of ‘exists’ EPers, and those who reject it non-EPers. Although non-EPers do not have disagreement over EP itself as an additional reason to fear equivocation amongst themselves, some of the arguments of the previous section apply, as obstacles to their attempts to defuse the threat of equivocation when they disagree with each other on fundamental issues of ontology.

Suppose that one non-EPer says ‘To exist it is necessary and sufficient to cause or to be caused’ while another says ‘To exist it is necessary and sufficient to perceive or to be perceived’; what reason have they to think that they mean the same by ‘exists’? Disagreement over the satisfaction conditions for a predicate does not automatically overturn the initial presumption that members of a speech community mean the same by it, of course, but this initial presumption is a feeble thing indeed if it can survive critical reflection only by default. It may be reasonable for non-EPers to believe, prior to seeking evidence, that they mean the same by ‘exists’, but it does not follow that this belief remains reasonable for them after they have tried and failed to find evidence for it (should they therefore refuse to look?). Perhaps the non-EPers will be able to confirm the belief by discovering substantive agreement amongst themselves as to the extension of ‘exists’. The trouble with this method is that, to the extent that it rules out the possibility of equivocation, it does so by limiting the room for ontological disagreement. In this sense, the question ‘What exists?’ may be a more radical one for EPers than for non-EPers, since the former can agree on its sense (by means of EP) without making non-logical assumptions as to its answer.

One might therefore ask, what is the most metaphysically interesting sense of ‘exists’? The non-EPer Terence Parsons raises the question ‘Why Should anyone Care Whether God Exists?’ and finds no reason why someone should care whether the God whom they worship is an ‘existent’ or a ‘non-existent’ God; one is reminded of the line that Robert Nozick quotes, ‘God is so perfect he doesn’t need to exist’.14 A notion of ‘non-existence’ with so little impact on a thing’s other properties is not of much metaphysical interest. No doubt many non-EPers would avoid such extravagances. Nevertheless, it seems to me that the deepest notion of ‘non-existence’, because the most radical, is the EPers’ one, on which if it is not the case that God exists then it is not the case that God has any kind of property whatsoever (not that of being worshipped, nor that of being God, nor even that of being self-identical), and thus not the case that God has any kind of being at all, however exiguous. It is also the notion that makes the prospect of one’s own death—as the end of one’s existence—most vertiginously absolute.

# V

In the remainder of this essay the word ‘existence’ will be used in the postulated sense in which it obeys EP, and which the non-EPer may take to be something like that of self-identity. This terminological readjustment is far from dissolving all the philosophical problems. For the non-EPer’s characteristic arguments can be reinterpreted as attempts, not to refute EP, but to exhibit by its means the bizarre variety of existents in the EPer’s sense. It is a matter of no great import whether the non-EPer’s intention is to discourage the EPer from so using the word ‘existence’; the point is that when the EPer asks ‘What exists?’ a genuine metaphysical question is being raised, to which the non-EPer’s arguments indicate substantive and surprising answers. The EPer’s characteristic (but not compulsory) response is to argue that EP does not have the consequences the non-EPer claims it to have.

Consider, for example, the argument ‘Casanova is famous, therefore Casanova exists’. It seems to be a straightforward application of EP, yet to hold out hope for, quite literally, survival after death. Since both premiss and conclusion are to be read as present-tensed, someone might propose to restrict EP to eternal sentences. That sounds ad hoc, and does not seem to go to the heart of the problem (it does not help with the intuitively similar argument ‘Vulcan is famous, therefore Vulcan exists’, where ‘Vulcan’ may purport to name either a god or a planet). Nor is the premiss simply to be denied; there is a perfectly good sense in which it does not just seem to be the case that Casanova is famous, but is the case. Furthermore, ‘Casanova is famous’ has every appearance of being a subject-predicate sentence with ‘Casanova’ as its subject term. Must one therefore conclude that EP leads to a pathetically etiolated concept of existence, on which Casanova does still exist, perhaps only in virtue of being self-identical? At this point the EPer may reply with an analysis of ‘Casanova is famous’ on which its logical form is not what its surface form suggests (a supporting argument: ‘Rogue elephants are famous’ does not entail ‘At least one rogue elephant is famous’). For instance, it may be proposed that the ‘logical subject’ of ‘Casanova is famous’ is not Casanova himself but a certain property which only he could instantiate, but which can exist even when he does not; the claim will not of course be that this property is itself famous—for it is indeed deeply obscure—but rather that it has an as yet unspecified second-order property of, as one might say, bearing fame.

The argument cannot be pursued here. Instead, I want to give a final application of the ideas about non-equivocality from section I. Much of the controversy over EP concerns apparently relational expressions such as ‘seeks’, ‘envies’ and ‘worships’. Is ‘Cecil envies Casanova, therefore Casanova exists’ an instance of EP? For purposes of argument I am quite prepared to accept that these transitive verbs really are relational expressions. A further question remains: does EP license inferences of the form $Rst\vdash E!t$? Can $Rst$ be bracketed as $(Rs)t$ and $Rs$ treated as an instance of $F$?

It is technically possible to give a negative answer. I have already appealed to Stalnaker’s predicate notation in regimenting for EP; it allows one to draw a sharp line between instances of $Ft$ and instances of $Rst$. To obtain a substitution instance of $Ft$ one must substitute a monadic predicate for $F$, and the only complex monadic predicates in his notation have the form $\hat{x}\textit{ø}$, where $x$ is a variable, $\text{ˆ}$ a special variable-binding device and $\textit{ø}$ a formula. Thus the nearest substitution instances of $Ft$ to $Rst$ is $\hat{x}(Rsx)t$, which one might read as: $t$ is such that $s$ is $R$ to it. Intuitively, the idea is that a syntactically non-ambiguous sentence cannot be an instance of both $Ft$ and $Rst$ because instances of the former have exactly two immediate constituents while instances of the latter have exactly three. (An anti-instrumentalist line on syntax therefore seems to be required.) Now $\hat{x}(Rsx)t\vdash E!t$ is a special case of EP, since $\hat{x}(Rsx)$ is an instance of $F$. In order to derive $Rst\vdash E!t$, it would therefore be enough to have $Rst\vdash\hat{x}(Rsx)t$. This is a special case of Abstraction: the inference from $\textit{ø}$ to $\hat{x}\textit{ø}'t$, where $\textit{ø}'$ results from replacing occurrences of $t$ in $\textit{ø}$ by $x$, subject to the usual constraints about variable-binding. Abstraction is not a universally valid rule: it fails for quotational contexts, for example (‘Kinnock’ contains seven letters, but Kinnock is not such that ‘he’ contains seven letters). More interestingly, its rejection has already been suggested for negative formulae, in order to block the inference from $\mathord{-}E!t$ (‘It is not the case that Casanova exists’) to $E!t$ (‘Casanova exists’), for $\hat{x}(\mathord{-}E!x)t$ (‘Casanova is such that he does not exist’) entails $E!t$ by EP, so $\mathord{-}E!t$ had better not entail $\hat{x}(\mathord{-}E!x)t$. An EPer might reject Abstraction for relational as well as negative formulae, and thus block unwanted arguments involving transitive verbs (presumably, $\mathord{-}Ft$ and $Rst$ would still entail $\hat{x}(\mathord{-}Fx)t$ and $\hat{x}(Rsx)t$ respectively in the presence of the extra existential premiss $E!t$, but that premiss is just the conclusion desired in the present case).15

It is thus possible consistently to accept EP while rejecting the following generalization of it, which will be called EP+:

$$Rt_1\ldots t_n\vdash E!t_i\qquad 1\leq i\leq n$$

($R$ is an $n$-adic predicate). On the other hand, it is possible consistently to accept EP+; there are other ways of blocking the unwanted arguments involving transitive verbs (for instance, an extension of the technique alluded to in connection with ‘Casanova is famous’). Now consider two logicians, one of whom accepts EP+ and therefore EP and the other of whom accepts EP but rejects EP+. Does their dispute turn on equivocation over the ‘existence’ predicate? It does not. For an earlier argument showed that any two predicates which obey EP are logically equivalent, so that one obeys EP+ if and only if the other does. To at least this extent the dispute is genuine: our two logicians cannot both be right.
