Higher-order metaphysics and small differences

Timothy Williamson

Analysis 83 (1) (2023): 213–224.

The Bounds of Possibility: Puzzles of Modal Variation By Cian Dorr and John Hawthorne with Juhani Yli-Vakkuri. Oxford University Press, 2021. xii + 436 pp.

Conspicuously missing some planks, a latter-day Ship of Theseus catches the eye when one picks up this Book of Three Authors. Teasingly, differentiated prepositions between their names on the title page hint at its complex history. The result is a formidable achievement, exemplifying contemporary metaphysics at its best.

Puzzles of variation arise from pre-reflectively plausible tolerance principles, on which an artefact survives any small variation in its constituents but no large variation in them: unfortunately, many small variations make a large variation. The variations may be across times or across possibilities, though the analogy between the temporal and modal dimensions is not assumed to go all the way to the puzzles’ solutions. In particular, what is future in the future is future, but Iteration – that what is possibly possible is possible – is much less obvious. According to Nathan Salmón (1989), puzzles of modal variation refute Iteration, and so undermine the structural analogy with time, for which the corresponding principle is uncontentious – though one might suspect that whatever measures are needed to resolve the temporal puzzles will resolve the modal puzzles too, independently of modal logic (Williamson 1990).

The puzzles are not just brain teasers. They cast serious doubt on whether object-oriented human cognition is suited to making coherent sense of reality, even of those bits of it which we ourselves construct. Anyone who suspects the puzzles of being mere sophisms can learn from this book (BOP) that they have no pain-free solution. More generally, BOP can be used as a high-level introduction, as well as a contribution, to an emerging kind of formal metaphysics (for more on which see soon Bacon forthcoming; Fritz and Jones forthcoming). I will explain BOP’s innovative approach.

Formal metaphysics is not a study of uninterpreted formal systems – that would not be metaphysics. It is closer in spirit to Plato’s Academy, with the injunction ‘Let no one ignorant of geometry enter here’ supposedly written over the entrance, except that ‘first-order logic’ has replaced ‘geometry’. Formal metaphysics uses interpreted formal systems to achieve systematicity. For instance, mereology has long contributed to formal metaphysics by using formal systems whose primitive symbols are interpreted in terms of parthood and overlap. Formal metaphysics is more systematic than the helpful habit of clarifying individual claims and arguments by formalizing them, because it works with whole theories, not just individual sentences.

BOP’s formal systems are of higher-order logic. The reader needs some knowledge of first-order logic before entering, but is taught its higher-order extensions once inside.

Syntactically, the formal language is typed. The lowest type is that of singular terms. Higher types are defined iteratively: any finite sequence of types is itself a type, the type of predicates with arguments of those types in that order. Concatenating such a predicate with such a sequence of terms yields a sentence, open or closed. The language has infinitely many variables and perhaps some constants of any given type. The type of n-place predicates of a first-order language is just the sequence of n occurrences of the type of individuals, for any natural number n. If n=0n = 0, it is the empty sequence <>\mathord{<}\,\mathord{>}, the type of sentences. Both negation and the modal operators □□ and ◊\lozenge are of type <<>>\mathord{<}\mathord{<}\,\mathord{>}\mathord{>}, since concatenating any of them with a sentence yields a sentence. Binary sentence operators such as conjunction, disjunction, and the material conditional and are of type <<>,<>>\mathord{<}\mathord{<}\,\mathord{>}, \mathord{<}\,\mathord{>}\mathord{>}.

One can form complex predicates of any given predicate type by lambda abstraction, prefixing the untyped expression λ\lambda and a sequence of variables of appropriate types to a sentence. For any type σ\sigma, the language has quantifiers ∀σ\forall_\sigma and ∃σ\exists_\sigma of type <<σ>>\mathord{<}\mathord{<}\sigma\mathord{>}\mathord{>}, which means that they are higher-type 1-place predicates applicable to 1-place predicates, which are in turn applicable to terms of type σ\sigma. In effect, this allows quantification into type σ\sigma position. From a sentence A(v)A(v) containing a variable vv of type σ\sigma, one forms the complex predicate λv(A(v))\lambda v(A(v)) of type <σ>\mathord{<}\sigma\mathord{>}, and then applies a quantifier to get the sentence ∀σλv(A(v))\forall_\sigma\lambda\, v(A(v)) (‘A(v)A(v) holds everywhere’) or ∃σλv(A(v))\exists_\sigma\lambda\, v(A(v)) (‘A(v)A(v) holds somewhere’). In particular, one can quantify into predicate position for any given type of predicate, including into sentence position. By contrast, in first-order logic, one can only quantify into singular term position.

The language also has an analogue of the identity predicate, =σ=_\sigma, for any type σ\sigma; one can formulate a corresponding version of Leibniz’s Law.

One might try to understand sentences of the higher-order language by paraphrasing them into English or another natural language. For instance, one could read ∀σ\forall_\sigma as ‘every individual’, ‘every proposition’, ‘every property’ or ‘every relation’, depending on whether σ\sigma is the type of singular terms, sentences, one-place predicates or many-place predicates, with further restrictions to reflect the complexities of the type-theoretic hierarchy. Such readings treat the higher-order language as if it were a first-order language with a single heterogeneous domain of entities, partitioned into individuals proper, propositions and properties and relations of various kinds. That in effect discards all the advantages of going higher-order. Consequently, the authors recommend learning the higher-order language ‘by the direct method’, through use, without trying to reduce it to anything more familiar. BOP provides ample exposure to the higher-order language in use. Rough paraphrases in English are offered too, as study aids, but the official formulations of metaphysical principles, the most logically perspicuous, are formulas of the higher-order language.

But what are the advantages of going higher-order? Consider the argument from ‘Al is silent and Ben isn’t’ to ‘Al is something that Ben isn’t’. It is a rough English paraphrase of the argument from Sa∧¬SbSa \wedge \neg Sb to ∃<e>λX(Xa∧¬Xb)\exists_{\mathord{<}\mathrm{e}\mathord{>}}\lambda\, X(Xa \wedge \neg Xb), which is easily derivable in BOP’s official minimal system of higher-order logic. For Sa∧¬SbSa \wedge \neg Sb is equivalent to λX(Xa∧¬Xb)S\lambda X(Xa \wedge \neg Xb)S by its principle of Extensional Beta-Conversion, from which the conclusion follows by its unrestricted principle of Existential Generalization. By contrast, the first-order strategy regiments the conclusion as something like ‘For some property xx, Al has xx and Ben does not have xx’, in symbols ∃x(Property⁡(x)∧Has⁡(a,x)∧¬Has⁡(b,x))\exists x\,(\operatorname{Property}(x) \wedge \operatorname{Has}(a,x) \wedge \neg\operatorname{Has}(b,x)). To derive it from the premise Sa∧¬SbSa \wedge \neg Sb, one needs the generalization ‘For some property xx, anything has xx if and only if it is silent’, in symbols ∃x(Property⁡(x)∧∀y(Has⁡(y,x)↔S(y)))\exists x\,(\operatorname{Property}(x) \wedge \forall y\,(\operatorname{Has}(y,x) \leftrightarrow \mathrm{S}(y))). But the underlying general property existence principle is inconsistent: substituting ¬Has⁡(y,y)\neg\operatorname{Has}(y,y) for S(y)\mathrm{S}(y) yields the analogue for properties of Russell’s Paradox for sets! By contrast, the higher-order form of argument is under no such threat of disaster, since the suspect predicate ‘Has’ corresponds to nothing in the typed language.

In response, defenders of the first-order strategy may claim that ‘is silent’ is logically harmless in a way that ‘does not have itself is not, so nominalizing the former is fine, even if nominalizing the latter is not. But ad hoc claims to be doing nothing wrong are not enough. We need a more principled way of answering questions of the form ‘Is there a property p such that for every x, x has p if and only A(x)?’ (where ‘p’ does not occur in A(x)). In other words, property theory requires explicit, general comprehension principles. A depressing feature of most philosophical discussions of properties and relations, the problem of universals and the like is that no such comprehension principle is on the horizon. Not even the possibility of one is discussed. Yet standard higher-order logics have explicit, unrestricted comprehension theorems for all types of predicate. BOP rightly emphasizes the contrast to motivate the higher-order framework.

An extreme but tempting comparison is with the rigorization of analysis in 19th-century mathematics, after the old methods started yielding apparent counterexamples to apparently proved theorems. Contemporary philosophers who persist in theorizing about properties and relations in the old-fashioned discursive way, without clear comprehension principles, remind me of 19th-century mathematicians who persisted with the old-fashioned methods of argument when those developed by Cauchy and Weierstrass were already available, as if what was good enough for 1800 was good enough for 1900.

Naturally, BOP emphasizes the usefulness of higher-order logic in philosophy, but its applications elsewhere should not be ignored. The semantics of natural languages provides examples. Even if there is no genuine quantification into predicate position in natural language, the semanticist seeks a general account of the semantics of actual and possible predicate constants in a natural language, not just one-off accounts for a few selected predicates. To formulate such an account in the meta-language, one must in effect quantify into predicate position. Moreover, to do its job properly, that generality should be unrestricted, for predicates – especially complex predicates – in natural language are not required to cut at the joints, or to be qualitative or non-disjunctive or non-negative in meaning, or anything like that. The required generality is plenitudinous.

The background logic of mathematics is also better understood as higher-order than as first-order. Consider the principle of mathematical induction. Formalized as a first-order schema, it allows one to deduce from the premises P(0)\mathrm{P}(0) and ∀n(P(n)→P(n+1))\forall n\,(\mathrm{P}(n) \to \mathrm{P}(n+1)) the conclusion ∀nP(n)\forall n\,\mathrm{P}(n), where the individual variable ‘nn’ ranges over natural numbers. The principle’s instances correspond to the predicates of natural numbers in the language, any of which can replace the schematic letter ‘P’. Since there are only countably many such predicates, the principle has only countably many instances, even though the set of natural numbers has uncountably many subsets, each of which is a candidate extension for a possible predicate of natural numbers. Now any true first-order theory of arithmetic has non-standard models, with non-standard ‘natural numbers’ beyond the standard ones. Pre-theoretically understood, the principle of mathematical induction fails in such models, since the premises are true and the conclusion false when ‘P’ is conceived as applying to all and only the standard natural numbers in the model, but there is no such predicate in the language. By contrast, on the standard semantics for higher-order logic, second-order arithmetic captures a unique structure, the right one: all its models are isomorphic to each other. It succeeds where first-order arithmetic fails because the second-order quantifiers are mandatorily unrestricted, on the standard semantics. Thus we need unrestricted second-order quantifiers to capture the principle mathematicians intend by mathematical induction.

Similar comments apply to standard set existence principles such as Separation and Replacement, represented by a predicate schema in a first-order setting and a second-order universal generalization in a second-order setting. Only the full second-order version captures the principle mathematicians intend.

As such examples from mathematics and natural language semantics show, generality with respect to predicate position is no mere plaything of metaphysicians. It is implicit in serious work outside philosophy too. Those applications also require a plenitudinous version of such generality. Before plugging a predicate into the principle of mathematical induction, it would be ridiculous for the mathematician to ask whether it cuts at the joints, or is qualitative or non-disjunctive or non-negative in meaning, or anything like that. Such considerations provide external support for BOP’s approach, with its unrestricted higher-order quantification. In general, by becoming more serious about the logical framework in which they work, metaphysicians get more serious about something with consequences for non-philosophers too, a potentially fruitful reality check on metaphysical theorizing.

Why is the higher-order framework relevant to puzzles of modal variation? To explain the reasons, I will use rough paraphrases in English, for familiarity, but one must appreciate that higher-order logic provides more perspicuous, if less familiar, formulations, without the confusion introduced by the imagined ‘has’ relation.

The puzzles are generated by tolerance principles, which can be simply formalized in general higher-order terms. The modal variation is tracked through properties of objects, which typically specify how they are constituted. Small variations correspond to ones between close properties. A modal tolerance principle says that for any properties F and G, if G is close to F, and something has F, it could have had G. G is ancestrally close to F just in case, for some finite sequence of properties starting with G and ending with F, each member of the sequence (except the last) is close to the next. Obviously, large variations can correspond to ones between ancestrally close properties. The corresponding modal hypertolerance principle says that for any properties F and G, if G is ancestrally close to F, and something has F, it could have had G. For a suitable closeness relation, the tolerance principle is very plausible, but the hypertolerance principle is very implausible. Unfortunately, the tolerance principle threatens to entail the hypertolerance principle, given plausible background assumptions: Iteration and the non-contingency of closeness and tolerance. Which assumption should be given up? The best framework for exploring the issues is higher-order logic.

Another advantage of the higher-order framework is that it facilitates the reduction of (possible) worlds to special propositions or properties of propositions. However, BOP makes little use of worlds, preferring to work directly with modal operators.

A more central use of the higher-order framework in BOP is to generalize over modal operators, understood as properties of propositions. The idea goes back to Kripke’s claim in Naming and Necessity that some truths are ‘necessary in the highest degree – whatever that means’ (1980: 99). A truth may be necessary in a low degree by being ‘locally’ necessary: it could not easily have been otherwise. Iteration fails when read locally. The question is whether it fails when read non-locally, for instance, in terms of metaphysical possibility, the dual of metaphysical necessity, which is often – but not always – equated with necessity in the highest degree. Presumably, when □□ is read as ‘it is necessary in the highest degree that’, the principle □p→□□p□ p \to □□ p holds, for otherwise □□□□ would express a higher degree of necessity than □□, contrary to hypothesis; then the principle ◊◊p→◊p\lozenge\lozenge p \to \lozenge p holds too, on the dual reading of ◊\lozenge. BOP explores a range of associated issues in depth. Necessity-like properties are treated as those obeying appropriate structural constraints. To show that such a collection of properties has a maximal member, one typically proves closure results, for instance, that any conjunction of structurally appropriate properties is itself structurally appropriate. Some strong necessity-like properties can be defined quite simply; for instance, the property of being identical with the proposition that everything is self-identical is necessity-like. BOP’s detailed logical results support a view on which there is a maximal necessity, which is metaphysical necessity, and which obeys Iteration. All this development is framed within higher-order logic.

The higher-order framework also plays a more general unifying role in BOP. It is introduced in the first few pages and remains in place thereafter.

Of course, higher-order modal logic comes in many different strengths, on many different dimensions. BOP’s basic system is a curious mixture of strength and weakness. It extends classical non-modal first-order logic with identity, and it provides unrestricted comprehension principles, but no analogue of the Axiom of Choice. It neither proves nor refutes the identity p=p∧pp = p \wedge p. The super-controversial necessitist thesis □∀x□∃y(x=y)□\forall x\,□\exists y\,(x = y) (necessarily everything is necessarily something) is a theorem, unlike the modal principles □p→p□ p \to p, □p→□□p□ p \to □□ p, and ◊p→□◊p\lozenge p \to □\lozenge p.

What standard was applied in selecting the axioms and rules of the basic system? Wisely, the authors did not require them to fit a preconceived image of logic as metaphysically neutral; no principle of logic is incapable of being questioned by a wrong-headed metaphysician. Likewise, they avoid claiming the obscure status of ‘analytic’ or ‘conceptual’ truth for its principles. The selection was responsive to other pressures.

One kind of consideration was dialectical: Iteration was omitted in order not to beg the question against Salmón’s rejection of it. By contrast, necessitism is much less directly involved in the puzzles. Such concerns are understandable. Still, they are not the best guide to theory-building. After all, Salmón and other opponents are not dialectically obliged to accept even the proposed basic system of higher-order logic (some of Salmon’s ideas mentioned in BOP are inimical to the type-theoretic approach). As for the baked-in necessitism, contingentists are briefly offered the option of treating it as just a manner of speaking, as they might do with other necessitist discourse, but they are unlikely to trust the deliverances of a basic logical framework that (from a contingentist perspective) goes so badly wrong right from the start.

A standard of convenience was also applied. Modifying many of BOP’s arguments to accommodate different varieties of contingentism ‘would require several different kinds of qualification, making them unpleasantly complicated’. Fair enough, though elsewhere BOP is not frightened of complexity.

Another reason for working with a weak basic system and introducing stronger principles as needed is to isolate the role of those additional principles. But one still needs to decide which principles to withhold from the basic system for later study. For example, the authors could have started with intuitionistic, rather than classical propositional logic, in order to study the role of double negation elimination in the arguments. They show no inclination to do so, presumably because they regard double negation elimination as not in serious doubt.

The upshot is that BOP’s basic logical system looks rather ad hoc. For example, it has the strong necessitist theorem, but not the Barcan formula ∀Y(◊∃xYx→∃x◊Yx)\forall Y\,(\lozenge\exists x\,Yx \to \exists x\,\lozenge Yx). If the Barcan formula fails, there could have been something that had a given property, even though there is nothing that could have had that property: in a sense, there could have been more than there is. But it is hard to motivate the necessitist principle in the face of obvious objections without a metaphysical picture on which ontological structure is beyond the reach of contingency, so there could not have been more or less than there is. Thus a higher-order logic of metaphysical modality with the necessitist theorem but not the Barcan formula looks under-motivated. Similarly, BOP’s basic system has a restricted form of the necessity of identity, but not the necessity of distinctness. A clearer picture includes both identity and distinctness in ontological structure, beyond the reach of contingency.

Recent work by Zachary Goodsell and Juhani Yli-Vakkuri (MS) also indicates that systems like BOP’s are too weak for mathematical purposes, because they lack an adequate comprehension principle for functions. Goodsell and Yli-Vakkuri’s own system LF of higher-order logic has such a principle, as a corollary of an axiom of choice. Although LF individuates functions extensionally, it is not extensional, for it implies that some materially equivalent propositions are distinct. LF is intensional; whenever a biconditional is provable, so is the corresponding propositional identity. They show LF to suffice for most of contemporary mathematics: a new version of logicism. My money has always been on mathematical power to beat metaphysical qualms, not least in higher-order modal logic (Williamson 2003; 2013; 2016). LF incorporates modality by identifying necessity with being identical to the weakest truth. They derive the strong modal logic S5, the necessity of identity and distinctness and the Barcan formula and its converse. Mathematical strength and modal strength go naturally together.

Fortunately, BOP does not insist that its basic system is optimal; BOP is more provisional in spirit. The reader has a sense of work in progress, even though BOP is far richer in logically polished theses and arguments than most works of metaphysics. The field is developing with almost frightening rapidity. One suspects that in a very few years, all three authors will have considerably advanced their ideas, perhaps not all in the same direction. Meanwhile, there is an exciting sense of new intellectual landscapes coming into view, quite unlike the tired rounding-up of the usual suspects one so often has to endure in philosophy.

BOP’s methodology still requires a pre-theoretic starting point. That role is played primarily by tolerance principles, with instances such as ‘This table could have been made from slightly different materials’. Where such claims come from is not much discussed. BOP’s attitude seems to be that ordinary speakers assent to them, so charity mandates an interpretation of their words on which tolerance principles come out true. The trouble is that ordinary speakers also assent to tolerance principles of slightly different forms, for instance, that this table will survive the removal of one molecule, whose repeated application can generate sorites paradoxes. BOP firmly rejects soritical tolerance principles as just false, and treats any philosophical reasoning based on the thought ‘The difference is too small to matter’ as thereby discredited. Thus the special tolerance principles it aims to vindicate had better not be based on similar bad thinking.

BOP divides the contribution of tolerance principles to the modal puzzles into two premises. Take the case of tables. The first premise, Tolerance, says that every table is tolerantly a table, in the sense that for any properties F and G, if G is close to F, any table with F could have been a table with G. The second premise, Non-Contingency, says that if Tolerance holds, it does so necessarily. As a first step, the two premises are combined to yield the lemma that Tolerance necessarily holds. The puzzle proceeds from there.

With Tolerance treated as pre-theoretically plausible, the focus is on motivating Non-Contingency. Attempted motivations dependent on bad soritical thinking are dismissed. A better, non-soritical motivation is proposed instead: the Security Argument. Roughly, it says that if Tolerance is contingent, our belief in it will often be true only by luck, and so fail to constitute knowledge. Thus, if we know the first premise, the second premise is true.

One of BOP’s main moves is an ingenious response to the Security Argument. It exploits a form of semantic plasticity once suggested by Eli Hirsch. On this picture, the word ‘table’ subtly shifts its meaning, in particular its associated standard of modal individuation, depending on the world in which it is used. In each relevant world w, every tablew\mathrm{table}_w is tolerantly a tablew\mathrm{table}_w, where ‘tablew\mathrm{table}_w’ refers to whatever the word ‘table’ refers to as used in w. Although the proposition that every table is tolerantly a table is contingent, we could not easily have believed it falsely, for in a world w where not every table is tolerantly a table, what we believe is instead the proposition, true in w, that every tablew\mathrm{table}_w is tolerantly a table in tablew\mathrm{table}_w. Thus we can still know that every table is tolerantly a table, just as we can know that we live in this world, even though we could easily have lived in some other world, and thought ‘We live in this world’ – truly. Necessitated Tolerance is paradoxical, but what gets us into trouble is a combination of common sense (Tolerance) and a subtle error in propositional attitude ascription under a modal operator (Non-Contingency); soritical thoughts play no role.

How realistic is such an account of our pre-theoretic thinking about the puzzles? The proposed motivation for Non-Contingency involves sophisticated epistemological reflection on the status of belief in Tolerance. Even the separation between Tolerance and Non-Contingency seems artificial. In pre-theoretically assessing Tolerance, I imagine a table, and contrast that scenario with a counterfactual alternative with slightly different composing materials. The initial scenario is not specifically imagined as actual. I do not first assent to Tolerance for the actual world, and then generalize to all possible worlds. It feels more like first assenting to Tolerance for a more or less arbitrary world, and then endorsing Necessitated Tolerance as a corollary, though the strict word ‘necessarily’ acts as a warning to be cautious. Perhaps I endorse Tolerance only for worlds similar to ours, but they suffice for reaching a paradoxical conclusion. I doubt that I am unusual in these respects.

Even if the factorization of Necessary Tolerance into Tolerance and Non-Contingency and the Security Argument for Non-Contingency are no part of our ordinary pre-reflective entry into the modal puzzle, they might still make an interesting philosophical challenge. But there is little point in trying to keep bad soritical thinking out of the motivation for Non-Contingency if it is already in the motivation for Tolerance.

Arguably, sorites-generating tolerance principles are best understood as simply the results of a labour-saving, fairly reliable heuristic by which the default is to ignore small differences (Williamson 2020: 63–67). If told that Mary was in London and then took a step, you may reasonably assume that she was still in London after that step, even though it is possible to walk out of London; the assumption is reasonable irrespective of the vagueness or precision of the name ‘London’. Likewise, normally one need not keep rechecking someone’s scalp to retain knowledge that they are not bald, even though they lose several hairs each day. Again, if you have borrowed a book, you need not keep asking yourself whether you still have that book every time you rub a few molecules off a page with your fingers. We apply this persistence heuristic just as much under imagined suppositions. You probably did so when you thought through those examples. To block the default, something drastic is needed, either a much larger variation or a warning that the relevant boundary is nearby. If I tell you that Mary lives right on the edge of London or that she has seven league boots, you will probably not conclude that she is still in London. If I tell you that John is not yet bald but is rapidly going bald, you may keep glancing at his scalp. If the book you borrowed is a priceless, crumbling, medieval manuscript, you may worry more about its survival. Our cognitive processing of counterfactual identity works similarly. ‘I wish this table had been made slightly longer’ is much less likely than ‘I wish this table had been made ten times longer’ to prompt the default-breaking thought ‘Would that still have been this table?’ The persistence heuristic explains such patterns, obviating the need to postulate more elaborate forms of proto-metaphysical thinking.

Heuristics are described negatively as ‘cheap and dirty’, positively as ‘fast and frugal’. We normally apply them without being aware of doing so, as perceptual illusions reveal. Human cognition depends on heuristics. They are efficient and reliable enough to be adaptive, but not perfectly reliable. Their limitations emerge in unusual circumstances. If you apply the persistence heuristic once at random, it will almost certainly be truth-preserving. Both sorites paradoxes and puzzles of modal variation work by chaining together many different applications of the heuristic into an argument that is manifestly not truth preserving.

If such an account is correct, Tolerance is motivated by the very heuristic that generates sorites paradoxes, even though Tolerance by itself cannot generate such paradoxes. Does that make the puzzles of modal variation problems of vagueness? The persistence heuristic itself has nothing specifically to do with vagueness; it is applied to terms irrespective of their place on the spectrum from vagueness to precision. The heuristic makes more philosophical trouble in cases of vagueness because awareness of a nearby boundary for a relevant term tends to block the persistence default as too risky, while we may lack such awareness where the relevant term is vague. BOP acknowledges that the key terms in the modal puzzles, such as ‘table’, are relevantly vague; the issue is whether their vagueness is relevant in the same way as in sorites paradoxes. On the heuristic account, the relevance is of broadly the same kind.

The heuristic account is far from making BOP’s detailed metaphysical discussions redundant. Although heuristics help explain various features of our thought and talk, identifying them is no substitute for the work of interpretation. After all, questions of reliability and truth preservation arise for heuristics because heuristics operate on cognitive attitudes with truth-evaluable contents, and the relevant terms refer. Just as BOP assumes, interpretation must be constrained by some principle of charity: perhaps one which, as far as possible, maximizes our true beliefs, or, as I prefer, our knowledge. Although heuristics are not always truth-preserving, they can still deliver knowledge – for instance, perceptual knowledge – where they are locally truth-preserving, or safe. Much can be learnt from BOP about the most charitable ways of interpreting our thought and talk about the persistence of things, given the puzzles of modal variation. But since part of what must be charitably interpreted is our modal thought and talk about how things would have been, not just about what we would have thought or said about things, the role of semantic plasticity may be smaller than the authors suggest.

The scope for charitable interpretation depends on how wide a range of candidates for reference reality affords. BOP argues for several principles of plenitude to fill out reality in natural ways and facilitate charitable interpretation; it has little sympathy for error theories.

There is far more metaphysics and higher-order logic to learn from BOP than this review can convey; I hope to have conveyed at least an impression of its intellectual style. BOP’s analyses of the puzzles in the rigorous framework of higher-order modal logic focuses a microscope on the underlying issues and distinctions. Much previously obscured structure comes into clear view. Although BOP does not pretend to reach definitive conclusions, it provides the means to build on its achievements, perhaps to supersede them. That is what one expects of good science.
