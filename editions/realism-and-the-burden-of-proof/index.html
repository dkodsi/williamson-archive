Realism and the burden of proof

Timothy Williamson

Irish Philosophical Journal 3 (1) (1986): 42–57.

Realism, as I shall use the term, is the principle that a language can express true propositions which its speakers cannot, even in principle, verify as true. Anti-realism is the negation of this principle. In brief, anti-realism asserts, and realism denies, that truth entails verifiability. Hilary Putnam has recently advanced arguments which, if sound, would refute realism. This paper concludes that they are not sound (parts III and IV). More generally, it examines some rather delicate issues about the dialectical logic of the debate between realism and anti-realism (part II). Still more generally, it concerns the nature of philosophical argument (part I).

I

Arguments against a theory T are of two kinds.1 Roughly, the weak kind aim to show that T need not be true, the strong kind that T cannot be true. On one, we are not obliged to believe T; on the other, we are obliged not to believe T. Typically, the former claim that some T’, incompatible with T, is possible, the latter that T is not possible.2 The former tend to attack sufficient conditions for T, the latter, necessary conditions.

However, these ways of drawing the distinction can mislead. For they suggest a continuum of arguments, from those assigning T a probability of less than one to those assigning it a probability of zero (as it were). Such gradations, though certainly possible, conceal a deeper dichotomy. For epistemic probability – in the loosest sense – must be assessed from a point of view. Take, say, an argument against vulgar phenomenalism, showing it to have consequences repugnant to common sense (Russell’s unsupported tablecloth) yet fully consonant with that theory. From the common sense point of view, the theory becomes much less probable: from the theory’s own point of view, it remains as probable as before. So arguments against T divide over their intended audience, some aimed at those uncommitted to T, some at those committed to it. Both say ‘No’: the former to the question ‘Should I adopt T?’, the latter to the question ‘Should I retain T?’. However, advertence to intention makes the distinction extrinsic: different philosophers could intend the same argument for different audiences. The crucial distinction concerns different kinds of dialectical validity and is intrinsic: some arguments use premises plausible to a ‘neutral’ party but not mandatory on T, others use only premises mandatory on T (I prefer ‘mandatory on T’ to ‘entailed by T’, to include propositions not entailed by T but which T tells us to adopt in certain conditions, described in terms of T, which do in fact occur). The first discourage us from entering T, but only the second encourage us to leave it (whether ‘encourage’ becomes ‘force’ is another matter).

Now if an argument of the first sort is valid, the so-called neutral party is miscalled. For the argument makes essential use of premises not mandatory on T but plausible to that party. Since its conclusion contradicts T, given T (and so any premises that are mandatory on T) those supposedly plausible premises are false. Thus the argument has simply made explicit an already implicit disagreement between T and the ‘neutral’ view. That is still a bad result for T if we were already more strongly inclined to the ‘neutral’ view than to T, before we recognized their conflict, for we now have reason to reject the latter on the basis of the former rather than vice versa. Otherwise, we merely oppose T from the standpoint of another theory, without reason to start there rather than at T. Since this antecedent bias is the condition for such arguments to be effective, I shall speak of the ‘ordinary’ rather than the ‘neutral’ view, opposing it to the theoretical view, that of T.

Here too a continuum underlies the dichotomy. For neither the pure ordinary man nor the pure theoretician inhabits mundane reality: who would listen to an argument against a theory they had no inclination to accept or to reject? What fanatic cannot be caught off guard by pre-theoretical instinct? In practice, argument is often fruitful because ad hominem, exploiting the embryo of theory in the common man, the vestiges of common sense in the theoretician. Sometimes even agreement is reached. However, amongst those primarily responsive to the dictates of theory, the opposite phenomenon is more common: polarization. Each side, to preserve its ruling idea in consistency, expels the traces of its antithesis that threatened it with contradiction. So this dichotomy of points of view is not arbitrarily imposed on a continuum, but represents its inherent tendency to tear and shrivel towards opposite poles. In contrast, the previous continuum – of degrees of conclusiveness – tends to concentrate on its centre: most philosophical arguments end up classified as of some but not much force.

So opponents of T must decide whether to appeal to proponents of T or to those of some other theory they hope to show incompatible with T. Common sense might be such another theory, though I do not myself believe that common sense, or anything else, is both pretheoretically privileged and theoretically potent. When common sense purports to state an obvious fact inconsistent with some philosophical theory (‘this is a table’), that theory, if of any merit, can redescribe the ‘fact’ as consistent with itself, leaving common sense, except in so far as it is ‘the metaphysics of the stone age’, unable to choose between the descriptions. However, I need not invoke this suggestion, for whether the anti-realist arguments I shall discuss appeal only to pre-given common sense will emerge clearly enough.

Certainly the only quite satisfying argument against a theory is one aimed at the theoretician, valid on the theory’s own terms: a reductio ad absurdum. Anything less is unsatisfying both logically – because dependent on extra premises – and humanly – because our opponent rejects it and consensus is not achieved. Perhaps Hegel more than any other great philosopher took this point to heart, and a programme that the introduction to the Phenomenology suggests, to watch at least one of each pair of serious, conflicting theories fail on its own terms, must be completed if philosophy is to achieve perfect rationality. Few would suppose Hegel to have executed this task, many would suspect it of being impossible: but in so far as it is, philosophy must learn to live with an approximation, more or less rough, to perfect rationality. If two theories, neither in a privileged relation to common sense, are both consistent on their own terms, but inconsistent with each other, how do we choose between them?3

Moreover, this hard necessary condition for the ideal may not be sufficient. For even had one seen one of each conflicting pair of theories fail on its own terms, how could one know that one had? One would need a census of all theories, to check them off: what could provide that but a theory or part of one, and how would that be established? So this method seems to yield only negative results. A theory can be refuted, but not established, on its own terms. One might use the slogan, ‘If you think you’ve got problems, you have got problems’: for if you think you have problems, if you had no problems that false belief would itself be a problem. The corresponding slogan, ‘If you think you’ve no problems, you have no problems’, is mere whistling in the dark: if you think you have no problems, if you had problems that false belief would itself be a problem. Perhaps Popper’s asymmetry between verification and falsification has some truth on this informal understanding.4

Hegel’s answer is that a theory’s failure on its own terms is not a merely negative result, because it has been seen to fail in a specific way.5 This seems inadequate, for a specific contradiction, though a positive fact, is not a positive theory. A diagnosis of why the old theory had failed in the way it did might yield a new theory – but the diagnosis itself could only be made on the basis of theory, and so would not yield the presuppositionless theory for which we long. Greater attention to the role of developing self-consciousness in the dialectic might facilitate the links between the stages, if each is the turning of awareness on its predecessor, but what could rule out the possibility of a true theory lying altogether outside the progression, leaving the dialectic to stumble from error to error, ever far from the truth?

II

So far I have spoken of philosophical argument in general, and in pessimistic tone. A theory consistent on its own terms may always be contradicted by another theory, consistent on its own terms. Might not realism and anti-realism be two such theories? However, the specific content of anti-realism complicates matters. It says that truth must be verifiable. So, by its own lights, it must if true be verifiable. So, absolutely, it must if true be verifiable. What might seem stalemate, neither realism nor anti-realism able to refute the other, is defeat for anti-realism: in failing to falsify realism it fails to verify itself, and so falsifies itself.6 For of what interest is an anti-realism that makes no claim to truth? If a stipulation rather than a proposition, it can simply be rejected.

Can so neat an argument be valid? Sometimes such arguments are accused of neglecting the vital distinction between object and metalanguage: a principle stated in the metalanguage about sentences of the object language has (it is said) been illicitly applied to a sentence of the metalanguage. This criticism is perverse. Anti-realism is not an empirical doctrine about what happens to be expressible in some particular language – which might contain only decidable sentences – but a philosophical one about what is expressible in any possible language. Indeed, the only grounds for asserting anti-realism for the sentences of a powerful, prima facie realist natural language such as English are general philosophical ones, applicable to any possible language. So on the only plausible or philosophical version of anti-realism, a theorem in the metametalanguage will say that all sentences of the metalanguage are, if true, verifiable; since a theorem in the metalanguage says that all sentences of the object language are, if true, verifiable, anti-realism comes to the conclusion that it is verifiable that all sentences of the object language are, if true, verifiable. If realism has survived anti-realist attacks more or less unscathed, it will consistently deny that all sentences of a particular object language such as English are verifiable. Since anti-realism cannot, by hypothesis, refute this denial, it cannot verify that all sentences of the object language are, if true, verifiable. Hence anti-realism is falsified: it cannot escape up a hierarchy of metalanguages.

Could anti-realism have refuted realism on its own understanding of ‘refute’ but not on the realist understanding? Then, on its own terms, anti-realism would be verified. Philosophers would make short work of their opponents if they were allowed to tailor the concept of refutation to their own needs. But one does not win a war by redefining ‘victory’.7 To show that realist premises led through anti-realist logic to contradiction would be trivial; anti-realist premises certainly lead through realist logic to contradiction.8 If a grey area remains, assume that nothing that anyone could reasonably call a self-refutation of realism exists, for the interesting question is: what then?

Could anti-realists continue to assert the existence of a self-refutation of realism, while always admitting that it had not yet been found? On most anti-realist logics they could not: for, constructively speaking, one may assert that an F exists only if one has an example, or at least a method guaranteed to produce one within a finite time. But here a self-refutation of realism is not even in the offing. Such optimistic anti-realism refutes itself – unless so optimistic in estimating its prospects of refuting realism as to incur the strictures of the previous paragraph, by redefining ‘guarantee’. Let S\mathrm{S} be a situation where no method of producing a self-refutation of realism is known. Granted that anti-realism is not warrantedly assertible in S\mathrm{S}, it may not have refuted itself in S\mathrm{S}. It has done so only if the impossibility of a self-refutation of realism is warrantedly assertible in S\mathrm{S}: but it may not be. Clearly, the mere present absence of a self-refutation of realism yields no general method for refuting any future putative self-refutation of realism.9 Those with anti-realist instincts should be agnostic in S\mathrm{S}. I claim that the actual situation is such an S\mathrm{S}.

However, if philosophical investigation continues to fail to find a self-refutation of realism for long enough, any reasonable anti-realism must admit that the inductive evidence that realism does not refute itself is increasingly good, hence that the inductive evidence against anti-realism is increasingly good. Of course, the inductive evidence against a theory cannot be equated, even quantitatively, with the inductive evidence against one of its consequences: but as the latter mounts, so in the long run must the former. Thus anti-realism would refute itself by degrees.

Could anti-realism challenge this use of induction? It cannot adopt Popper’s anti-inductivism, for on Popper’s view a true empirical generalization is in no sense verifiable. Could it say that an hypothesis whose only evidence is inductive must be false? So extreme a view could make little sense of the use of empirical statements in natural languages. Could it allow that some hypotheses are projectible, but that ‘any purported reductio ad absurdum of realism is fallacious’, like ‘any emerald is grue’, is not one of them? As special pleading, this could not be taken seriously, but it might be based on a general principle: that inductive evidence never has any weight for philosophical hypotheses, say. But that is implausible: most philosophers, for instance, would take the sheer failure to find a convincing translation of material object language into sense datum language as evidence that none exists, over and above any general argument against phenomenalism. Thus if realism is internally coherent, certain forms of anti-realism could always consistently deny the existence of any evidence that this was so, but probably at the cost of viewing evidence in a way so extreme or ad hoc as to be intellectually disreputable. In short, anti-realists would have to be cranks: the self-refutation of realism their Godot. Given that no proof that realism is internally coherent is likely to be valid on any anti-realist terms, the realist should hope for an inductive self-refutation of anti-realism.

Hilary Putnam has endorsed a self-refutation argument against Logical Positivism.10 However, he sees it as threatening only an anti-realism that would have all verification criterial11 – that is, certifiable by institutionalized norms. He seems to assume that it leaves open a form of anti-realism on which truth must be verifiable, but in a looser, less formal way. Though one cannot anticipate the conversation of mankind, this leaves anti-realism with a heavy task. For if the realist cannot be driven into contradiction, or even uncontroversially bizarre conclusions, he has not been refuted even in this looser, less formal way – nor, therefore, has anti-realism been verified in that way. Could Putnam regard the consistent realist as the philosophical equivalent of the consistent Nazi,12 with a conceptual scheme that we can see to be irrational on the basis of the conceptual scheme we have adopted, even though it is so ‘warped’ that it cannot be brought to recognize its own irrationality? At present, such a ‘we’ cannot be defined without begging the question: realists are not yet pariahs. I doubt if Putnam even hopes to inspire his reader to drive them into the wilderness. Such a line would become plausible only if realism had to reject evidently valid13 arguments against itself. Putnam’s arguments for the incoherence of realism have much to do.

Anti-realism fails unless it can find internal incoherence in realism. However, the failure of anti-realism would not be the success of realism. For it leaves open extreme, perhaps Wittgensteinian, views on which all abstract philosophical theses are meaningless. Such a view might itself be self-refuting if it said that all abstract philosophical theses are meaningless, since that too is an abstract philosophical thesis. However, it could avoid self-refutation by calling each abstract philosophical thesis meaningless individually, while refusing to assert (or deny) it collectively, or it could confine the charge of meaninglessness to certain philosophical theses, including realism and anti-realism. But realism survives such diversity of possible opinion, for it can regard itself as an unverifiable truth (though perhaps supported by good evidence).

III

Any satisfying attack on realism – and therefore, as Section II claimed, any satisfying defence of anti-realism – would show its incoherence on its own terms. Is this how Putnam argues?

The target of ‘Realism and Reason’14 is ‘metaphysical realism’, a view on which the terms of the reference relation, words and things, are radically distinct. Putnam takes it to entail that a false theory could be verified.15

Is, then, metaphysical realism (verifiability does not entail truth) the converse of ordinary realism (truth does not entail verifiability)?16 No: the word ‘verification’ is being used differently. In our sense, verification, where it occurs, is conclusive. In Putnam’s, verification is the best operational status a theory can hope for, which may not be conclusive. If P\mathrm{P} is verifiable but not true in Putnam’s sense, −P\mathord{-}\mathrm{P} is true but not verifiable in ours, for if −P\mathord{-}\mathrm{P} were verifiable in our sense, P\mathrm{P} would not have the best operational status it could hope for, hence would not be verifiable in Putnam’s sense. Thus metaphysical realism implies ordinary realism. However, ordinary realism does not imply metaphysical realism. For instance, to say that arithmetic has unprovable truths is not to say that it has disprovable truths, to say that we cannot have conclusive evidence for a theory is not to say that we can have good evidence against it. So even had Putnam refuted metaphysical realism, he would not thereby have refuted ordinary realism. But the points made below will not turn on which realism is at issue.

Putnam argues that, given a supposedly false theory verifiable in his sense, one can always define a new ‘reference’ relation making the theory true, such that the realist cannot say in virtue of what his reference relation, not Putnam’s, is correct. Yet on Putnam’s relation, ‘cat’ might refer to a set of entities with which, the metaphysical realist wants to say, we have no causal connection. Aware of this, Putnam remarks:

Notice that a ‘causal’ theory of reference is not (would not be) of any help here: for how ‘causes’ can uniquely refer is as much of a puzzle as how ‘cat’ can, on the metaphysical realist picture.17

This is very strange. The metaphysical realist picture is accused of internal incoherence because it cannot explain how words refer uniquely; it offers an explanation, in causal terms, of how they do, which is failed for assuming that the words it uses refer uniquely. Now if this is the demand that the realist use only words whose unique reference he has already explained, it is blatantly absurd. For when he begins to speak, by hypothesis he cannot already have explained how his first words refer uniquely: he would have to remain forever silent.18 The ‘theory’ that language is meaningful at all could be dismissed on the same grounds, any explanation it gave of how language means being accused of begging the question, by assuming that the language of the explanation was meaningful.

Interpret Putnam more charitably, then, as accusing the realist of using the assumption that ‘cause’ refers uniquely to explain how ‘cat’ refers uniquely, whereas what must be explained is how any word can refer uniquely. But does the realist explanation assume that ‘cause’ refers uniquely, except in the sense just discussed? For a causal theory of reference uses ‘cause’, but need not mention it (and might be true even of a language with no word for ‘cause’19). The realist explanation need include no premise that ‘cause’ refers uniquely. Let R\mathrm{R} be some causal relation. Consider the argument:

For any x\mathrm{x} and y\mathrm{y}, x\mathrm{x} refers to y\mathrm{y} if and only if x\mathrm{x} bears the relation R\mathrm{R} to y\mathrm{y}.

‘Cat’ bears the relation R\mathrm{R} uniquely to the set (or whatever) of cats.

Therefore:
‘Cat’ refers uniquely to the set (…) of cats.

For the realist, this is a perfectly good, if skeletal, explanation of how ‘cat’ refers uniquely, which does not refer to the word ‘cause’. Putnam might object20 that it is useless because the reference of ‘R\mathrm{R}’, and therefore of ‘the set of cats’, is indeterminate too. But to do so would be profoundly to misconceive the dialectical situation. Realism is accused of failing on its own terms, of making explanatory demands on itself that it cannot meet. Hence the pertinent question is whether the explanation is good on realist terms. To object that it fails on anti-realist terms because, on them, ‘R’ is indeterminate, is quite beside the point. It merely shows that the realist cannot convince the anti-realist that he can refer uniquely. But he was not trying to convince the anti-realist, he was trying to explain to himself how he could do something that he already assumed he could do: in that, he succeeded. He avoids internal incoherence, and in doing so returns that threat to anti-realism.

Putnam’s argument about reference parallels scepticism about perception. Imagine an upholder of a causal theory of knowledge challenged on his knowledge of the external world: ‘How do you know that a table is before you?’. He may, indeed must, quite legitimately assume that a table is before him in explaining the causal production of his belief that a table is before him and therefore how he knows that a table is before him. To accuse one who assumes that P\mathrm{P} in explaining how they know that P\mathrm{P} of begging the question is sheer error (so in further explaining how one knows that one knows, one may assume that one knows, and so on). Such an explanation will not convince a sceptic who doubts that P\mathrm{P}, that P\mathrm{P} is known, but it does precisely what was asked of it: answer the question ‘how do you know that P\mathrm{P}?’ The sceptic, who has failed to show internal incoherence in his opponent’s position, accuses him of begging the question by not having shown internal incoherence in the sceptic’s position. The sophistry is transparent.

Now the metaphysical realist, in using ‘cause’, presupposes its unique reference. So that too is a fact that he would want to explain; though he will not be surprised if the realization of the reference relation turns out to be rather different for ‘cat’ and for ‘cause’.21 Suppose that he can explain, on a causal theory, thus using ‘cause’, how ‘cause’ refers uniquely: this is quite legitimate on realist terms, and they are what count. The realist offers a virtuous regress: each explanation can be followed – not preceded – by another, explaining how the terms used in the first refer uniquely. Indeed, the regress is likely to be infinite only for the rather trivial reason that the reference of the object language word for ‘reference’ can be discussed only in a metalanguage: radically new concepts will hardly need to be introduced after the first few stages.

Arguments so schematic22 point to nothing that the realist needs to and cannot on his own terms explain. Realism remains internally coherent.

So far I have accepted without demur Putnam’s implicit assumption that realism is obliged on its own terms to explain how words can refer uniquely. This seems to rest on a principle of sufficient reason: all semantic phenomena are so and not otherwise in virtue of some reason. Might a realist deny this? Realism satisfying such a principle is certainly more attractive than realism that does not, but if – what I do not believe – realism turned out unable to satisfy the principle, the alternative to a realism flouting it would be anti-realism: need the latter be more attractive? At present, though, the demand that realism explain its own claims on its own terms is reasonable.

Hartry Field encapsulates a similar point:

The reason why accounts of truth and primitive reference are needed is not to tack our conceptual scheme on to reality from the outside; the reason, rather, is that without such accounts our conceptual scheme breaks down from the inside.23

However, I have associated the need for such accounts with general demands on explanation rather than with the physicalism Field advocates24 (which is no part of my conceptual scheme). Whether such accounts are finally given in causal terms and of truth and primitive reference or in other terms and of other concepts does not matter here (for definiteness, I will continue to speak of causal theories, but without assuming anything specific to causality). I am not concerned to show in detail how the realist is to explain his claims, only that the very general considerations Putnam adduces do not show that he cannot.25

To take another case: the realist wants to show how speakers can give truth-conditions such that the law of excluded middle holds even for undecidable sentences. He may quite legitimately use the law of excluded middle in explaining why it holds for our language (roughly: the explanation of why ‘sv−s\mathrm{s} \mathbin{\mathrm{v}} \mathord{-}\mathrm{s}’ is true assumes that Pv−P\mathrm{P} \mathbin{\mathrm{v}} \mathord{-}\mathrm{P}); and a corresponding explanation of why the law holds in the metalanguage can be given in the metametalanguage. Clearly this does not prove the law: but it does undercut any attempted disproof. Nor is it trivial: the law will not hold in the object language simply because it holds in the metalanguage: the metalogic used for intuitionist logic is often classical.26

IV

Putnam has a further explanatory task for realism.27 Suppose that reference is a certain causal relation R\mathrm{R}: what makes that so? Now had the realist identified reference with a general causal relation S\mathrm{S} such that, as a matter of scientific fact, R\mathrm{R} plays the causal role of S\mathrm{S} in this world, he could, non-trivially, have explained why x\mathrm{x} refers to y\mathrm{y} if and only if Rxy\mathrm{Rxy}. But Putnam can assume R\mathrm{R} to be what fundamentally corresponds to reference (in that case, S\mathrm{S}). Thus the realist is saying that necessarily x\mathrm{x} refers to y\mathrm{y} if and only if Rxy\mathrm{Rxy}, or:

(A0)(\mathrm{A}_0)

Reference is R\mathrm{R}.

Putnam will then ask: what makes (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0) so? Here explanation does seem to come to an end. Putnam says that (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0) would be ‘a kind of primitive, surd, metaphysical truth’.28 He contrasts it with the Kripkean example:

(B0)(\mathrm{B}_0)

Water is H2O\mathrm{H_2O}.29

This latter ‘metaphysical necessity’ ‘is explained by mundane chemistry and mundane facts about speakers’ intentions to refer’,30 whereas these intentions would signify only in virtue of, and so could not explain, (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0).

This too is very strange. Water would have been H2O\mathrm{H_2O} regardless of what intentions speakers had had in respect of ‘water’, or any other word (except on the crudest anti-realism of all, that cannot even distinguish between use and mention). These intentions are irrelevant to explaining (B0)(\mathrm{B}_0). Perhaps Putnam was thinking of:

(B1)(\mathrm{B}_1)

‘Water’ refers to H2O\mathrm{H_2O}.

Speakers’ intentions might be relevant to explaining (B1)(\mathrm{B}_1), but it corresponds to:

(A1)(\mathrm{A}_1)

‘Reference’ refers to R\mathrm{R}.

not to (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0); (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0) corresponds to (B0)(\mathrm{B}_0), not to (B1)(\mathrm{B}_1).

Now (B0)(\mathrm{B}_0) has an odd status, for while ‘mundane facts about speakers’ intentions’ are unnecessary to its explanation, ‘mundane chemistry’ is insufficient. For neither ‘water’ nor any roughly intertranslatable term is even part of the technical vocabulary of chemical theory: hence such theory alone cannot possibly explain (B0)(\mathrm{B}_0) (anyone who thinks that (B0)(\mathrm{B}_0) is a tautology or triviality can think the same of (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0): neither would call for explanation). For, familiarly, to explain something under one description is not to explain it under another: (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0) and (B0)(\mathrm{B}_0) are explicable only in a theory describing the identity as they do. Similarly, the technical vocabulary of the causal theory in which R\mathrm{R} appears will hardly include ‘refer’. This is, of course, a case of the problem of ‘bridge laws’ and their status in science: a theory cast in purely non-observational terms (as high-level theories are) cannot explain their link with observation as such.31 This is no mere technical hitch in the philosophy of science, but an eruption of profound difficulties about subjectivity and objectivity. For a theory explaining (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0) or (B0)(\mathrm{B}_0) would have satisfyingly to relate (relatively) objective reality (R\mathrm{R}, H2O\mathrm{H_2O}) and its subjective appearance (as reference, water) to us: but to know what such a theory could be like would be almost to have solved the mind-body problem. This is no place for such an attempt. Fortunately, the present point is just the fallaciousness of Putnam’s attempted disanalogy between (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0) and (B0)(\mathrm{B}_0). One may not know what to say about either, but it has not been shown that what must be said about one cannot be said about the other. If (B0)(\mathrm{B}_0) is acceptable metaphysics, why should not (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0) be?

The apparent inexplicability of (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0) is no objection to realism. Putnam could instead ask for an explanation of (A1)(\mathrm{A}_1). Now (B1)(\mathrm{B}_1) is presumably to be explained by (B0)(\mathrm{B}_0) and:

(B2)(\mathrm{B}_2)

‘Water’ refers to water.

Similarly, (A1)(\mathrm{A}_1) is presumably to be explained by (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0) and:

(A2)(\mathrm{A}_2)

‘Reference’ refers to reference

(this blurring of the distinction between object and metalanguage is harmless). (A2)(\mathrm{A}_2) in turn should be explained. Is this the place for Putnam’s objection, that (A2)(\mathrm{A}_2) could only be explained in terms of speakers’ intentions, which themselves signify only in virtue of (A2)(\mathrm{A}_2), making any explanation of it impossible? If the realist were to make reference require a formulated intention, he might be vulnerable to that charge: but he would also have begun an obviously vicious regress, since the formulation could refer to the words only by another formulated intention, and so on. Instead, the realist should take R\mathrm{R} as giving the causal basis on which any relevant intentions supervene,32 so that (A2)(\mathrm{A}_2) would hold simply in virtue of the word ‘reference’ having R\mathrm{R} to the reference relation: and Section III has already defended explanations like that.33

Even without the difficulty of relating different levels of appearance, the idea of explaining (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0) or (B0)(\mathrm{B}_0) is obscure. Putnam could seem to give a straightforward explanation of (B0)(\mathrm{B}_0) only by misrepresenting it as contingent while calling it necessary. Given that (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0) and (B0)(\mathrm{B}_0) are not contingent – I have no wish to challenge the necessity of true identity statements34 – they are not contingent on anything: so what could explain them? Only necessary truths can explain a necessary truth, lest its generality be missed. To ‘explain’ why either an explosion occurs at spatio-temporal location x\mathrm{x} or one does not by explaining why an explosion occurs at x\mathrm{x} and then applying the ‘or’ introduction rule, is at best obtuse. For the occurrence of an explosion is contingent, so at least some of the premises used to explain it must have been contingent, since what necessary premises entail is itself necessary (nor has probabilistic explanation from necessary premises any chance here): but the disjunction would have been true even had those premises been false. (Note that it concerns a decidable matter, so that anti-realist worries about the law of excluded middle do not apply here.) Whether or not such a deduction counts as an explanation, it is certainly not a good one. In practice, if asked why either an explosion occurs at x\mathrm{x} or one does not, one might say something like: it couldn’t have been otherwise. This could be construed either as a rejection of the question, or as an explanation of P\mathrm{P} by deduction from □P□\mathrm{P}; either course would be applicable to (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0) and (B0)(\mathrm{B}_0), still yielding no asymmetry between them. One who asks why reference is R\mathrm{R} may not feel much enlightenment on being told that reference is necessarily R\mathrm{R}: but neither may one who asks why water is H2O\mathrm{H_2O} on being told that water is necessarily H2O\mathrm{H_2O}. Again, some proofs in mathematics seem to explain why their (necessary) conclusions are true: but is the resemblance to standard forms of explanation deep or superficial? If, for instance, to explain something is to give the causes or intentions behind it, explanation in mathematics is but pretence. Anyway, (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0) and (B0)(\mathrm{B}_0) cannot hope for mathematical lucidity. The problems set for realism seem rather to be problems for our concept of explanation (which was not itself used in defining realism).

However, (B0)(\mathrm{B}_0), though necessary, is closely related to a contingent truth:

(B3)(\mathrm{B}_3)

H2O\mathrm{H_2O} is what appears to us in the way we actually associate with water.

Another liquid, with the same phenomenal qualities as water, could have predominated instead in our environment, as we are often reminded. This contingent truth presumably has a straightforward explanation: someone might claim that this is what defuses (B0)(\mathrm{B}_0). The parallel for (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0) is:

(A3)(\mathrm{A}_3)

R\mathrm{R} is what appears to us in the way we actually associate with reference.

Could another relation, with the same ‘phenomenal’ qualities as reference, have predominated instead in our environment? That sounds incoherent: assume that it is, for otherwise the symmetry between (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0) and (B0)(\mathrm{B}_0), which I have been defending for other respects, remains here too.

The reconstructed argument parallels Kripke’s celebrated attack on mind-brain identity:35 only pain can appear as pain, just as only reference can appear as reference. The argument against (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0) would be, roughly, that whereas the real contingency of (B3)(\mathrm{B}_3) explains the apparent contingency of (B0)(\mathrm{B}_0), (A3)(\mathrm{A}_3) is not really contingent, so that no corresponding explanation of the apparent contingency of (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0) can be given: hence (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0) must be assumed at most contingently true; as an identity it is, if true, necessarily true; hence it is false. Now whatever one thinks of Kripke’s original argument, this imitation uses the concept of appearance uncritically. For what does the apparent contingency of (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0) come to? To imagine (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0) false one can only imagine that philosophical discussion finds irremediable divergences between reference and R\mathrm{R}. In the same sense one can imagine that somebody will find a proof of 0=10 = 1 in Peano arithmetic. In neither case does one know how to fill in the picture: being able to imagine in this sense that P\mathrm{P} is no evidence that P\mathrm{P} is really possible. One could imagine speakers saying ‘a\mathrm{a} refers to b\mathrm{b}’ where a\mathrm{a} obviously was not R\mathrm{R} to b\mathrm{b}, and ‘a\mathrm{a} does not refer to b\mathrm{b}’ where a\mathrm{a} obviously was R\mathrm{R} to b\mathrm{b}: but one cannot then assume that they would mean by ‘reference’ what we mean by it; reference is not a phenomenological notion like pain.

The argument has become so general that it threatens any philosophical analysis whatsoever. For instance, Putnam wants to say something like:

(C)

Truth is what rational discussion would ultimately converge on.

One can as easily imagine rational discussion turning against (C) as against (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0): Putnam would rightly deny that this sense of ‘imagine’ is strong enough for the contingency of (C) to follow, so he cannot argue in this way against (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0).36 As for some strong Humean sense of ‘imagine’ in which the imaginable is somehow fully imaginable and therefore possible: to imagine an argument would already be to possess it, to imagine (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0) false would anticipate the future of philosophy. In Humphrey Lyttleton’s words: ‘If I knew where Jazz was going I’d be there already’.

The Kripke argument cannot be paralleled. Could one still argue that realism must and cannot explain (A3)(\mathrm{A}_3)? Now the necessity of (A3)(\mathrm{A}_3) adds to (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0) only that nothing but reference could have played the role which reference plays in our lives. In itself, this is quite unpuzzling, for that is just the kind of concept that reference is: concerned with the role, not what plays it, with form, not with matter. Without attempting more precision, one can easily explain the difference: ‘water’ is a term of embryonic chemistry, ‘reference’ of embryonic philosophy. The only question is whether this generality of reference is compatible with the specifically causal nature of the relation R\mathrm{R}. But if causality is understood as realizable by different specific relations in different universes, the difficulty disappears. Whether or not such an understanding is correct for the everyday English usage of ‘cause’, it is what the causal theory needs. So the long search for a disanalogy between (A0)(\mathrm{A}_0) and (B0)(\mathrm{B}_0) has found that ‘reference’ is a more abstract word that ‘water’. In philosophy, as in mathematics, the price of following the argument where it leads is sometimes to find oneself back where one started.

Neither a disproof of realism nor a disproof of the possibility of such a disproof has emerged. How many philosophers since – and before – Berkeley must have felt that at last they had the fallacy of realism ‘formulated, sprawling on a pin’? ‘Metaphysics is the finding of bad reasons for what we believe upon instinct, but to find these reasons is no less an instinct.’ Must the purging of fallacy lie deeper in us than its commission?
