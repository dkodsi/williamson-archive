Peacocke’s theory of modality

Timothy Williamson

Philosophy and Phenomenological Research 64 (3) (2002): 649–654.

Chapter 4 of Being Known makes a bold and sophisticated attempt to integrate the metaphysics of modality with its epistemology. Memory sometimes enables us to know of something not happening in the present that it happened in the past; what enables us to know of something not happening in the past, present or future that it could have happened? Christopher Peacocke’s answer roots the possible in the actual, more specifically in different ways of assigning actually existing items as the semantic values of Thoughts (on a broadly Fregean understanding) and their constituent concepts. These are his Characterizations of Possibility and Necessity (p. 150):

A Thought or proposition is possible iff it is true according to some admissible assignment.

A Thought or proposition is necessary iff it is true according to all admissible assignments.

Of course much of the work goes into describing the principles that are to determine whether an assignment is admissible, in ways which are at least sometimes epistemically accessible to us. One of the most important is the Unified Modal Extension Principle (p. 136):

MEP
An assignment ss is admissible only if: for any concept CC, the semantic value of CC according to ss is the result of applying the same rule as is applied in the determination of the actual semantic value of CC.

Peacocke states further Principles of Possibility too.

I will raise three issues for Peacocke’s theory of modality. Sections 1 and 2 ask what principles it validates in quantified and unquantified modal logic respectively. Section 3 concerns the interpretation of MEP.

1. Peacocke insists that his approach requires an actualist conception of modality (p. 153). His assignments actually exist. That is why they are epistemically accessible.

Peacocke’s actualism allows us to argue within his framework for the truth of the contentious Barcan formula (or Thought) in the form ◇∃xϕ(x)⊃∃x◇ϕ(x)◇\exists x\,\mathrm{\phi}(x) \supset \exists x\,◇\mathrm{\phi}(x) (‘If there could have been something to which ϕ\mathrm{\phi} applied, then there is something to which ϕ\mathrm{\phi} could have applied’), as follows. Suppose that the Thought ◇∃xϕ(x)◇\exists x\,\mathrm{\phi}(x) is true. Then the Thought ∃xϕ(x)\exists x\,\mathrm{\phi}(x) is possible. Therefore, by Peacocke’s Characterization of Possibility, ∃xϕ(x)\exists x\,\mathrm{\phi}(x) is true according to some admissible assignment ss. Now ss ‘has an associated domain of objects, which is the range of the quantifiers under that assignment’ (p. 126). On Peacocke’s view, ss actually exists, so the objects in its constituent domain actually exist too (what would his actualism amount to otherwise?). By Peacocke’s understanding of MEP, ∃xϕ(x)\exists x\,\mathrm{\phi}(x) is true according to ss if and only if the concept ϕ\mathrm{\phi} is true according to ss of some object o\mathrm{o} in the domain of ss; thus o\mathrm{o} actually exists. Let CC be an actual de jure rigid designator of o\mathrm{o}. By Peacocke’s application of MEP to de jure rigid concepts (‘For any concept CC which is de jure rigid, and whose actual semantic value is A\mathrm{A}, and for any admissible assignment ss, the semantic value of CC according to ss is A\mathrm{A}’, p. 136), CC also designates o\mathrm{o} according to ss. Consequently, the Thought ϕ(C)\mathrm{\phi}(C) is true according to ss. Therefore, by the Characterization of Possibility again, ϕ(C)\mathrm{\phi}(C) is possible, so the Thought ◇ϕ(C)◇\mathrm{\phi}(C) is true. Since CC rigidly designates the actually existing object o\mathrm{o}, and Peacocke treats what actually exists as the domain of the quantifiers in the determination of actual semantic values, the Thought ∃x◇ϕ(x)\exists x\,◇\mathrm{\phi}(x) is true. This seems to establish the truth of ◇∃xϕ(x)⊃∃x◇ϕ(x)◇\exists x\,\mathrm{\phi}(x) \supset \exists x\,◇\mathrm{\phi}(x) on Peacocke’s conception.

What if nothing rigidly designates o\mathrm{o} de jure? Since Peacocke permits quantification into modal contexts, he needs a characterization of possibility that states when a concept is possibly true of a sequence of objects. The natural extension of his Characterization of Possibility to this case is the principle that a concept is possibly true of a sequence of objects if and only if it is true of that sequence according to some admissible assignment. Therefore, since ϕ(x)\mathrm{\phi}(x) is true of o\mathrm{o} according to ss, ϕ(x)\mathrm{\phi}(x) is possibly true of o\mathrm{o}, so ◇ϕ(x)◇\mathrm{\phi}(x) is true of o\mathrm{o}; since o\mathrm{o} actually exists, ∃x◇ϕ(x)\exists x\,◇\mathrm{\phi}(x) is actually true, and the Barcan formula still holds.

Yet Peacocke describes the Barcan formula as ‘intuitively invalid’ (p. 153; he is speaking of the equivalent form with universal quantifiers and necessity operators, but the point is exactly the same). In the language of possible worlds, the Barcan formula requires the domain of the actual world to contain the domain of every possible world; why should the actual world have that privilege? For example, if ϕ\mathrm{\phi} is the concept child of Ludwig Wittgenstein and Betty Hutton, although there could have been a child of Ludwig Wittgenstein and Betty Hutton, it is far from obvious that any actually existing person or thing is such that it could have been a child of Ludwig Wittgenstein and Betty Hutton.

Peacocke suggests that he is in a position to deny the Barcan formula. ‘The actualist is not’ he writes ‘committed to endorsing only model theories in which the domain of every possible world is a subset of the domain of the actual world’ (ibid.). Why not? Actualists as such are presumably not committed to possible worlds semantics at all; they might take the modal operators as primitive. But Peacocke’s specific form of actualism does seem to require the domain of actually existing objects to contain the domain of every admissible assignment, which is just what is needed to verify the Barcan formula.

According to Peacocke, ‘The actualist can acknowledge the possibility of objects which do not actually exist, provided that these are constructed from the materials of the actual world’ (ibid.). But that remark fails to engage with the argument for the Barcan formula from Peacocke’s premises. If nothing actually is constructed out of the actual materials in the relevant way, then there is no such object to figure in the domain of any admissible assignment. Given the Barcan formula, there could have been something so constructed out of those materials only if there already is something that could have been so constructed out of them.

It is much harder than Being Known suggests for anything like its approach to avoid commitment to the Barcan formula and its contentious implications. My own view is that the Barcan formula is in fact a harmless necessary truth. There are many objects that could have been a child of Ludwig Wittgenstein and Betty Hutton, but they all lack spatio-temporal location; they are merely possible people, modal analogues of past people. They are not actually people, just as past people are no longer people. Nevertheless, they actually exist in the only sense of ‘exist’ relevant to quantified modal logic, just as past people still do (see my ‘Bare Possibilia’, Erkenntnis 48 (1998), pp. 257-73). I will not attempt to determine whether Peacocke’s account is consistent with such a metaphysics. If not, it is at some risk of internal inconsistency, by entailing both the Barcan formula and its negation.

2. From quantified modal logic I turn briefly to its unquantified base. Peacocke argues that his conception of modality validates all theorems of the propositional modal logic T\mathrm{T}. T\mathrm{T} is a rather weak system; its specifically modal part amounts only to the principles that necessity entails truth and is deductively closed. Its theorems do not include all sentences of the forms □A⊃□□A□ \mathrm{A} \supset □□ \mathrm{A} and ◇A⊃□◇A◇ \mathrm{A} \supset □◇ \mathrm{A}, the characteristic axioms of the systems S4 and S5 respectively. Peacocke argues that his conception may not validate the S4 schema; since it follows in T\mathrm{T} from the S5 schema, his argument extends to the latter (pp. 195-97). Thus his conception allows that whether something is necessary may itself be a contingent matter. But does his conception really validate at least all theorems of T\mathrm{T}?

Peacocke offers a proof of the following Metatheorem (pp. 193-95):

Every theorem of T\mathrm{T} is a schema the necessity of any instance of which, in the sense of the principle-based conception, follows from the Modal Extension Principle, the Characterization of Necessity, and the rules for determining the actual semantic values of the propositional logical constants.

In the course of his proof, Peacocke has to argue that his principles imply that every instance of the T\mathrm{T} axiom schema □A⊃A□ \mathrm{A} \supset \mathrm{A} (in present notation) is true. He writes ‘From MEP, it […] follows that s@s_{@} is admissible’ (p. 194), where s@s_{@} is the assignment that assigns to each concept its actual semantic value. Thus an instance of A\mathrm{A} is true according to all admissible assignments only if it is true according to s@s_{@} and therefore true absolutely, so by the Characterization of Necessity and the rule for ⊃\supset the corresponding instance of □A⊃A□ \mathrm{A} \supset \mathrm{A} is true. But how can it follow from MEP that s@s_{@} is admissible, as Peacocke claims? MEP purports to state a necessary condition for an assignment to be admissible. On Peacocke’s own view, that condition is insufficient for admissibility, because it is met by assignments that violate de re necessary truths by assigning objects to the wrong fundamental kinds; further Principles of Possibility are needed to exclude such assignments (pp. 144-46). In fact, MEP, the Characterization of Necessity and the rules for determining the actual semantic values of the propositional logical constants do not entail that there are any admissible assignments at all, and therefore do not entail that all instances of □A⊃A□ \mathrm{A} \supset \mathrm{A} are true. The same would hold even if one added all the other Principles of Possibility and showed that s@s_{@} satisfied all their necessary conditions on admissibility, for although doing so is in fact a sufficient condition for admissibility (p. 149), one would still have to prove that one’s list of Principles of Possibility was indeed complete, which on Peacocke’s account would be no easy task. Alternatively, one might appeal to some kind of general constraint on Principles of Possibility. I will not speculate on the prospects for reconstructing a proof of Peacocke’s conjecture along such lines. It is hard to speak confidently without more precise formulations of MEP and the other principles; the final section discusses one of the unclarities in MEP. In any case, Peacocke has not yet shown that his conception validates all theorems of T\mathrm{T}.

3. Given Peacocke’s broadly neo-Fregean approach, one might suppose the rule for determining the semantic value of a first-level concept such as mountain to be something like this:

MOUNTAIN
The semantic value of mountain is the function from objects to truth-values that maps an object o\mathrm{o} to the True if and only if o\mathrm{o} is a mountain.

By MEP, the semantic value of mountain according to an admissible assignment ss is the result of applying the same rule. Presumably, therefore, the semantic value of mountain according to ss is the function from elements of the domain of ss to truth-values that maps an element o\mathrm{o} to the True if and only if o\mathrm{o} is a mountain. In general, the semantic value of a first-level concept according to an admissible assignment will be the restriction of its actual semantic value to the domain of that assignment. But that is not at all what Peacocke intends. For consider any contingently true universal generalization, such as No mountain is a cube. Since it is true, no object is mapped to the True by both the semantic value of mountain and the semantic value of cube. A fortiori, for any admissible assignment ss, no element of the domain of ss is mapped to the True by both the semantic value of mountain according to ss and the semantic value of cube according to ss. Therefore, by the application of MEP to the quantifier No, the Thought No mountain is a cube is true according to every admissible assignment. But then the Characterization of Necessity falsely implies that No mountain is a cube is a necessary truth.

When Peacocke raises the question ‘What, in the general case, is the rule which determines the actual semantic value of a concept?’ (p. 134), he suggests that it may be determined by a possession condition for the concept, or by an implicit conception governing the concept, but stipulates that his account of modality does not involve those theoretical notions. MEP is supposed to work on any reasonable conception of the rules determining the semantic values of concepts (p. 135). MOUNTAIN looks reasonable enough. Even if the concept mountain could somehow be analysed into more basic concepts, it seems reasonable to suppose that the overall effect would still be tantamount to MOUNTAIN. So what rules out the disastrous application of MEP to MOUNTAIN just considered?

Someone might object that, on the proper application of MEP to MOUNTAIN, the semantic value of mountain according to an admissible assignment ss is the function from elements of the domain of ss to truth-values that maps an element o\mathrm{o} to the True if and only if o\mathrm{o} is a mountain according to ss, rather than in fact (see Peacocke’s discussion of the concept diamond-shaped on p. 135). But that objection does not fit Peacocke’s notion of an assignment. Assignments are not possible worlds in any metaphysically serious sense; they are simply functions from concepts to semantic values (and some associated apparatus, such as a domain of quantification). To be a mountain according to ss just is to be mapped to the True by the semantic value to which ss maps the concept mountain, which is just the semantic value we are trying to determine.

In practice, Peacocke seems not to apply MEP in full force to first-level atomic concepts. He treats their semantic values as constrained only by his other Principles of Possibility. It is not clear why that does not constitute an ad hoc exception to his approach. Although the modal concepts which he is seeking to explicate might themselves be ad hoc in that respect, that does not seem terribly likely, and Peacocke himself emphasizes the principled nature of his approach. Until we know how, if at all, MEP is to be applied to first-level atomic concepts, it is hard to check that Peacocke’s theory of modality does not lead to some kind of modal collapse.
