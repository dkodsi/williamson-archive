Wright on the epistemic conception of vagueness

Timothy Williamson

Analysis 56 (1) (1996): 39–45.

1

According to the epistemic conception of vagueness defended in Williamson 1994, what we use vague terms to say is true or false, but in borderline cases we cannot know which. Our grasp of what we say does not open its truth-value to our view. Crispin Wright 1995 offers a lively critique of this conception. A reply may help to clarify the issues.1

The first point to note is that epistemicism is not a denial that our terms are vague; it is a theory about what their acknowledged vagueness consists in. We all recognize examples of vagueness when we encounter them in borderline cases; we can then go on to construct alternative hypotheses as to the underlying nature of the phenomenon. Wright acknowledges this point, for he credits epistemicism with ‘[t]he merit […] of bringing out that the ordinary idea of genuine semantic indeterminacy is not itself a datum, but a proto-theory of data’ (134, Wright’s emphasis). Unfortunately, some of his criticisms neglect the point. For example, he takes the epistemicist to hold that ‘when I intend that you should stand roughly here, […] the demarcation of the range of cases in which you would comply from that in which you would not is already perfectly precise’ (155, Wright’s emphasis; other such remarks occur on 153 and 155). Precision is the absence of vagueness. On the epistemic view, ‘Stand roughly here’ is a vague request, not a precise one, but its vagueness is an epistemic matter. Our understanding of it is such that we cannot know where we cease to comply with it. Even the metaphor of vague concepts as blurred shadows (133) can be interpreted epistemically. Fortunately, Wright’s main arguments do not depend on this misstatement; they must now be addressed.

2

Part of the case for epistemicism is an explanation of our inability to know whether vague terms apply in borderline cases (Williamson 1994: 216–47). It uses independently motivated principles about knowledge. Suppose that xx judges truly that PP. If xx could too easily have judged falsely by the same method, then xx has judged truly only by luck, and xx does not know that PP.2 Contrapositively, if xx does know that PP, then xx could not too easily have judged falsely by that method. But xx could only too easily have judged by that method in circumstances that differ from the actual ones by less than xx’s threshold of discrimination. Such judgements must therefore be true, if xx knows that PP. Not all circumstances indiscriminable by xx from the actual ones could very easily have occurred (consider brains in vats): but some could have. For example, if xx judges truly that a tower is at least a mile away, on the basis of a casual glance, then xx could very easily have made the same judgement by the same method while standing a few inches nearer. Thus for xx to know at a glance that the tower is at least a mile away, the tower must be at least a few inches more than a mile away (although xx need not know that it is). In such cases, knowledge requires a margin for error. Vague knowledge is distinctive, for the relevant alternative situations in which error undermines actual knowledge include some in which xx makes a judgement by the same method whose content differs slightly from the content of xx’s actual judgement, because xx’s dispositions to use the vague terms in question differ slightly but systematically from xx’s actual dispositions. For simplicity, consider Wang’s Paradox (if 0 is small and every small natural number has a small successor, then every natural number is small). If xx judges ‘7 is small’, then xx could very easily have made a judgement by the same method, expressed in the same words, while xx and others were in general slightly less willing to apply the term ‘small’. Such a slight shift in dispositions could make ‘small’ apply to nn with only the correctness with which it actually applies to n+1n+1; that is what vague terms are like. Thus if nn is known to be small, the application of ‘small’ to n+1n+1 in those counterfactual circumstances would be correct, so its actual application to nn is also correct. This yields the margin for error principle that if nn is known to be small, then n+1n+1 is small. Hence one cannot know where the boundary of smallness runs. For to know the conjunction that nn is small and n+1n+1 is not, one would have to know the first conjunct, but then the second conjunct would be false, by the margin for error principle, so the conjunction would be false, and therefore not known.

Wright points out that ignorance of the exact location of cut-off points is not the only ignorance to be explained. Suppose that nn, n+1n+1 and n+2n+2 are borderline for ‘small’ (presumably three such numbers exist). Given epistemicism, either it is unknowably true that nn is small, even though n+1n+1 is small, or it is unknowably true that n+2n+2 is not small, even though n+1n+1 is not small. Assume the first disjunct (the argument for the second is parallel). Why can’t we know that nn is small? Since n+1n+1 is also small, such knowledge does not conflict with the margin for error principle above. The answer to Wright is that the principle generalizes. It holds because the difference between nn and n+1n+1 is less than the relevant margin for error. The general principle here is that if the difference between nn and mm is at most the relevant margin for error, and nn is known to be small, then mm is small. The width of the margin is set by the vagueness of ‘small’, which desensitivizes particular uses of ‘small’ to the overall pattern of use. Since at least three numbers are borderline, the margin for error is greater than one. Suppose, for simplicity, that it is two, and that n+1n+1 is small while n+2n+2 is not. Then it is both unknowably true that nn is small and unknowably true that n+1n+1 is small. The explanation in Williamson 1994 generalizes as it should.

Wright thinks that ‘what is most fundamentally unsatisfying’ about that explanation is something else (151). Granted that Williamson 1994 has explained why we cannot know n+1n+1 to be small in the way in which we actually know nn to be small, why can’t we know it in some other way? Even if our margin for error prevents us from knowing at a glance that the tower is at least a mile away, when it is less than an inch more than a mile away, we can still use measuring instruments to find out. Why can’t we do something similar for ‘small’?

The answer is that ‘mile’ is precisely defined. A mile is 1760 yards; a yard is 36 inches; ‘inch’ is itself precisely defined. Our ordinary perceptual application of these terms does not fix their reference; it defers to the precise definitions. In contrast, the ordinary application of vague terms like ‘small’ defers to no such definitions. If we tried to construct an instrument that would measure whether something was a heap, we should not know how to calibrate it. Although it might detect the number of grains, that would not help, for our ignorance in principle is not of how many grains there are but of whether that many grains can constitute a heap. The margin for error principles distinctive of vagueness differ from those distinctive of ordinary perceptual inexactness in just that way.

None of this shows that in borderline cases knowledge is metaphysically impossible. Who knows what infinite minds with unimaginable methods could do? To demand explanations of their capacities or incapacities is pointless. The datum is that we have no idea how to acquire knowledge in borderline cases; epistemicism can explain it.

3

According to Wright, ‘The root and most fundamental criticism of epistemicism is that it has no account to offer of what constitutes reference. […] Epistemicism cannot even gesture at an account of how the transcendent referential relations in which it believes might be constituted’ (153, Wright’s emphases). This is puzzling, for epistemicism is consistent with most available theories of reference. For illustrative purposes only, take an implausibly crude causal theory, on which a term refers to whatever predominantly causes its use. Thus ‘red’ refers to the property that predominantly causes the use of ‘red’. Now ‘predominantly causes’ is itself vague, so the theory is not guaranteed to clear up borderline cases for ‘red’. When we cannot know whether something is red, we may be equally incapable of knowing whether it has the property that predominantly causes the use of ‘red’. That use will be causally correlated with many properties of slightly varying specificity; we shall not know which one to identify as the predominant use. On this account, what decides correctness of application in borderline cases is exactly what decides it in clear cases.

Those points obviously generalize to many less crude theories of reference, causal or otherwise. When a verificationist theory of reference appears, it may be inconsistent with epistemicism, but that is not yet an objection to the latter (Wright makes no explicit appeal to verificationism in his paper). A more general challenge is this. Suppose that someone offers precise necessary and sufficient conditions for reference, and in particular for being the property to which ‘red’ refers. Since having that property is necessary and sufficient for being red, we are offered precise necessary and sufficient conditions for being red. Epistemicism is not the only conception of vagueness on which we cannot hope to identify such conditions. One moral is that, even if it were clear that some family of precise theories of reference contained the one true theory of reference, it would not be clear which one of them it was. Epistemicists do not expect that we shall ever find an algorithm for calculating reference; does anyone expect that we shall?

Wright reformulates his objection, charging epistemicism with helping itself to ‘a rampant form of content externalism’ (156). Content externalism is usually defined as something like the denial that content supervenes on what is in the head. Certainly epistemicism has no reason to reject the powerful arguments against such supervenience, but that is not the heart of the matter. The dispute between epistemicist and antiepistemicist accounts of ‘small’ would change little if both sides accepted, for the sake of argument, that the reference of ‘small’ as used by xx does supervene on what is in xx’s head. Wright seems to be using the phrase ‘content externalism’ in a non-standard sense. By ‘external factors’, he seems to mean factors unknown to xx, rather than factors outside xx’s head. Thus content internalism becomes the doctrine that the reference of xx’s terms is wholly determined by factors known to xx. Wright contrasts epistemicism with ordinary forms of content externalism, e.g. those for which Putnam and Burge have argued: the latter ‘always proceed on the basis of an intentional abrogation of semantic responsibility’; the former does not (155). This is to make content internalism the default setting for reference. Such a reading of the arguments for content externalism is far from obviously correct.

The epistemicist rejection of the conditional that epistemicism is correct only if users of vague terms intend it to be correct has counterparts for other forms of content externalism. For example, defenders of evolutionary theories of content are entitled to reject the conditional that a frog’s contents depend on evolutionary history only if the frog intends them to do so. More generally, it is not implausible to postulate that thought develops (in the species, perhaps also in the individual) through a stage at which there is reference to natural kinds in the environment but no concept of reference, and therefore no intention to refer one way rather than another. For example, the possibility that an elephant wants water (rather than whatever has some cluster of phenomenal properties) should not be ruled out merely because the elephant has not intentionally abrogated semantic responsibility. It is likewise a less than compelling objection to evolutionary theories of content that, since creationists do not intend their contents to depend on evolutionary history, their contents do not depend on evolutionary history.

Epistemicism has not been shown to be inconsistent with anything taught by the theory of reference.

4

One argument for epistemicism is the difficulty of giving a coherent non-epistemic characterization of borderline cases. In particular, characterizations of them as involving truth-value gaps run into difficulty with disquotational principles about truth. Wright toys with the idea of rejecting such principles.3 However, he prefers not to characterize borderline cases as involving truth-value gaps, on the grounds that genuine indeterminacy between two alternatives excludes neither. He uses the operator ‘definitely’ instead: to be borderline red is to be neither definitely red nor definitely not red, where ‘not definitely red’ and ‘not definitely not red’ do not entail ‘not red’ and ‘not not red’ respectively. One might therefore expect a non-epistemic account of what ‘definitely’ means (for some obstacles to providing one see Williamson 1995a). Surprisingly, that is not what Wright attempts to provide. Rather, he explains ‘definitely’ in epistemic terms, commenting ‘the epistemicist need not disagree with this’ (145). Wright does not insist on the details of his explanation; doubts about them can be laid aside for present purposes. Suppose that his explanation of ‘definitely’ is indeed acceptable to the epistemicist, who agrees that, in that sense, some things are neither definitely red nor definitely not red. On Wright’s view, ‘the epistemicist differs from the indeterminist not by rejecting the latter’s conception of what a borderline case is but by adding something to it: the addition, namely, of the hypothesis of universal determinacy in truth-value: the principle of bivalence’ (145, Wright’s emphasis), while the indeterminist is ‘unwilling to accept bivalence’ (146). So far, the parties do not disagree; the epistemicist has merely said more than the indeterminist. But that is not the only difference between them. The indeterminist regards the epistemicist’s account of borderline cases as positively incorrect. The epistemicist is supposed to regard borderline claims as determinate in truth-value, while the indeterminist regards them as not determinate in truth-value.

What is it to be determinate in truth-value? Since Wright describes the principle of bivalence as ‘the hypothesis of universal determinacy in truth-value’, one might initially assume that to be determinate in truth-value is to be bivalent, i.e. true or false. But then the indeterminist would be postulating truth-value gaps in borderline cases, contrary to Wright’s intention. The obvious alternative is that to be determinate in truth-value is to be either determinately true or determinately false. One might therefore expect an account of what ‘determinately’ means. Wright provides none. ‘Determinately’ cannot mean the same as ‘definitely’, for it is supposed to be consistent with epistemicism that borderline claims are neither definitely true nor definitely false but not consistent with it that they are neither determinately true nor determinately false. To make the intended distinction between epistemicism and indeterminism, we need a non-epistemic account of what ‘determinately’ means.4 But if such an account were available for ‘determinately’, why was it not used to explain ‘definitely’ in the first place? Most people use the two words more or less interchangeably. If Wright follows the indirect method of explaining ‘definitely’ in epistemic terms, it is presumably because he thinks that there is some obstacle to the direct method of explaining ‘definitely’ in non-epistemic terms. If so, is there not the very same obstacle to explaining ‘determinately’ in non-epistemic terms?

Epistemicism can be formulated without the use of a non-epistemic ‘determinately’ operator. It is indeterminism which needs such an operator, to distinguish itself from epistemicism without postulating truth-value gaps. When all Wright’s formidable ingenuity cannot begin to provide an account of what such an operator would mean, the coherence of indeterminism should not be taken for granted. As Wright says ‘Perhaps the most basic problem for the indeterminist is to characterize what vagueness consists in – to say what a borderline case is’ (146).
