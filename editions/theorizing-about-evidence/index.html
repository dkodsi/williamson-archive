Theorizing about evidence

Timothy Williamson

Philosophical Studies 179 (2022): 2697–2705.

Abstract

The paper defends the infallibilist account of evidential support in Knowledge and its limits from Jessica Brown’s objections in her book Fallibilism: Evidence and Knowledge. (1) By the standards of abductive methodology, Brown’s arguments are ineffective because she offers no developed alternative account of evidential support. (2) Most of her objections apply to the standard probabilistic structure of my account; they are not specific to its distinctively knowledge-first features. (3) Brown’s objection from ‘infelicity data’ is analogous to one from such data to standard accounts of deductive validity as truth-preservation. (4) Contrary to Brown’s assumption, my use of excusability in discussion of sceptical scenarios involves no attempt to give it a precise conceptual analysis.

Keywords: Evidence · Evidential support · Infallibilism · Jessica Brown · Abduction · Excusability · Infelicity

Fallibilism: Evidence and Knowledge (FEK, Brown 2018) displays the virtues which readers of Jessica Brown’s work have come to expect. It is clear, carefully argued, resourceful, undogmatic, up to date, and never silly. Brown engages in detail with what her opponents say, investigating questions in a spirit of leaving no stone unturned.

The book is a critique of contemporary infallibilism, which Brown defines as the principle that ‘one can know that p only if one’s evidence guarantees that p’ (2; all such references are to pages of FEK). I develop such a view in Knowledge and its Limits; it follows trivially from my equation of one’s evidence with what one knows. The equation is to be read in a ‘knowledge first’ way, as explaining evidence in terms of knowledge rather than knowledge in terms of evidence; unlike more traditional versions of infallibilism, it has no sceptical consequences. Most of Brown’s objections target my work, but sometimes she also has in her sights others who have contributed to the ‘knowledge first’ programme in epistemology and the philosophy of action, such as John Hawthorne, Maria Lasonen-Aarnio, Clayton Littlejohn, Jennifer Nagel, Amia Srinivasan, and Jason Stanley. Unsurprisingly, I dispute many of the book’s main claims and arguments—more than I can discuss here. I will focus on some of the most important issues.

1 Comparing Theories

One basic divergence concerns philosophical methodology. The dispute between fallibilism and infallibilism is obviously a theoretical one, and Brown treats it as such. Like many contemporary analytic philosophers, I take the appropriate methodology for theoretical disputes in philosophy to be abductive. Rival theories are compared with respect to their abductive virtues: fit with evidence, but also simplicity, elegance, informativeness, and explanatory power. Philosophical explanations are normally non-causal; instead, they involve unifying diverse phenomena under illuminating general principles. I have defended this methodology elsewhere, and explained why it does not imply a reduction of philosophy to natural science (Williamson, 2016, 2018, 2021).

Brown does not address such methodological issues in her book. She simply applies a more traditional philosophical methodology of piecemeal dialectic, laying out various possible lines of objection and reply. This is not another way of presenting abductive arguments. The reason is that she is not comparing two properly worked-out theories. She is comparing one worked-out theory, infallibilism as found in Knowledge and its Limits and other ‘knowledge first’ writings, with a much sketchier version of fallibilism. Sketchiness is itself an abductive vice; it implies a lack of informativeness and explanatory power. But it does not figure as a dimension of assessment in Brown’s accounting of the merits and demerits of fallibilism and infallibilism.

To understand why Brown’s methodology is problematic, consider Thomas Kuhn’s claim that any scientific paradigm at any time has unresolved anomalies (Kuhn, 1962). At least as a generic generalization, that is highly plausible. Thus, if a scientific paradigm is compared with a mere sketch as the alternative by Brown’s methodology of piecemeal dialectic, the paradigm is likely to come out second-best, because its opponent can press its unresolved anomalies against it, while the sketch can be kept vague and unspecific enough not to generate such anomalies. Done that way, science would get nowhere. Since philosophical theories are just as subject to unresolved anomalies as are theories in natural science, the methodological moral applies to philosophy too.

The issue is already implicit in Brown’s characterizations of fallibilism and infallibilism. As already noted, she treats infallibilism as a universal generalization (on ‘p’), ‘one can know that p only if one’s evidence guarantees the truth of p’, which follows from my equation of evidence with knowledge when, as seems to be intended, ‘guarantee’ is read as ‘entail’ and ‘can’ as meaning that the ‘only if’ connection is necessary. Brown defines fallibilism as the view that ‘one can know that p even though one’s evidence does not guarantee the truth of p’ (2). That may look like another universal generalization (on ‘p’), but I take that not to be what Brown intends, for two reasons. First, if both fallibilism and infallibilism are read as universal generalizations, there is obvious room for views which are both anti-fallibilist and anti-infallibilist. On such views, some propositions can only be known if one’s evidence guarantees their truth, while others can be known even though one’s evidence does not guarantee their truth. However, Brown structures the debate as simply between fallibilism and infallibilism, with no middle ground. Thus fallibilism and infallibilism should be contradictories, not just contraries. Since infallibilism is a universal generalization, fallibilism should be a mere existential generalization: at least one proposition can be known even though one’s evidence does not guarantee its truth. Second, if Brown’s fallibilism is a universal generalization, it faces obvious counterexamples. For instance, on that reading it entails the universal generalization ‘one can know that p’ (dropping the second conjunct), which is false for impossible propositions, and for unknowable truths (Williamson, 2000: 270–301).

Thus Brown’s definitions should be read as making infallibilism a universal generalization, fallibilism an existential one. Infallibilism is like a proposed scientific law, fallibilism like the proposition that there is at least one counterexample to the ‘law’. From an abductive perspective, that is a huge asymmetry. Fallibilism as such is barely up for abductive assessment at all, because it is so minimal, while infallibilism is exactly the sort of claim that is up for abductive assessment. Of course, the situation might change once both views are developed and extended by further commitments. The next section tests that by considering a key principle about evidence.

2 Evidence for

Knowledge and its Limits defends a simple probability-raising account of the evidence-for relation, this simple universal generalization:

PR
e is evidence for h for S if and only if S’s evidence includes e and P(h∣e)>P(h)\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{h} \mid \mathrm{e}) > \mathrm{P}(\mathrm{h})

here P(h)\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{h}) is the unconditional prior probability of the hypothesis h and P(h∣e)\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{h} \mid \mathrm{e}) is the conditional prior probability of h on e, usually defined as the ratio P(h&e)/P(e)\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{h} \mathbin{\&} \mathrm{e})/\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{e}) (for P(e)>0\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{e}) > 0). The idea that evidence for a hypothesis just is evidence which raises its probability is a rather standard one, though not universally accepted. In itself, it carries no commitment to the ‘knowledge first’ programme. Formally, PR can be combined with the view that S’s evidence may contain falsehoods, and so propositions which S does not know. PR has distinctive ‘knowledge first’ consequences only when combined with the equation of S’s evidence with what S knows. Equally, PR can be combined with the fallibilist view that S’s evidence is restricted to truths about S’s internal mental states, while S’s knowledge is not so confined.

One major advantage of PR is that it anchors the evidence-for relation firmly within the well-understood mathematical framework of probability theory. That greatly clarifies its structural properties, and imposes a salutary discipline on our thought and talk about what is evidence for what. Such an account has obvious abductive virtues.

An easy consequence of PR is the self-support principle that whatever is evidence for anything is evidence for itself:

SS
e is evidence for h for S only if e is evidence for e for S.

To argue from PR to SS, suppose that e is evidence for h for S. By PR from left to right, S’s evidence includes e and P(h∣e)>P(h)\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{h} \mid \mathrm{e}) > \mathrm{P}(\mathrm{h}). But one can easily show that if P(e)=1\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{e}) = 1 then P(h∣e)=P(h)\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{h} \mid \mathrm{e}) = \mathrm{P}(\mathrm{h}). Thus P(e)<1\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{e}) < 1. But P(e∣e)=1\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{e} \mid \mathrm{e}) = 1. Hence S’s evidence includes e and P(e∣e)>P(e)\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{e} \mid \mathrm{e}) > \mathrm{P}(\mathrm{e}). Therefore, by PR from right to left, e is evidence for e for S, as required for SS. This little proof makes no distinctively ‘knowledge first’ or infallibilist assumptions about evidence; it simply uses the logico-mathematical structure of PR itself.

Brown regards SS as a disastrous consequence, sufficient to discredit PR. She spends most of chapters 2 and 3 attacking the idea that a proposition can be evidence for itself. Her main weapon is the ‘infelicity data’, that people are unhappy with a proposition’s being cited as evidence for itself.

What Brown notably fails to do is to offer any alternative to PR. Fallibilists need an account of the evidence-for relation just as much as infallibilists do. As noted, merely rejecting infallibilism in no way blocks the argument from PR to SS.

Of course, Brown is not automatically obliged to characterize the evidence-for relation in specifically probabilistic terms. However, two points are worth noting about the prospects for a non-probabilistic characterization. First, if one abandons the framework of probability theory for anything less formally constrained, one thereby provides less structural information about the evidence-for relation, and so does worse in that respect on abductive comparisons. Second, even if one holds that a probabilistic framework is not relevantly applicable to some cases where the evidence-for relation does apply, it is uncontroversial that a probabilistic framework is applicable to many other cases where the evidence-for relation also applies in ways correlated with the probabilistic relations. In the latter cases, one might expect the more general evidence-for relation to boil down to some probabilistic relation; one wonders what that probabilistic relation would be, and how it would avoid SS. The higher the conditional probability of a hypothesis on the evidence, the better for whether the evidence is evidence for the hypothesis; since the conditional probability of the evidence on itself is maximal, something like SS looks hard to avoid without implausible consequences in other directions.

Even were Brown willing to defend some alternative to PR, she would need to show that it was not equally available to infallibilists, since infallibilism in itself is not committed to PR. She does consider the Threshold view, which replaces ‘P(h∣e)>P(h)\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{h} \mid \mathrm{e}) > \mathrm{P}(\mathrm{h})’ in PR by ‘P(h∣e)>t\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{h} \mid \mathrm{e}) > \mathrm{t}’ for some probability threshold t, but correctly notes that it too entails SS, unless t is unreasonably set at 1 (47–8). She also mentions the ad hoc variant of PR which merely adds the conjunct ‘e is not h’ to the right-hand side of PR (50), and so is incompatible with SS, though that would permit small circles of support, where e is evidence for f for S while f is evidence for e for S. People unhappy with S’s citing e as evidence for itself are unlikely to be happy with S’s citing e as evidence for f having just cited f as evidence for e. Anyway, since my version of infallibilism includes PR and so SS, I will defend those principles.

Could Brown just take the infelicity data as decisive evidence against PR, trumping all other abductive considerations even in the absence of alternatives? She is not so uncompromising, since she responds in detail to various strategies for explaining away the infelicity data (50–66). Thus, from an abductive perspective, Brown’s failure to provide any alternative to PR may well leave PR holding the field, even in the presence of those data. One might regard the latter as just the sort of unresolved Kuhnian anomaly which just about any scientific paradigm has to put up with. However, infallibilists need not be so concessive, for the infelicity data should not surprise them.

Consider an analogy: the classification of arguments as ‘valid’ or ‘invalid’, ‘sound’ or ‘unsound’. Logicians standardly define validity as truth-preservation: whenever the premises are true, so is the conclusion. A sound argument is a valid argument with true premises (and so a true conclusion). ‘Whenever’ in the definition can be cashed out in various ways, for example as ‘in every model’ or ‘in every possible world’; those differences are irrelevant here. On all those ways, any argument from a premise to itself is trivially valid: whenever the premise is true, the conclusion is true, because the conclusion is the premise. Similarly, if the argument from g to h is sound, so is the argument from g to itself (a principle with the same structure as SS). Validity is reflexive, and soundness is quasi-reflexive.

The infelicity data for the words ‘valid’ and ‘sound’ seem similar to those for the phrase ‘evidence for’. To adapt one of Brown’s examples, imagine this exchange between two non-logicians in the context of a conversation about the trial of Charlie for murder (58):

Ben:
There is evidence that Charlie was seen attacking the victim.
Anne:
What is it?
Ben:
That Charlie was seen attacking the victim is evidence for the proposition that Charlie was seen attacking the victim.

Probably, the exchange will leave Anne bemused and annoyed, and ordinary readers will share her reaction. The proffered evidence sounds entirely question-begging. Now imagine a variant exchange like this in the same context:

Ben:
There is a valid/sound argument that Charlie was seen attacking the victim.
Anne:
What is it?
Ben:
Charlie was seen attacking the victim. Therefore, Charlie was seen attacking the victim.

Probably, either version of this exchange will leave Anne as bemused and annoyed as the previous one did, and ordinary readers will again share her reaction. The proffered argument sounds entirely question-begging.

As Brown rightly emphasizes, negative reactions to Ben’s second speech in the evidence-for exchange are not confined to doubts as to whether he is in an epistemic position to make it—he might not know that the proposition that Charlie was seen attacking the victim is part of the evidence base at issue (as PR requires). A natural thought is: ‘Whatever Ben knows, that Charlie was seen attacking the victim can’t be evidence for the proposition that Charlie was seen attacking the victim, because that evidence just begs the question’. Similarly, negative reactions to Ben’s second speech in the soundness exchange are not confined to doubts as to whether he is in an epistemic position to make it—he might not know that Charlie was seen attacking the victim (soundness requires truth, though validity does not). A natural thought is: ‘Whatever Ben knows, that argument can’t be sound, because it just begs the question’. Such reactions seem quite similar for evidence-for, validity, and soundness.

Nevertheless, it would be frivolous to reject standard logical definitions of validity and soundness on the basis of the corresponding infelicity data. One possibility is that logicians use the words ‘valid’ and ‘sound’ in technical senses not coextensive with their ordinary senses. Perhaps the latter concern more general matters of context-relative dialectical appropriateness, which logicians are not trying to capture in their use of the words ‘valid’ and ‘sound’; they are after something else, which may be more theoretically fruitful. Similarly, it is possible that formal epistemologists use the combination ‘evidence for’ in a technical sense (as in PR and SS) not coextensive with its ordinary sense. Perhaps the latter also concerns more general matters of context-relative dialectical appropriateness, which formal epistemologists are not trying to capture in their use of the combination ‘evidence for’; they too are after something else, which again may be more theoretically fruitful. On this ambiguity approach, the infelicity data need not reflect ordinary errors, but are no concern of logicians and formal epistemologists—including infallibilists—whose aim is not faithfulness to ordinary usage.

Another possibility is that logicians use the words ‘valid’ and ‘sound’ in senses coextensive with their ordinary senses, but when ordinary people use the words they are confused by more general matters of context-relative dialectical appropriateness, and so misapply them. Similarly, it is possible that formal epistemologists use the combination ‘evidence for’ (as in PR and SS) in a sense coextensive with its ordinary sense, but when ordinary people use the combination they are again confused by more general matters of context-relative dialectical appropriateness, and so misapply it. Such misapplications might result from reliance on an imperfectly reliable heuristic, for the extensions of our terms are rarely transparent to us (see Williamson, 2020 for other examples). On this error approach, the infelicity data do reflect ordinary errors, but are still no concern of logicians and formal epistemologists—including infallibilists—who aim to overcome such errors.

We need not decide between the ambiguity and error approaches here, since they serve present purposes equally well. Rather than treating Brown’s infelicity data as an unresolved anomaly for the infallibilist account, we can resolve it in either way.

Brown’s discussion of error theories (60–66) suggests that she would press the question: why accept even the disjunction of the ambiguity and error accounts? The answer is that we are doing theoretical epistemology, for which an abductive methodology is the best available, and our theoretical terms are best used to mark the most theoretically significant distinctions. We are not doing ordinary language philosophy. By offering no abductively viable alternative account of an evidence-for relation, Brown has in effect ceded the field to PR, at least for the time being. Although we can imagine that someone will produce an abductively superior account, none is to be found in the book.

None of this means that we should give up trying to understand the dialectical considerations which seem to underlie the infelicity data. However, we should expect the results to be messy. The infelicity data seem closely related to our rejection of some arguments as question-begging, but the large literature on ‘the fallacy of begging the question’ has come to no satisfying conclusion. When one tries give a natural generalization of the fallacy beyond arguments whose conclusion is also a premise, a typical result is a definition on which all valid arguments beg the question (see Sinnott-Armstrong, 1999 for an introduction to the issues). What gets counted as begging the question is highly sensitive to the mind-set of the people one wishes to persuade. For purposes of epistemological theorizing, it is better to start with something much simpler, such as the evidence-for relation defined by PR. Probably no theory of begging the question and the like has much abductive virtue (but see Fratantonio (2021) for more discussion of the infelicity data).

From an abductivist viewpoint, Brown’s style of argument against PR never had much prospect of success, because it offered no serious alternative. Thus her leading argument against infallibilism poses no threat. Of course, Brown may reject the abductivist approach, though I can find no reason for doing so in the book. I hope that she will discuss these methodological issues in the future.

3 Excuses

Not all Brown’s objections to infallibilism depend on the infelicity data. She also uses more familiar forms of fallibilist argument, from sceptical scenarios and Gettier cases, in both of which one seems to know without really doing so. For simplicity, I will concentrate on sceptical scenarios, in particular those starring a brain in a vat (BIV).

Having sketched a standard pair of a good case and a corresponding bad case, in which appearances to the BIV are just as they would be in the good case, Brown writes: ‘Intuitively, both the person and the BIV are equally justified in believing that they have limbs, and have the same strength of support for the claim that they have limbs’ (68). But, on my equation of evidence with knowledge, the regular person and the BIV differ in their evidence. The regular person knows that she has limbs, so the proposition that she has limbs is part of her evidence. By contrast, the BIV lacks limbs, and so does not know that it has limbs, so the proposition that it has limbs is not part of its evidence. Since the regular person’s evidence entails that she has limbs, while the BIV’s evidence does not entail that it has limbs, on my view they do not have the same strength of support for the claim that they have limbs, contrary to what Brown presents as ‘intuitively’ the case. She then argues in detail against various infallibilist attempts to explain away this clash with ‘intuition’.

Brown’s ‘intuitively’ is contentious, for several reasons. Amia Srinivasan (2020) gives examples of culturally and politically charged sceptical scenarios about which many people are unreflectively inclined to deny the analogous claims of equal justification and strength of support, suggesting that there may be no clear ‘intuitive’ view. Moreover, Brown uses semi-technical terms to articulate what she takes to be ‘intuitively’ the case. Although ‘justified’ is a word in reasonably common use, epistemologists normally specify that when they describe someone as ‘justified in believing’ something, they mean epistemically justified, as contrasted with the way in which someone might be pragmatically justified in believing something because their doctor told them that believing it would be good for their health. Thus the relevant ‘intuitive’ judgment that the regular person and the BIV are equally justified in believing that they have limbs is in effect an ‘intuitive’ judgment that they are equally epistemically justified in believing it. But what authority can be attributed to an ‘intuitive’ judgment about the theoretical issue of equality in epistemic justification? Similarly, the relevant ‘intuitive’ judgment that the regular person and the BIV have the same strength of support for the claim that they have limbs is in effect an ‘intuitive’ judgment that they have the same strength of epistemic support for the claim. But what authority can be attributed to an ‘intuitive’ judgment about the theoretical issue of sameness of strength of epistemic support? There is a danger here that philosophical prejudices are being recycled as pretheoretical intuitions.

Elsewhere, I have defended the view that the BIV is not strictly justified in believing that it has limbs, because it is violating the knowledge norm of belief (believe p only if you know p), but we look kindly on the BIV, because its belief is the product of the same cognitive dispositions as the regular person’s, though exercised under what are in fact unfavourable conditions; thus the BIV has a good excuse and is blameless (Williamson 202X). Given a forced choice between ‘justified’ and ‘unjustified’, we choose ‘justified’ to do justice to the BIV’s blamelessness, but that only reflects the poverty of normative statuses on offer. Given limitations of space, I will confine myself to making rather peremptory comments on some of Brown’s objections to this account.

Surprisingly, Brown takes my use of the category of excuses to involve an attempt to give necessary and sufficient conditions for something to be an excuse. She considers various such attempts, in both epistemic and non-epistemic terms, and argues that none of them succeeds (79–91). She may be right about that, but I made no such attempt. I take the category of excuses to be a way of cutting people some slack, of creating a flexible buffer zone between the inflexible simplicity of norms (such as the knowledge norm of belief) and the messy, unpredictable complexities of real life. The demand for clear, informative necessary and sufficient conditions for something to be an excuse strikes me as a chillingly legalistic denial of the very reason for which we need the category in the first place: because we cannot survey in advance all the relevant combinations of circumstances which may arise. We must be left the discretion to judge how good an excuse something is when the occasion arises. Although I explained how any norm gives rise to various general kinds of excuse relevant to epistemology, there was never any suggestion of the absurd claim that all excuses are of those kinds.

One general kind of excuse is of this form: X violated norm N, but X did what someone disposed to comply with N would do in those circumstances. Brown notes that an existential reading of ‘someone’ makes the condition too easy to meet, while a universal reading makes it too hard to meet, given the variety of people disposed to comply with N, and a reading with ‘most’ run into related difficulties (88–90; my original paper already warned of such complications, at n. 5). However, if Brown’s considerations were decisive, they would also show that apparently sensible questions like ‘What would a law-abiding person have done in those circumstances?’ backfire, and do not mean what they might be taken to mean, for instance in a legal context. But there are more promising ways to interpret such sentences. For instance, we can read the quantifier as generic, within the scope of ‘would’, read as a contextually restricted necessity operator (for the latter see Williamson, 2020: 168–74). Such an interpretation is suitably robust to the sorts of objection Brown makes, and allows such questions to mean what they seem to mean.

4 Conclusion

For all Brown’s meticulous efforts, ‘knowledge first’ infallibilism is alive and well.
