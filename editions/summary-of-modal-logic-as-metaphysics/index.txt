# Summary of Modal Logic as Metaphysics

Timothy Williamson

Analysis 76 (2016): 153–155.

What is the relation between modal logic and modal metaphysics? As a technical discipline, modal logic is in effect a branch of applied mathematics that studies extensions of standard formal languages by additional sentence operators, independently of any particular interpretation of those operators. Modal metaphysics studies the nature and extent of possibility and necessity, where those terms are understood in a strong non-epistemic ‘metaphysical’ sense: something is necessary just in case it would have obtained no matter what, and possible just in case it does not necessarily not obtain. Informally, metaphysicians can read the operators □ and ◇ of modal logic as expressing such necessity and possibility, although that reading plays no official role in the technical development. When the modal language also contains quantifiers, metaphysicians will naturally read them as unrestricted. That metaphysical reading of the modal operators and quantifiers will be assumed in what follows. Metaphysicians want to know which formulas of the modal language are metaphysically universal, in the sense of being true on every interpretation of the non-logical constants and free variables in them. The metaphysically universal formulas, whichever they are, constitute the core metaphysical theory of the most general structure of modal reality. We can use modal logic as a technical aid in studying that theory.

Not all metaphysically universal formulas are uncontroversial platitudes. Consider this formula:

NNE
$□\forall x\,□\exists y\;x = y$

NNE says that necessarily everything is necessarily something. In the minimal sense in which to exist is just to be something, NNE says that necessarily everything has necessary existence. NNE is either true or false. Since it contains no non-logical constants or free variables, if it is true it is metaphysically universal and if it is false its negation ¬NNE is metaphysically universal. Thus either NNE is metaphysically universal or ¬NNE is. Necessitists accept NNE; contingentists accept ¬NNE.

NNE is no platitude. On a contingentist view, NNE is false because a material thing exists only contingently; it could have been nothing at all. But ¬NNE is no platitude either. On a necessitist view, NNE is true because, although the material thing could have failed to be material, it would still have been a possibly material thing, and other alleged counterexamples can be dealt with similarly. Each view is formally coherent. Common sense has no obvious authority to adjudicate between them: why should the everyday cognitive capacities equipped to inform us about properties and relations in the domain of material things also be equipped to inform us about the domain of immaterial (but perhaps possibly material) things? A more theoretical approach is needed to decide for or against NNE.

The necessitist-contingentist dispute has extensive ramifications in modal logic and metaphysics. Most necessitists accept the Barcan formula and its converse; most contingentists reject them. In the model theory of quantified modal logic, necessitism corresponds to constant domain semantics, contingentism to variable domain semantics. Given necessitism, a strong truthmaker principle makes all modal distinctions collapse; given contingentism, it does not. The necessitism–contingentism dispute captures one clear issue caught up in the obscure debate between actualism and possibilism, which was bogged down in unclarity as to what it takes to be actual, beyond just being.

One of the book’s main aims is to develop an abductive case for necessitism, by showing that necessitist modal logic constitutes a smoother and more powerful background logic for reasoning, including mathematical and other scientific reasoning, about counterfactual possibilities. The differences are especially marked when one moves from first-order to higher-order modal logic.

The ascent to higher-order modal logic is already implicit in the definition of metaphysical universality for first-order modal languages. When a formula contains non-logical predicate constants, its metaphysical universality depends on a generalization over their interpretations which is best made explicit in the object-language by adding quantification into predicate position, that is, higher-order quantification. In such a higher-order extension of the modal object-language, one can capture the gist of metaphysical universality in non-metalinguistic terms. The higher-order quantification is best understood directly, as sui generis. Attempts to reduce it to first-order quantification over properties or the like fall to versions of Russell’s paradox. Plural interpretations are inappropriate for a modal language because they prevent the extension of a predicate variable from varying freely across possibilities. Substitutional interpretations depend inappropriately on the expressive capacity of the language. In Quinean jargon, the sui generis interpretation is not ontological, because it is not reducible to first-order quantification, but it is also not merely ideological, because it is just as metaphysically committing as first-order quantification: the assumption that the ontological–ideological distinction is exhaustive is a relic of first-orderist dogma. Worries about ontological commitment should be replaced by more general abductive comparisons of the simplicity and power of theories, as in the natural sciences.

First-order contingentists face a dilemma in higher-order modal logic. They must choose between higher-order necessitism and higher-order contingentism. The combination of first-order contingentism with higher-order necessitism is a poorly motivated hybrid. But higher-order contingentist logic is too weak to underpin science’s free application of mathematical reasoning in modal contexts.

Contingentists sometimes try to simulate necessitist ways of thinking in contingentist terms, in order to get the practical benefits without paying the metaphysical costs. Such attempts may succeed for first-order modal logic, but they fail for higher-order modal logic.

Necessitism yields a satisfying picture of the most general structure of modal reality, as wholly non-contingent: any true formula of higher-order modal logic with no non-logical constants or free variables is a necessary truth. This does not abolish contingency altogether, but merely contains it within a necessary framework. There is a threat to all genuine contingency, but it comes from the idea that the fundamental facts are non-contingent ones about which states of affairs obtain relative to which possible worlds. Neither necessitism nor contingentism mandates that idea, nor is it required for the semantics of modal languages. We can make sense of possible worlds, but we should assign them no such fundamental role.
