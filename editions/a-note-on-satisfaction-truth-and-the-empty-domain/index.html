A note on truth, satisfaction and the empty domain

Timothy Williamson

Analysis 59 (1) (1999): 3–8.

An attractive principle about domains of quantification is the analogue of the Separation Axiom in set theory: restricting a domain by an arbitrary predicate yields a domain. In particular, restricting a domain by a predicate that applies to nothing yields a domain. Thus if there is a nonempty domain, there is an empty domain. But semantics for the empty domain involves some neglected subtleties. Untangling them requires us to revise the usual definition of truth in a model, avoiding the detour through Tarski’s notion of satisfaction.

Some terminology: An individual variable is a variable that takes name position. A model as usually understood interprets quantified individual variables as ranging over a domain whose members are of the type used to interpret names; call them ‘individuals’. The terms ‘variable’ and ‘domain’ will henceforth be tacitly qualified by ‘individual’. An empty model is a model whose domain is the empty set. Even for a fixed language, empty models can differ from each other in their assignments to atomic sentence letters. An inclusive logic is a logic whose theorems are true in all models, including the empty ones.1 An inclusive logic with constant singular terms is a free logic, in the sense that those terms denote nothing in the domain, but the interpretation of singular terms is not the primary concern of this note. For readability, the presentation will be informal.

It is usual to assume, following Quine, that every formula of the form ∀vϕ\forall v\,\mathrm{\phi} is vacuously true in every empty model, and every formula of the form ∃vϕ\exists v\,\mathrm{\phi} false, where the variable vv may but need not occur in the formula ϕ\mathrm{\phi}, and that theorems of ordinary first-order logic such as ∀xFx⊃∃xFx\forall x\,Fx\supset\exists x\,Fx, ∃x(Fx⊃Fx)\exists x\,(Fx\supset Fx) and ∃xx=x\exists x\,x=x are therefore not theorems of inclusive logic, even though they are true in every nonempty model.2 The proof theory of inclusive logic is supposed to involve corresponding restrictions to block the proof of such theorems.

Consider, by contrast, a standard Tarski-inspired definition of truth in a model. For any model M\mathrm{M}, an M\mathrm{M}-assignment is a function mapping each variable of the language to a member of the domain of M\mathrm{M}; functions are treated as sets of ordered pairs. One first defines what it is for an M\mathrm{M}-assignment to satisfy an open or closed formula ϕ\mathrm{\phi} in M\mathrm{M} by a recursion on the complexity of ϕ\mathrm{\phi}. If ϕ\mathrm{\phi} is closed, every M\mathrm{M}-assignment satisfies ϕ\mathrm{\phi} in M\mathrm{M} if any does. One then defines truth in M\mathrm{M} as satisfaction in M\mathrm{M} by every M\mathrm{M}-assignment. If M\mathrm{M} is empty, there are no M\mathrm{M}-assignments, for no function maps each (or any) member of a nonempty set (the set of variables of the language) to a member of the empty set. Consequently, every formula of the language, irrespective of its structure, is vacuously true in every empty model (Schneider 1958: 86). A fortiori, every formula true in every nonempty model is also true in every empty model, so a logic sound and complete for nonempty models is already a sound and complete inclusive logic without any modification of its proof theory, even though it has theorems such as ∀xFx⊃∃xFx\forall x\,Fx\supset\exists x\,Fx, ∃x(Fx⊃Fx)\exists x\,(Fx\supset Fx) and ∃xx=x\exists x\,x=x. Inclusive logic seems to be easier than we had thought.

The disadvantage of this resolution is that it grossly violates the intended meaning of many formulas. All distinctions of truth-value are lost in empty models. ϕ\mathrm{\phi}, ¬ϕ\neg\mathrm{\phi} and ϕ∧¬ϕ\mathrm{\phi}\wedge\neg\mathrm{\phi} come out equally true, as do formulas such as ∃xx=x\exists x\,x=x which intuitively should express the nonemptiness of the domain. If falsity in M\mathrm{M} is defined by analogy to truth in M\mathrm{M} as satisfaction by no M\mathrm{M}-assignment in M\mathrm{M}, then every formula is false as well as true in every empty model; even ϕ⊃ϕ\mathrm{\phi}\supset\mathrm{\phi} comes out false. The same follows if ϕ\mathrm{\phi} is stipulated to be false in M\mathrm{M} just in case ¬ϕ\neg\mathrm{\phi} is true in M\mathrm{M}. If one makes the alternative stipulation that ϕ\mathrm{\phi} is false in M\mathrm{M} just in case ϕ\mathrm{\phi} is not true in M\mathrm{M}, then no formula is false in any empty model. The Quinean ruling that ∀vϕ\forall v\,\mathrm{\phi} is true and not false and ∃vϕ\exists v\,\mathrm{\phi} false and not true in every empty model comes much closer to our intention, but it is not what our standard semantics delivers.

Things would be even worse if one defined truth in M\mathrm{M} as satisfaction by some (in place of every) M\mathrm{M}-assignment in M\mathrm{M}. For then no formula would be true in an empty model, so a sound inclusive logic would have no theorems at all.

We might reflect that, for a fixed model M\mathrm{M}, whether an M\mathrm{M}-assignment satisfies ϕ\mathrm{\phi} in M\mathrm{M} depends only on the values it assigns variables occurring free in ϕ\mathrm{\phi}. We might therefore define an M,ϕ\mathrm{M},\mathrm{\phi}-assignment as a function mapping each variable free in ϕ\mathrm{\phi} to a member of the domain of M\mathrm{M}, recursively define what it is for an M,ϕ\mathrm{M},\mathrm{\phi}-assignment to satisfy ϕ\mathrm{\phi} in M\mathrm{M}, and then define ϕ\mathrm{\phi} to be true in M\mathrm{M} just in case every M,ϕ\mathrm{M},\mathrm{\phi}-assignment satisfies ϕ\mathrm{\phi} in M\mathrm{M}. In particular, an M,∀vϕ\mathrm{M},\forall v\,\mathrm{\phi}-assignment aa satisfies ∀vϕ\forall v\,\mathrm{\phi} in M\mathrm{M} just in case every M,ϕ\mathrm{M},\mathrm{\phi}-assignment differing from aa at most over vv satisfies ϕ\mathrm{\phi} in M\mathrm{M}; an M,∃vϕ\mathrm{M},\exists v\,\mathrm{\phi}-assignment aa satisfies ∃vϕ\exists v\,\mathrm{\phi} in M\mathrm{M} just in case some M,ϕ\mathrm{M},\mathrm{\phi}-assignment differing from aa at most over vv satisfies ϕ\mathrm{\phi} in M\mathrm{M}. When ϕ\mathrm{\phi} is closed, the quantification over M,ϕ\mathrm{M},\mathrm{\phi}-assignments in the definition of truth in M\mathrm{M} is thereby neutralized, for exactly one function maps each variable free in ϕ\mathrm{\phi} to a member of the domain of M\mathrm{M}; it is the empty set of ordered pairs, and constitutes the only M,ϕ\mathrm{M},\mathrm{\phi}-assignment. Since the M,¬ϕ\mathrm{M},\neg\mathrm{\phi}-assignments are just the M,ϕ\mathrm{M},\mathrm{\phi}-assignments, and an M,¬ϕ\mathrm{M},\neg\mathrm{\phi}-assignment satisfies ¬ϕ\neg\mathrm{\phi} in M\mathrm{M} just in case it does not satisfy ϕ\mathrm{\phi} in M\mathrm{M}, a closed formula ¬ϕ\neg\mathrm{\phi} is true in M\mathrm{M} if and only if ϕ\mathrm{\phi} is not true in M\mathrm{M}, so closed formulas are no longer all equivalent in truth-value in empty models. When ϕ\mathrm{\phi} is open and M\mathrm{M} empty, there are no M,ϕ\mathrm{M},\mathrm{\phi}-assignments, and ϕ\mathrm{\phi} is again vacuously true irrespective of structure, but open formulas are in any case hard to interpret; what matters most is the treatment of closed formulas.

To be more specific, suppose that vv occurs free in ϕ\mathrm{\phi}. Then an M,∀vϕ\mathrm{M},\forall v\,\mathrm{\phi}-assignment aa satisfies ∀vϕ\forall v\,\mathrm{\phi} in M\mathrm{M} just in case for every member dd of the domain of M\mathrm{M} the M,ϕ\mathrm{M},\mathrm{\phi}-assignment a∪{⟨v,d⟩}a\cup\{\langle v,d\rangle\} satisfies ϕ\mathrm{\phi} in M\mathrm{M}; correspondingly, an M,∃vϕ\mathrm{M},\exists v\,\mathrm{\phi}-assignment aa satisfies ∃vϕ\exists v\,\mathrm{\phi} in M\mathrm{M} just in case for some member dd of the domain of M\mathrm{M} the M,ϕ\mathrm{M},\mathrm{\phi}-assignment a∪{⟨v,d⟩}a\cup\{\langle v,d\rangle\} satisfies ϕ\mathrm{\phi} in M\mathrm{M}. Thus if M\mathrm{M} is empty, every M,∀vϕ\mathrm{M},\forall v\,\mathrm{\phi}-assignment vacuously satisfies ∀vϕ\forall v\,\mathrm{\phi} in M\mathrm{M} and no M,∃vϕ\mathrm{M},\exists v\,\mathrm{\phi}-assignment satisfies ∃vϕ\exists v\,\mathrm{\phi} in M\mathrm{M}. Hence if vv is the only variable free in ϕ\mathrm{\phi}, so ∀vϕ\forall v\,\mathrm{\phi} and ∃vϕ\exists v\,\mathrm{\phi} are closed, ∀vϕ\forall v\,\mathrm{\phi} is true in M\mathrm{M} and ∃vϕ\exists v\,\mathrm{\phi} is not. Non-vacuously quantified formulas are distinguished in truth-value in the Quinean manner. By contrast, if vv is not free in ϕ\mathrm{\phi}, the M,∀vϕ\mathrm{M},\forall v\,\mathrm{\phi}-assignments are just the M,ϕ\mathrm{M},\mathrm{\phi}-assignments, and such an assignment satisfies ∀vϕ\forall v\,\mathrm{\phi} if and only if it satisfies ϕ\mathrm{\phi}; likewise for ∃vϕ\exists v\,\mathrm{\phi}. Thus if ϕ\mathrm{\phi} is closed, ∀v(ϕ∧¬ϕ)\forall v\,(\mathrm{\phi}\wedge\neg\mathrm{\phi}) is not true in an empty model while ∃v(ϕ⊃ϕ)\exists v\,(\mathrm{\phi}\supset\mathrm{\phi}) is true, but such vacuous quantifications constitute another peripheral case.

The method of M,ϕ\mathrm{M},\mathrm{\phi}-assignments is an ad hoc patch, with many unattractive features. For instance, as Mostowski in effect pointed out, it does not validate modus ponens: if FF is a monadic predicate, Fx⊃FxFx\supset Fx and (Fx⊃Fx)⊃∃x(Fx⊃Fx)(Fx\supset Fx)\supset\exists x\,(Fx\supset Fx) are valid, in particular true in empty models because open, while ∃x(Fx⊃Fx)\exists x\,(Fx\supset Fx) is invalid, because untrue in empty models (Mostowski 1951). Moreover, a method that sometimes makes both ϕ\mathrm{\phi} and ¬ϕ\neg\mathrm{\phi} true in the same model, as this one does whenever ϕ\mathrm{\phi} is open and the model empty, thereby deviates at least somewhat from the intuitive reading of ¬ϕ\neg\mathrm{\phi} as the negation of ϕ\mathrm{\phi}.3

The fault may be traced to the indirect definition of truth in a model, by a recursion on satisfaction rather than truth itself. Indeed, the definition of truth in terms of satisfaction is intuitively suspect even for nonempty models, for like supervaluationism it permits true disjunctions without a true disjunct. For example, if the extension of FF in M\mathrm{M} includes some but not all members of the domain, Fx∨¬FxFx\vee\neg Fx is true in M\mathrm{M} even though neither FxFx nor ¬Fx\neg Fx is true in M\mathrm{M}. Similarly, the definition of truth in terms of satisfaction permits true existential generalizations without a true instance, even when every member of the domain is the denotation of some constant. For example, if the domain of M\mathrm{M} has at least two members, ∃yx=y\exists y\,x=y is true in M\mathrm{M} but x=cx=c is not true in M\mathrm{M} for any constant cc. These results make sense if an open formula standing alone is interpreted as its universal closure, but that involves postulating a curious kind of ambiguity. Thus FxFx is interpreted as ∀xFx\forall x\,Fx when it stands alone but not when it occurs as a constituent of a complex formula, for Fx∨GxFx\vee Gx is interpreted as ∀x(Fx∨Gx)\forall x\,(Fx\vee Gx), which is not equivalent to ∀xFx∨∀xGx\forall x\,Fx\vee\forall x\,Gx. The proposition expressed by Fx∨GxFx\vee Gx (in a context of utterance) is not the disjunction of the propositions expressed by FxFx and GxGx (in that context). Alternatively, one could restrict the application of ‘true’ and ‘false’ to closed formulas, which implies that open formulas do not express propositions. A final stage of interpretation would be applied to closed formulas but not to open ones. A semantics that fully interprets closed formulas by recursion on less than fully interpreted open formulas is not straightforwardly compositional; it does not generate the full interpretations of complex expressions from the full interpretations of their constituents. While not decisive, these puzzles prompt the consideration of other approaches.

If one defines truth in a model by a direct recursion, one will stipulate that ¬ϕ\neg\mathrm{\phi} is true in M\mathrm{M} just in case ϕ\mathrm{\phi} is not true in M\mathrm{M}, and automatically avoid the lack of truth-value discrimination in empty models. The challenge is to state intuitively adequate truth-conditions for ∀vϕ\forall v\,\mathrm{\phi} and ∃vϕ\exists v\,\mathrm{\phi} in terms of truth in M\mathrm{M}, given that the possibly uncountable domain of M\mathrm{M} may contain unnamed members. Notoriously, ∀xFx\forall x\,Fx may be false in M\mathrm{M} even though FcFc is true in M\mathrm{M} for every constant cc of the language; likewise, ∃xFx\exists x\,Fx may be true in M\mathrm{M} even though FcFc is false in M\mathrm{M} for every constant cc. The problem can be overcome by quantification over possible extensions of the language, if one is willing to assume that every member of the domain of a model has a name in some possible extension of the language.4 But that assumption becomes highly contentious if combined with the (not uncontentious) view that every possible language could be used by some possible speaker; it is not obviously incoherent to suppose that there are or could have been objects essentially incapable of being singled out for naming by any possible speaker. They might include very small particles or very large sets.5 In any case, the apparatus of possible languages is suspiciously cumbersome.

One can achieve the same effect more economically by defining truth in M\mathrm{M} by direct recursion for a single expansion of the language in which for every variable vv and member dd of the domain of M\mathrm{M} the pair ⟨v,d⟩\langle v,d\rangle is a closed singular term denoting dd.6 Let ϕ[v∣d]\mathrm{\phi}[v\mid d] be the result of replacing each free occurrence of vv in ϕ\mathrm{\phi} by ⟨v,d⟩\langle v,d\rangle; this replacement is well-defined if we think of ϕ\mathrm{\phi} as a sequence in a mathematical rather than a spatial or temporal sense. Then ∀vϕ\forall v\,\mathrm{\phi} (∃vϕ\exists v\,\mathrm{\phi}) is true in M\mathrm{M} just in case for every (some) member dd of the domain of M\mathrm{M} ϕ[v∣d]\mathrm{\phi}[v\mid d] is true in M\mathrm{M}; open formulas do not figure in this recursion. If M\mathrm{M} is empty, ∀vϕ\forall v\,\mathrm{\phi} is vacuously true in M\mathrm{M} and ∃vϕ\exists v\,\mathrm{\phi} is not true in M\mathrm{M}. Even this semantics is compositional only in a stretched sense; an instance ϕ[v∣d]\mathrm{\phi}[v\mid d] of ∀vϕ\forall v\,\mathrm{\phi} is not strictly its constituent.

There is no pretence that a possible speaker could use this extension of the language as a language, or ⟨v,d⟩\langle v,d\rangle as a name of dd. Rather, the device may be one step towards a semantics in which Russellian propositions mediate between sentences and their truth-values.

An alternative approach is to view quantifiers over the ‘inner’ domain as the result of restricting quantifiers over a nonempty ‘outer’ domain or absolutely unrestricted quantifiers, permit assignments to assign variables members of the outer domain, define satisfaction by a recursion where an assignment aa satisfies ∀vϕ\forall v\,\mathrm{\phi} (∃vϕ\exists v\,\mathrm{\phi}) in M\mathrm{M} just in case every (some) assignment differing from aa at most over vv and assigning vv a member of the inner domain satisfies ϕ\mathrm{\phi} in M\mathrm{M}, and define truth as satisfaction by all assignments.7 That would yield the Quinean rulings. But it still generates the puzzles about compositionality noted above. Moreover, some elements outside the inner domain are extrinsic to the model as usually understood. For purposes of model theory, we may wish to make the definition of truth in M\mathrm{M} intrinsic to M\mathrm{M}; the direct recursion on truth above meets that condition.
