Inexact Knowledge

Timothy Williamson

Mind 101 (402) (1992): 217–242.

Most of our knowledge is inexact, and known by us to be so. An example of such known inexactness will be described in some detail. The description seems to entail a contradiction. However, the paradoxical reasoning rests on an assumption. It will be suggested that the description is correct and this assumption false. Its failure will be explained by means of a picture of inexact knowledge in which the notion of a margin for error is central. This picture suggests diagnoses of other paradoxical arguments: Surprise Examinations, backwards inductions about Iterated Prisoner’s Dilemma, and the Heap.

1. The Distant Tree

Looking out of my window, I can see a tree some distance off. I wonder how tall it is. Naturally, I cannot tell to the nearest inch just by looking. My eyesight and ability to judge heights are nothing like that good. Since I have no other source of relevant information at present, I do not know how tall the tree is to the nearest inch. For no natural number mm do I know that the tree is mm inches tall, i.e. more than m−0.5m-0.5 and not more than m+0.5m+0.5 inches tall. Nevertheless, by looking I have gained some knowledge. I know that the tree is not 60 or 6,000 inches tall; I do not know whether it is 600 inches tall. For many natural numbers mm, I do not know that the tree is not mm inches tall. More precisely, for many natural numbers mm, I do not have knowledge whose content is expressed by the result of replacing “mm” in “The tree is not mm inches tall” by a numeral designating mm (e.g. “600”). Not under discussion is knowledge (whose content is expressed by a sentence) in which mm is designated by a definite description, such as “The number of inches the tree is tall minus one”, for I may not know which number fits the description.

The least number principle says that every non-empty set of natural numbers has a least member; it is equivalent to the principle of mathematical induction. Since the set of natural numbers mm such that I do not know that the tree is not mm inches tall is non-empty, it has a least member nn by the least number principle; thus:

(1)
I know that the tree is not n−1n-1 inches tall.
(2)
I do not know that the tree is not nn inches tall.

By a remark in the previous paragraph, nn is at most 600. “nn” abbreviates the definite description “the least number mm such that I do not know that the tree is not mm inches tall”; “n−1n-1” abbreviates a corresponding description. However, by remarks at the end of the last paragraph, (1) and (2) do not concern knowledge in which numbers are designated by such descriptions; what makes them true or false is knowledge or the lack of it in which nn or n−1n-1 is designated by an ordinary numeral such as “600” or “599”. In technical terms, the definite descriptions in (1) and (2) have wide scope.

I do not know how tall the tree is to the nearest inch. I might guess but, even if I guessed right, that would not be knowing how tall the tree is to the nearest inch. If I judge that the tree is mm inches tall, and in fact it is, for all I know it is really m−1m-1 or m+1m+1 inches tall—I do not know that it is not. A fortiori, if I do not judge that the tree is mm inches tall, when in fact it is, I do not know that it is not m−1m-1 or m+1m+1 inches tall. Either way, if the tree is mm inches tall, I do not know that it is not m−1m-1 or m+1m+1 inches tall. These reflections are quite independent of the value of “mm”. There can be no natural number mm such that the tree is mm inches tall and I know that it is not, say, m−1m-1 inches tall, for if the tree is mm inches tall and I judge that it is not m−1m-1 inches tall, I am merely guessing. Anyone who can tell by looking that the tree is not m−1m-1 inches tall, when in fact it is mm inches tall, has much better eyesight and a much greater ability to judge heights than I have. For every natural number mm, if the tree is mm inches tall, I do not know that it is not m−1m-1 inches tall.1

The foregoing reflections are mine. I know that my eyesight and ability to judge heights are not good enough for me to tell by looking that a tree is not m−1m-1 inches tall, when in fact it is mm inches tall. I know that for all natural numbers mm, if the tree is mm inches tall, I do not know that it is not m−1m-1 inches tall. Indeed, I may be assumed to have pedantically instantiated this knowledge for every number less than 800. For each such number mm, I know that if the tree is mm inches tall, I do not know that it is not m−1m-1 inches tall. For example, I know that if the tree is 600 inches tall, I do not know that it is not 599 inches tall. Since nn in (1) and (2) is at most 600, it follows too that:

(3)
I know that if the tree is nn inches tall, I do not know that the tree is not n−1n-1 inches tall.

Now contradiction looms. By (1), I do know that the tree is not n−1n-1 inches tall. If I combine this knowledge with my knowledge in (3), I can apparently deduce, and thereby come to know, that the tree is not nn inches tall. But that contradicts (2). What has gone wrong?

We can be more explicit. There is a limit to how much I can add to my knowledge of the height of the tree by reflecting on the limitations of my eyesight and ability to judge heights and making deductions from what I know. It is indeed plausible, although this point will not be pressed, that such processes add nothing to the knowledge of the height of the tree I can gain just by looking and judging. Be that as it may, it is legitimate to build into the example the supposition that I go to the trouble of deducing in a logically competent way all propositions of the form “the tree is not mm inches tall” that can be deduced from what I know, for every mm less than 800. After some time I have completed the task. We can define “nn” in (1)-(3) with respect to a time tt at which this reflective equilibrium has been attained; it still designates a number less than 800. Since the defining description for “nn” does not figure in the content of the knowledge under discussion, this procedure involves no vicious kind of self-reference or ungroundedness. We can also take tt as the time of the knowing at issue in (1)-(3). Thus (3) says that I know at tt that if the tree is nn inches tall, I do not know at tt that it is not n−1n-1 inches tall. There is again nothing viciously self-referential or ungrounded about this present knowledge of present (rather than past) ignorance, for it is based on general considerations about my eyesight and ability to judge heights, not on a futile attempt to survey all the propositions I do not presently know. Now if it follows from what I know at tt that the tree is not nn inches tall, I have already deduced that by tt, and have presumably thereby come to know it. Thus at tt:

(4)
If I know some propositions, and from those propositions it logically follows that the tree is not nn inches tall, I know that the tree is not nn inches tall.

Note that (4) is not a general principle about knowledge; it is simply a description of my state in a particular situation (the same goes for (1)-(3), of course). The paradox is that each of (1)-(4) appears to be true with respect to the envisaged mundanely possible situation, yet they appear to be mutually inconsistent.

One thought is that I might know my premises and competently deduce my conclusion without thereby coming to know it because, although the premises are probable enough to count as known, the conclusion is not. For a logical consequence of two propositions may be less probable than each of them. Thus (4) might fail. For several reasons, this is an unpromising diagnosis. (a) It is counterintuitive to suppose that making competent deductions from what we know is not in general a way of extending our knowledge. (b) The connection assumed between knowledge and probability has not been made out. No degree of probability less than 1 by itself makes knowledge out of true belief; the pessimistic owner of a lottery ticket who rightly believes that it will not win does not know that it will not, however many tickets have been sold. (c) What sort of probability is in question? Degrees of believe seem too subjective, and worldly propensities too objective, to form a standard for knowledge. If we consider probabilities conditional on what I know, what I know has probability 1 and the objection to (4) lapses. (d) Nothing in the example appears to disallow the assumption that it is absolutely certain, in whatever sense is relevant to the kind of probability at issue, that my eyesight and ability to judge heights are not good enough for me to know that the tree is not n−1n-1 inches tall, given that it is in fact nn inches tall. Then the probability of the conditional in (3) will be 1, as will be the probability of its consequent conditional on its antecedent. But then the objection to (4) still lapses, for the required drop in probability from premises to conclusion cannot occur. If two premises entail a conclusion, and one premise has probability 1, the conclusion is at least as probable as the other premise. (e) We can always introduce a notion of knowledge#, where to know# a proposition is to know either it or propositions from which one has competently deduced it. I do not know# how tall the tree is to the nearest inch. We could run through the argument with “know#” in place of “know”; (4) would then be correct.2 In the light of (a)-(e), the paradox should not be blamed on the failure of competent deduction to extend knowledge.

Another thought is that vagueness is somehow to blame. Some think that the least number principle cannot be used in the presence of vagueness; what is the least number of grains to make a heap? The charge is that “nn” in (1)-(4) fails to refer. It was defined by “the least number mm such that I do not know that the tree is not mm inches tall”. The only word here that might be relevantly vague is “know”. It is indeed vague to some extent. The question is whether this vagueness is the source of the paradox. If it is, the paradox would vanish if “know” were made precise. We might stipulate principles about exactly how much justification, reliability etc. is required for knowledge. We could not expect in practice to eliminate all the vagueness in “know”, but we do not need to. All we need are principles that resolve each borderline case of the form “I know that the tree is not mm inches tall” in the present context, for then we could apply the least number principle to define “nn”, making (1) and (2) true. Surely we could reduce vagueness to that extent. Of course, although each case would have been resolved, I might not know which way, for I might not know whether my level of justification or reliability came up to the required standard (we are not interested in ad hoc stipulations to the effect that I do, or do not, know a specified proposition). Now our stipulations would have done nothing to improve my eyesight or ability to judge heights. Conditionals of the form “If the tree is mm inches tall, I do not know that it is not m−1m-1 inches tall” would remain true on the new reading of “know” for the same reason as before; to make the concept of knowledge more precise is not to start counting lucky guesses as knowledge, for they are clear cases of lack of knowledge. Moreover, my attitude towards the conditionals is a clear case of knowledge, so I should know them in the new sense of “know”. Thus (3) remains true on the new reading, as does (4). Thus the apparently inconsistent propositions (1)-(4) are still apparently true. Since the paradox would not vanish if “know” were made relevantly precise, and no other term is relevantly vague, vagueness is not the source of the difficulty. It is therefore legitimate in what follows to treat “know” as though it were precise.

We need to examine the paradoxical reasoning in more detail. When (4) is applied, what are the propositions from which it logically follows that the tree is not nn inches tall? The major premise is the conditional “If the tree is nn inches tall, I do not know that the tree is not n−1n-1 inches tall”. The form of the inference is from “If AA, not BB” and BB to “Not AA”. Thus AA is “The tree is nn inches tall” and BB is “I know that the tree is not n−1n-1 inches tall”. If I am to use the inference to gain knowledge of its conclusion, I must know its premises, as (4) requires. (3) says that I know the major premise. I must also know the minor, BB, i.e. (1). That is, the paradoxical reasoning assumes:

(5)
I know that I know that the tree is not n−1n-1 inches tall.

The mutually inconsistent propositions are (2)-(5), not (1)-(4).

One might think for a moment that since I know by definition of “nn” that (1) is true, (5) is true. However, that would be to mistake the role of the definite description abbreviated by “n−1n-1”, “the predecessor of the least number mm such that I do not know that the tree is not mm inches tall”. Certainly I know that for every number kk, if kk fits the description then I know that the tree is kk inches tall. But that is not what (5) says. The whole argument concerns knowledge in which numbers are designated by numerals, not by definite descriptions such as that above; only on this understanding is (3) plausible. What (5) says is that if a number fits the description abbreviated by “n−1n-1”, I know that I know that the tree is not that number (as designated by a numeral) of inches tall; in technical terms, the definite description must be given the widest possible scope. If (5) were read otherwise, the reasoning would be trivially fallacious. Since I do not know what number fits the definite description abbreviated by “n−1n-1”, reflection on the description does nothing to make (5) true.

The apparently true propositions are (1)-(4). The inconsistent ones are (2)-(5). The right thing to do is the obvious one: accept (1)-(4) and reject (5). This is of course to reject the “KK” principle that if I know something, I know that I know it. I know that the tree is not n−1n-1 inches tall, but I do not know that I know that the tree is not n−1n-1 inches tall.3

Before the proposal is adopted, we should check that it really does meet the difficulty. We can do this by giving a kind of consistency proof for (1)-(4) and the negation of (5). This will be done by means of a simple model of some situations in one of which (1)-(4) is true and (5) is false. The model is not intended to be realistic; what it shows is the structure of the envisaged solution to the paradox. Although the argument could be carried out in purely formal terms, the stipulations are more revealing when made informally.

For each number mm, let sms_m be a situation in which the tree is mm inches tall, so for any number kk (the result of substituting the numeral for kk for “kk” in) “The tree is kk inches tall” is true in sms_m if and only if k=mk=m. We now assign truth-values to compound sentences in different situations. The negation of AA is true in sms_m if and only if AA is not true in sms_m. “If AA, BB” is true in sms_m if and only if either AA is not true in sms_m or BB is true in sms_m. “I know that AA” is true in sms_m if and only if AA is true in each of sm−1s_{m-1} (if it exists), sms_m and sm+1s_{m+1}. It is easy to check that for any positive mm the conditional “If the tree is mm inches tall, I do not know that the tree is not m−1m-1 inches tall” is true in every situation; thus, “I know that if the tree is mm inches tall, I do not know that the tree is not m−1m-1 inches tall” is true in every situation. In particular, (3) is true in every situation. (4) is also true in every situation, for I know all the logical consequences of what I know in this simplified model. Since “The tree is not n−1n-1 inches tall” is true in every situation but sn−1s_{n-1}, “I know that the tree is not n−1n-1 inches tall”, i.e. (1), is true in every situation but sn−2s_{n-2}, sn−1s_{n-1} and sns_n, while (5) is true in every situation but sn−3s_{n-3}, sn−2s_{n-2}, sn−1s_{n-1}, sns_n and sn+1s_{n+1}. Similarly, “I know that the tree is not nn inches tall” is true in every situation but sn−1s_{n-1}, sns_n and sn+1s_{n+1}, so (2) is true just in those three situations. Thus (1)-(4) are all true in sn+1s_{n+1}, while (5) is false (see Appendix for detailed results).

The model proves that avoidance of the KK principle removes the inconsistency. Indeed, it shows that one can consistently combine (1) and (2) with unqualified versions of the generalizations from which (3) and (4) were derived:

(3+)
For every natural number mm, I know that if the tree is mm inches tall, I do not know that the tree is not m−1m-1 inches tall.
(4+)
For every natural number mm, if I know some propositions, and from those propositions it logically follows that the tree is not mm inches tall, I know that the tree is not mm inches tall.

In a realistic example, an upper bound would be imposed on mm, if I am not to know infinitely many propositions.

The paradoxical reasoning is generated by the combination of (3+) and (4+) with the KK principle. It can be reformulated as appealing directly to mathematical induction rather than to the least number principle. I certainly know that the tree is not 0 inches tall. But if I know that the tree is not m−1m-1 inches tall, the KK principle says that I know that I know that it is not m−1m-1 inches tall; so by (3+) I know the premises of a valid deductive argument whose conclusion is that the tree is not mm inches tall; so by (4+) I know that the tree is not mm inches tall. By induction, for every natural number mm I know that the tree is not mm inches tall. That is impossible, for the tree is some number of inches tall. In the original case, I do not know that the tree is not 600 inches tall. This upper bound means that strictly speaking the appeal to mathematical induction can be dispensed with; the appeal to the least number principle was equally unnecessary. However, the principles conveniently label the difference in flavour between the two versions, and speed up both. What neither argument can dispense with is the KK principle. It is the culprit.

The failure of the KK principle is not news. However, the standard counterexamples involve knowing subjects who lack the concept of knowledge or have not reflected on their knowledge and therefore do not know that they know. The present case is quite different. It concerns a subject who has the concept of knowledge and has reached reflective equilibrium with respect to the propositions at issue. Still I know without knowing that I know.

Our knowledge is riddled with failures of the KK principle, for it is riddled with inexactness. The problem about the tree could be duplicated with respect to almost any case of sense perception, for almost any such case gives inexact knowledge about measurable quantities. To the informed observer, hearing gives some knowledge about loudness in decibels and touch about heat in degrees Fahrenheit. When I smell the milk I have inexact knowledge of the number of minutes since it was opened; when I taste the tea I have inexact knowledge of how many grains of sugar were put in. The point generalizes to knowledge from sources beyond present perception, such as memory and testimony. This is partly because they pass on inexact knowledge originally derived from past perception, mine or another’s, partly because they add further inexactness of their own. How long was one’s last walk in steps? How long was someone else’s walk, described to one as “quite long”? In each case the possible answers lie on a scale, which can be divided so finely that if a given answer is in fact correct, one does not know that its neighbouring answers are not correct, and one can know that one’s powers of discrimination have that limit. The argument then proceeds as in the case of the distant tree.

The aim of §2 is to describe a model of inexact knowledge on which its failure to provide the KK principle looks utterly natural.

2. Margins for error

Suppose that, in the situation described above, the tree is ii inches tall and I have the true belief that it is not jj inches tall. If the difference between ii and jj is too small, then even if the tree had been jj inches tall, I might easily still have believed that it was not jj inches tall—it is not true that if the tree had been jj inches tall, I would not have believed that it was not jj inches tall. For my eyesight and ability to judge heights are limited. But then my true belief that the tree is not jj inches tall is not reliably true; it is too risky to constitute knowledge. On the other hand, if the difference between ii and jj is large enough, and my belief is formed in a normal way, then it may well be true that if the tree had been jj inches tall, I would not have believed that it was not jj inches tall. My true belief may be reliably true, and constitute knowledge.4 Other things being equal, I know that the tree is not jj inches tall if and only if the difference between ii and jj is large enough. How large is large enough? That depends on the circumstances: my eyesight and ability to judge heights, the distance, the quality of the light.

Where our knowledge is inexact, our beliefs are reliable only if we leave a margin for error. The belief that a general condition obtains in a particular case has a margin for error if the condition also obtains in all similar cases. The degree and kind of the required similarity depend on the circumstances. For given epistemic capacities, reliability increases with the width of the margin. The more accurate the epistemic capacities, the narrower is the margin needed to achieve a given level of reliability. Since a belief constitutes knowledge only if it is reliable enough, the belief that a general condition obtains in a particular case constitutes knowledge only if the condition obtains in all cases similar enough in the relevant respect to achieve the required level of reliability. Knowledge that the condition obtains is available only if it does obtain (whether knowably or not) in all similar cases. The margin for error principle says, vaguely but not trivially, that AA is true in all cases sufficiently similar to cases in which “It is known that AA” is true. If a proposition is true but there are sufficiently similar cases in which it is false, it is not available to be known. It cannot be known within its margin for error.

A special case of inexact knowledge is that in which the proposition AA is itself of the form “It is known that BB”. Just as we are not perfectly accurate judges of the heights of trees, so we are not perfectly accurate judges of the reliability of beliefs. When we substitute “It is known that BB” for “AA” in the margin for error principle, it says that if “It is known that it is known that BB” is true in a given case, “It is known that BB” is true in all sufficiently similar cases. The degree and kind of the required similarity depend on the circumstances, for example on one’s ability to judge reliability; “It is known that BB” and BB may need margins for error of different widths. If “It is known that BB” is true but there are sufficiently similar cases in which it is false, it is not available to be known. It cannot be known within its margin for error. The failure of the KK principle is a natural consequence of the margin for error principle.

Suppose that “It is known that it is known that BB” is true in a given case. By the margin for error principle for “It is known that BB”, the latter proposition is true in all cases similar to the given case. But then by the margin for error principle for BB, BB is true in all cases similar to cases similar to the given case. In effect, knowledge that one knows requires two margins for error. More generally, every iteration of knowledge widens the required margin. Any number of iterations of knowledge is possible in principle, but is available in a narrower range of cases than any lower number of iterations.

Believing is often compared to shooting at a target, the truth. The comparison is not quite apt, for the truth is a single point (the actual case), like a bullet, while the proposition believed is an area (a set of possible cases), like a target. Instead, the believer’s task may be conceived as drawing a boundary on a wall at which someone else has fired a bullet. The belief is true if the bullet hits the bounded area, false otherwise. If truth is a hit, knowledge is a safe hit. That is, the point of impact is within the bounded area and not so near its boundary that the bullet could very easily have landed outside (had a light breeze blown). For example, a hit might be safe just in case every point on the wall less than an inch from the point of impact is within the bounded area. The one-inch margin inside the boundary is the area in which BB is true but unknown; when this margin is removed, the remaining area is that in which BB is known.5 When another one-inch margin is removed, the result is the area in which BB is known to be known. The iteration of knowledge operators is a process of gradual erosion.6

An area lacks a margin only if no point in it is less than an inch from a point on the wall not in the area. Since any point on the wall can be reached from any other via a sequence of points each less than an inch from the next, if an area lacks a margin then either no points are in it or all points are in it. It is either the “null area”, corresponding to a contradiction, or the whole wall, corresponding to a tautology.7 A proposition available to be known whenever it is true is either logically true or logically false. A contingent proposition corresponds to a non-null area less than the whole wall and has a margin; such a proposition is true in some cases where it is not available to be known. Not only can any point on the wall be reached from any other via a sequence of points each less than an inch from the next: there is a finite bound to the number of points needed, since diagonally opposite corners of the wall are as far apart as any two points on it. Thus any area less than the whole wall is reduced to nothing by a finite number of removals of one-inch margins. This corresponds to the claim that for any proposition other than a logical truth there is a finite bound to the number of iterations of knowledge one can have of it—a mildly sceptical result.

The remarks in the last paragraph should be treated with more than usual caution, for they depend on the specific very simple layout imagined. If the wall were divided down the middle by a partition at right angles to it, so that there was no danger of a bullet fired on one side of the partition landing on the other, the half of the wall on one side of the partition would have no margin, and would therefore correspond to a contingent proposition available to be known whenever true. If the wall were infinitely long, with no partition, any two points on it would still be a finite distance apart, but the half of the wall on one side of an arbitrary vertical line would not be reduced to nothing by any finite number of removals of one-inch margins; it would correspond to a contingent proposition of which one could have any finite number of iterations of knowledge. The constant width of the margin is another simplification. If it were draughtier to the left than to the right, making the bullet’s flight less predictable on that side, a wider margin would be needed there. Our epistemic capacities are more accurate in some areas than in others.

Such qualifications could be multiplied, but the point of a model is to omit all but a few of the original’s complications. What has been sketched is a picture of inexact knowledge in which the systematic failure of the KK principle is utterly natural. It is to be expected not just because we are not perfectly reflective, but because however reflective we are, our epistemic capacities are not perfectly accurate.

3. The Surprise Examination

The Distant Tree and the Surprise Examination stand at diagonally opposite corners of a two-dimensional array of cases.8

#1.
A teacher’s pupils know that she rings all and only examination dates on the calendar in her office. The only knowledge they have of examination dates this term comes from a distant glimpse of the calendar, enough to see that one and only one date is ringed and that it is not very near the end of term but not to narrow it down much more than that. The pupils recognize their situation. They know now, at the beginning of term, that if the examination is mm days from the end of term, they do not know now that it will not be m−1m-1 days from the end (mm from 0 to the number of days in term).9 In particular, they know that if it is on the penultimate day, they do not know now that it will not be on the last day. But they do know now from their glimpse of the calendar that it will not be on the last day. They deduce that it will not be on the penultimate day. They also know that if it is on the antepenultimate day, they do not know that it will not be on the penultimate day. They deduce that it will not be on the antepenultimate day. And so on. They rule out every day of term as a possible date for the examination.

#2.
Like #1, but it is the school caretaker, not the pupils, who catches a glimpse of the calendar. He tells them that the ringed date is not very near the end of term.10 They know him to be a trustworthy observer and informant. In circumstances like these, reliable testimony is a channel for the communication of knowledge. Since the caretaker knows that the ringed date is not very near the end of term, the pupils know it too. They reason as in #1.

#3.
Like #2, but it is the teacher, not the caretaker, who gives the pupils information. She tells them just that the examination will not be very near the end of term. They know her to be a trustworthy informant. They reason as in #1.

#4.
Like #3, but what the teacher tells the pupils is that if the examination is mm days from the end of term, they do not know now that it will not be m−1m-1 days from the end (mm from 0 to the number of days in term). What the teacher says is true, for the date she has fixed is not very near the end of term, she is the pupils’ only source of information about the date, and what she has told them is only what they worked out for themselves in #1-#3: if it is mm days from the end, they do not know now that it will not be m−1m-1 days from the end, for they did not know that in #3, and they know no more about the date in #4 than they did in #3. The teacher knows all this, so she knows the truth of what she tells the pupils. As in #3, they know the truth of what she tells them. They reason as in #1.

##1.
Like #1, but the pupils reason slightly differently. They know now that if the examination is mm days from the end of term, they will not know then, on the morning of the examination, that it will not be m−1m-1 days from the end, for they know: it will not be very near the end of term; on no day not very near the end of term will their glimpse of the calendar and their memory of examinationless days enable them to know that it will not be the day after; they will not acquire further relevant information between now and then (by chance there has been a general tightening of school security). In particular, they know that if it is on the penultimate day, they will not know then that it will not be on the last day. But they will know that then, for they will remember their glimpse of the calendar. They deduce that it will not be on the penultimate day. They also know that if it is on the antepenultimate day, they will not know then that it will not be on the penultimate day. But they will remember the previous conclusion. They deduce that it will not be on the antepenultimate day. And so on. Their conclusion is as in #1.

##2.
The pupils’ source is as in #2; they reason as in ##1.

##3.
The pupils’ source is as in #3; they reason as in ##1.

##4.
The teacher tells the pupils what in ##1-##3 they worked out for themselves, that if the examination is mm days from the end, they will not know then that it will not be m−1m-1 days from the end, just as in #4 she tells them what in #1-#3 they worked out for themselves; they reason as in ##1.

###1.
Like ##1, but the knowledge the pupils use is that they will not know on the morning of the examination that it will be on that day (for, as in ##1, they will not know that it will not be the day after). They reason as in ##1, except that they need the additional premise that on the morning of the examination they will remember that it was not on any earlier day.

###2.
The pupils’ source is as in #2; they reason as in ###1.

###3.
The pupils’ source is as in #3; they reason as in ###1.

###4.
The teacher tells the pupils what in ###1-###3 they worked out for themselves (that they will not know on the morning of the examination that it will be on that day). They reason as in ###1.

#1 has exactly the same structure as the inductive version of the Distant Tree; ###4 is the usual Surprise Examination. They differ in two dimensions. The source of knowledge in #1-###1 is perception; in #3-###3 and #4-###4 it is testimony, with #2-###2 as mixed cases. The reasoning in #1-#4 concerns present knowledge; in ##1-##4 and ###1-###4 it concerns future knowledge. Nevertheless, the pupils’ reasoning is unsound in every case, and the cases are similar enough to make this unlikely to be mere coincidence. A common error should be sought. More specifically, the pupils’ reasoning does not seem to get any better as one moves from #1 to ###4; if they commit a fallacy in #1, they commit an analogous fallacy in ###4. ##1-##4 and ###1-###4 are the more complex cases, for the pupils are reasoning about future knowledge on the basis of present knowledge. The gap between what they know at the beginning of term and what they know later leaves room for mistakes which are not at issue in #1-#4, where the pupils reason about present knowledge on the basis of present knowledge. However, their reasoning is unsound in the simpler cases too: their error there is unlikely to have been corrected in the more complex ones. Thus any diagnosis of one or more of ##1-##4 and ###1-###4 which does not extend to #1-#4 is, although perhaps correct as far as it goes, presumably incomplete, not having identified the common error. Since #1 has the structure of the Distant Tree and ###4 is the Surprise Examination, any adequate diagnosis of the Surprise Examination should extend to the Distant Tree.

Some diagnoses of the Surprise Examination depend on the gap between what the pupils know at the beginning of term and what they know later, or at least on the epistemic possibility of such a gap.11 Now some variant paradoxes with the same structure concern what different people know at the same time, rather than what the same people know at different times, so the diagnosis is best generalized as depending on any gap between cognitive standpoints.12 The underlying point is the same. In ruling out a last day examination, the pupils assume that they will still know on the last morning that there will be a surprise examination. But even if they know that at the beginning of term, they could lose the knowledge later.13 The point is not that memory is fallible; the pupils may be assumed to know that they will not forget the teacher’s announcement. Rather, their memory of examinationless days would undermine their earlier knowledge of the truth of the announcement, like misleading evidence. For them, to know on the last day that there will be a surprise examination, when there has been none so far, is in effect to know “There will be an examination tomorrow and we do not know that there will be an examination tomorrow”. Such knowledge is impossible, for their knowledge of the first conjunct is inconsistent with the truth of the second. Thus if the examination is on the last day, the pupils will have lost their knowledge of the truth of the teacher’s announcement by the last morning; moreover, they can know this conditional in advance. Thus the reasoning by which they rule out a last day examination is unsound, for it assumes that knowledge will be retained in trying to refute a supposition on which it would not be retained.

The foregoing diagnosis can be elaborated in a variety of ways. There is clearly something to it. Nevertheless, it is incomplete. It yields no objection to the reasoning in the Distant Tree, which is an equally unsound simplification of the reasoning in the Surprise Examination. What is wrong with the former is wrong with the latter yet unmentioned in the diagnosis.

The analogy with the Distant Tree reinforces other points about the Surprise Examination. The teacher’s announcement corresponds to the claim that if the tree is mm inches tall, I do not know that it is not m−1m-1 inches tall (for relevant mm). Thus to say that the announcement is false or truth-valueless corresponds to saying that the description of my inexact knowledge is false or truth-valueless; to say that the pupils cannot know in advance that the announcement is true corresponds to saying that I cannot know that the description of my inexact knowledge is true. The obvious implausibility of these claims for the Distant Tree points up their implausibility for the Surprise Examination too. Advance knowledge that there will be a test, firedrill or the like, and one will not know in advance when, is an everyday fact of institutional life, but one denied by a surprising proportion of early work on the Surprise Examination. Who has not waited for a telephone call, knowing that it will ring within the next week and that one will not know a second before it rings that it will ring in the next second? Any adequate diagnosis of the Surprise Examination should allow the pupils to know that there will be a surprise examination.

Other points about the Distant Tree can be extended to the Surprise Examination. For example, the problem does not depend on self-reference or ungroundedness in the teacher’s announcement or the pupils’ knowledge of it. For the problem in the Distant Tree is not of that kind. Of course, a viciously self-referential twist can be given to the teacher’s announcement; that does not mean that it must be. As noted above, claims like the teacher’s are often true, and known to be so; any self-reference in them must be virtuous. The Distant Tree is not a Liar paradox, nor is the Surprise Examination on its natural reading.14 Again, that vagueness is not to blame is even clearer in the Surprise Examination than in the Distant Tree.15

After these negative remarks, the question is whether the diagnosis of the Distant Tree offered above generalizes to the Unexpected Examination. The argument in the Distant Tree relies on the KK principle for inexact knowledge; a model of such knowledge was provided on which it characteristically fails to satisfy the KK principle. Now although the argument in the Unexpected Examination can be reconstructed as using the KK principle, it need not be.16 A careful analysis shows that what is really needed is the assumption that the pupils know on the first morning of term that they will know on the second morning that… they will know on the penultimate morning that they will know on the last morning the truth of the teacher’s announcement. Similar iterations of knowledge are needed of the propositions that they will continue to be rational (deduce and come to know relevant consequences of what they know) and remember (know) on each morning before the examination that there has been no examination so far. Since each iteration of knowledge involves a change in cognitive standpoint, the KK principle—which involves a fixed cognitive standpoint—is not itself very relevant. However, the objection to the KK principle in §2 was that the iteration of knowledge operators leads sooner or later to falsity through a process of erosion resulting from the need for margins for error. This objection applies just as much when the knowledge operators refer to different cognitive standpoints. If anything, it applies with more force: knowledge of future knowledge is usually less exact than knowledge of present knowledge, so wider margins for error are needed, and the process of erosion is correspondingly faster. The same point applies when the cognitive standpoints vary in other respects: my knowledge of your knowledge is usually less exact than my knowledge of my knowledge, for example. Thus §2’s diagnosis of the Distant Tree does generalize to the Surprise Examination.

The diagnosis also passes the test that it should allow the pupils to know the truth of the teacher’s announcement. Once the iteration of knowledge operators is restrained, it becomes consistent to suppose that the pupils know at the beginning of term that there will be a surprise examination. If it were inconsistent, it would remain so if one ignored the distinction between what the pupils know at the beginning of term and what they know later: but it is provably consistent in the latter case. This result can be strengthened in various ways (see Appendix).17 The pupils are more than one margin for error away from cases in which the teacher’s announcement is false, so can know it to be true, but they are within a finite number of margins of such cases, so the number of iterations of knowledge they can have is limited.

It might be objected that the pupils can be idealized to the point where they do not need margins for error. Formally, “the pupils know that” might be treated as an operator for provability from a stock of assumptions. Under this idealization, the KK principle holds. However, the paradox then loses much of its interest. In everyday cases, the pupils know that there will be a surprise examination, and this is what must be explained in the face of an apparent proof of its impossibility. In the idealized cases in which the KK principle holds, there is no presumption that the pupils can know that there is to be a surprise examination, so there is nothing to be explained.18 The paradox of the Distant Tree disappears in the same way under the assumption that I have perfect eyesight.

The inexactness of the pupils’ knowledge is the message of the Surprise Examination, not distracting noise. Some evidence for this is that the intuitive datum is not all-or-nothing but a matter of degree. Most people are simply confused by the one-day version of the paradox, “There will be an examination today and you do not know it”. The more days are involved, the clearer it is that the pupils can know the truth of the teacher’s announcement. One can explain this effect by supposing that a small difference between actual pupils and idealized ones is magnified by each stage of the reasoning until it is clearly visible. This is the difference between inexact and exact knowledge, the margin for error.

The Surprise Examination is closely related to a number of paradoxes in decision theory.19 The most prominent is Iterated Prisoner’s Dilemma. Suppose that it is common knowledge for two agents that they are rational and face a series of ten thousand prisoner’s dilemmas. Orthodox game theory “proves” by a backward induction argument that they will never cooperate, for it is rational to cooperate at round ii only if it is not then common knowledge that there is no round after ii at which it is rational to cooperate (defection dominates cooperation unless the latter makes future cooperation more probable). The result is highly unrealistic: ordinarily reasonable players are likely to cooperate for all but the last few rounds. This is not the place to analyse such paradoxes in detail. However, they all seem amenable to the present approach. In every case the argument assumes something like common knowledge of the agents’ rationality. If it is common knowledge for two agents that they are rational, both know that they are rational, both know that both know it, both know that both know that both know it, and so on. A standard game-theoretic backward induction argument invokes a further iteration of this knowledge for each round of the game. But any players we can envisage have less than perfectly accurate epistemic capacities, and know only if they leave a margin for error. The usual erosion continues until falsity is reached after finitely many iterations of “both know that”. Real agents lack strict common knowledge of their rationality. The inexactness of their knowledge permits ordinarily reasonable players to cooperate in Iterated Prisoner’s Dilemma.20

4. Existential assumptions

The Surprise Examination and Iterated Prisoner’s Dilemma differ slightly in structure. The pupils’ argument assumes that there will be an examination on some day; if they did not assume this, a surprise examination could easily be held on the last day. The backward induction argument in Iterated Prisoner’s Dilemma has no need to make the corresponding assumption: that the players will cooperate on some round. The purpose of this section is to eliminate the existential assumption from the Surprise Examination, and with it attempted solutions which treat it as essential.21

Consider first the Distant Tree. Its existential assumption is that the tree is some number of inches tall. However, this assumption is inessential to the argument. Suppose that I lose sight of the tree and notice tree-fellers at work. The tree is in danger of being felled. I do not know whether it still exists. If it does not, count every proposition of the form “The tree is mm inches tall” as simply false. I still know that if the tree is mm inches tall, I do not know that it is not the case that the tree is m−1m-1 inches tall. Given the KK principle, the inductive argument in §1 still “shows” for any number mm that if I know that it is not the case that the tree is 0 inches tall, I know that it is not the case that the tree is mm inches tall. But I do know that it is not the case that the tree is 0 inches tall, for I know that if it still exists it is much taller than that. According to the argument, I know that it is not the case that the tree is 600 inches tall. That is absurd. For all I know, the tree still exists and is 600 inches tall. The paradox is independent of the existential assumption.

Now consider #1, the first variant of the Surprise Examination. Suppose that the teacher is known to ring all examination dates and her family’s birthdays on the calendar. The pupils do not know whether the ring they glimpsed is round an examination date or a birthday. As before, their glimpse is enough to see that one and only one date is ringed and that it is not very near the end of term but not to narrow it down much more than that. They still know now that if there is an examination mm days from the end of term, they do not know now that there will be no examination m−1m-1 days from the end. They reason as before, concluding that there will be no examination. That is absurd. For all they know, the ring on the calendar indicates an examination not very near the end of term. The paradox in #1 does not depend on the existential assumption.

Similar remarks apply to the other variants of the Surprise Examination. Suffice it to eliminate the existential assumption from ###4, the standard version. Call an examination conditionally expected if on the morning of it the pupils know that if there is an examination at all, it will be that day. The teacher announces just that there will be no conditionally expected examination. The pupils argue from this that there will be no examination at all. If there has been no examination by the last morning, the pupils will certainly then know “If there is an examination at all, it will be today.”22 Thus a last day examination would be conditionally expected, and so will not occur. The pupils will know this on the penultimate morning, so if there has been no examination by then, they will then know “If there is an examination at all, it will be today”. Thus a penultimate day examination would be conditionally expected, and so will not occur. And so on. The pupils conclude that there will be no examination. That is absurd. For all they know, there will be a not conditionally expected examination not very near the end of term.

The Surprise Examination does not depend on the existential assumption; indeed, it is a better paradox without it. The original argument is in effect a reductio ad absurdum (relative to background assumptions) of the supposition that the pupils know on the first morning that they know on the second morning that… they know on the last morning that there will be an unexpected examination. It would be obviously irrational to treat such an argument as justifying confidence that there will not be an examination. The new argument uses the supposition that the pupils know on the first morning that they know on the second morning that… they know on the last morning that there will be no conditionally expected examination to show that there will be no examination at all. This does not contradict the supposition; if there is no examination, the teacher’s announcement is vacuously fulfilled. If the teacher and the pupils’ memory and rationality are sufficiently reliable, why should they not treat the argument as justifying confidence that there will be no examination? It would be a quite inadequate response to remind the pupils that if there is an examination on the antepenultimate day (for example), they do not know on the first morning that they know on the second morning that… they know on the last morning that there will be no conditionally expected examination: why should they not treat the argument as justifying confidence that the antecedent of that conditional is false?23

The point can be seen from the pupils’ perspective. In the original version, the passage of day after day with no examination gradually undermines the pupils’ justification for believing the teacher’s announcement. It does no such thing in the new version. That there will be no examination looks increasingly likely: but this is the increasing likelihood of one way for the teacher’s announcement to be fulfilled. As day after day passes with no examination, the pupils appear to remain justified in believing the teacher’s announcement.

Consider for instance the pupils’ situation on the penultimate morning with no examination so far. The teacher’s announcement now reduces to “There will be no conditionally expected examination tomorrow and there will be no conditionally expected examination today”. Since any examination tomorrow will be conditionally expected for the trivial reason that no examination can be held after the end of term, the first conjunct boils down to “There will be no examination tomorrow”. An examination today is conditionally expected just in case the pupils know that if there is an examination at all, it will be today, which in the circumstances is just for them to know that there will be no examination tomorrow; thus the second conjunct boils down to “Either there will be no examination today or we do not know that there will be no examination tomorrow”. In the circumstances, the pupils know the truth of the teacher’s announcement if and only if they know:

(!)
There will be no examination tomorrow and either there will be no examination today or we do not know that there will be no examination tomorrow.

Suppose that the pupils know (!). Thus they know its first conjunct; they know that there will be no examination tomorrow. Moreover, the second conjunct of (!) is true; either there will be no examination today or they do not know that there will be no examination tomorrow. By disjunctive syllogism, there will be no examination today. Thus if there is an examination today, the pupils do not know (!) and therefore do not know the truth of the teacher’s announcement. However, (!) is not intrinsically unknowable; it follows logically from the straightforward conjunction “There will be no examination tomorrow and there will be no examination today”.24 If the pupils knew the latter, they would know (!) and therefore the truth of the teacher’s announcement. It is not illogical for the pupils to suppose themselves to know (!) in the way in which it would be illogical for them to suppose that they know “There will be an examination today and we do not know that there will be an examination today” on the last morning of the original Surprise Examination.

Suppose that the very reliable teacher announces “There will be no conditionally expected examination” at the beginning of a two-day “term” during which there is in fact no examination. It is quite coherent to suppose that at the beginning of term the pupils know that they know the truth of the announcement, know that they know (!), and therefore know that there will be no examination. Nor is any incoherence introduced when the number of days in term and the number of iterations of knowledge is increased, provided that there is no examination. It just becomes ever more implausible that the pupils have the ever greater number of iterations of knowledge of the truth of the announcement needed to rule out its non-vacuous truth. This is precisely the erosion effect predicted by the margin for error model of inexact knowledge.

Consider again the Distant Tree. Here the new version of the argument shows that if I have enough iterations of knowledge of instances of (3+) and (4+) and of the proposition that if the tree still exists it is between 0 and 6,000 inches tall, then the tree does not exist and I know it. But even if it does not exist, I cannot know that merely by reflecting on the limitations of my eyesight and ability to judge heights. So I cannot have that many iterations of knowledge of those propositions. In this case, the non-existence of the tree makes no more iterations of knowledge available to me than were already available when I knew that it existed. This is because the source of my knowledge is independent of the continued existence of the tree. In the Surprise Examination, by contrast, the source of the pupils’ knowledge is the teacher who determines whether there will be an examination: if there is no examination because of her reliability, more iterations of knowledge may be available to the pupils. This difference is quite compatible with the margin for error model.

5. The Heap

Although inexact knowledge can be of precise propositions, an account of it might be expected to have something to say about vagueness. Only the briefest indication will be given here.

“Heap” is a vague term, and our knowledge of how many grains make a heap is inexact.25 Thus knowledge that mm grains make a heap requires a margin for error. If we know that mm grains make a heap, m−1m-1 grains should make a heap (whether we know it or not). It follows that we cannot know any conjunction of the form “mm grains make a heap and m−1m-1 grains do not make a heap”, for our knowledge of the first conjunct is incompatible with the truth of the second. Thus we cannot locate a cut-off point for “heap”. This explanation does not assume that “heap” has a cut-off point but, more importantly, it does not assume that it lacks one. My ignorance of the height of the tree in inches can be explained in the same way. That “heap” has no cut-off point cannot be established merely by pointing out that we cannot locate one. Of course, I have an idea how to find out exactly how many inches tall the tree is; I have no idea how to find out exactly how many grains make a heap. My knowledge of heaps seems necessarily inexact. But it still does not follow that “heap” has no cut-off point. The margin for error model makes available, though not compulsory, the idea that vagueness depends on the unlocatability of cut-off points, not on their non-existence.26

The principle “If we know that mm grains make a heap, m−1m-1 grains make a heap” might be thought to generate a sorites paradox, not for “heap” but for “known heap”. If we know (by philosophical reflection) that if we know that mm grains make a heap, m−1m-1 grains make a heap, does it not follow that if we know that mm grains make a heap, we know that m−1m-1 grains make a heap? Since we know that 10,000 grains make a heap, it would follow by 9,999 applications of modus ponens that we know that one grain makes a heap, which it does not. However, this is just a descending variant of the Distant Tree. In order to know that m−1m-1 grains make a heap, we should have to know the premises from which we deduced “m−1m-1 grains make a heap”. They are “If we know that mm grains make a heap, m−1m-1 grains make a heap” and “We know that mm grains make a heap”. Thus we should have to know that we know that mm grains make a heap. With the KK principle, a sorites paradox would indeed be forthcoming. Without it, one iteration of knowledge is lost at each step of the argument. For any reasonable number of iterations at the start, the argument runs out of steam before reaching a false conclusion (see Appendix).

6. Inexact beliefs

The Distant Tree, the Surprise Examination, Iterated Prisoner’s Dilemma and the Heap have all been treated as problems about knowledge. However, similar problems can be raised about rationality, belief, and the like.

I do not only know that if the tree is mm inches tall, I do not know that it is not m−1m-1 inches tall. Given my evidence, it is rational for me to believe that if the tree is mm inches tall, it is not rational for me to believe that it is not m−1m-1 inches tall. Since I take myself to be fairly rational about the tree, I believe that if it is mm inches tall, I do not believe that it is not m−1m-1 inches tall. Arguments parallel to those in §1 can be given in terms of these concepts. For each mm, they appear to show that it is rational for me to believe that the tree is not mm inches tall, and that I do so believe. In contrast to the case of knowledge, those conclusions are not shown to be absurd merely by the fact that the tree is mm inches tall for some mm: there is no false knowledge but there are false rational beliefs. Even so, those conclusions are absurd. Since the tree may easily be 600 inches tall, it is obviously not rational for me to believe that it is not 600 inches tall, and I do not so believe.

Similar remarks apply to the Surprise Examination and Iterated Prisoner’s Dilemma, which often are formulated in terms of rational belief rather than knowledge. The rest of the discussion will focus on rational belief, since the actual or believed irrationality of the subject tends to defuse the paradoxes. For the Heap, it is rational for us to believe that if it is rational for us to believe that mm grains make a heap, m−1m-1 grains make a heap. Suppose that it is also rational for us to believe that mm grains make a heap and m−1m-1 grains do not make a heap. We could then deduce: mm grains make a heap and it is not rational for us to believe that mm grains make a heap. But we cannot have a rational true belief in that conjunction, for the rationality of our belief in the first conjunct would be incompatible with the truth of the second. Since rational belief aims at truth, it presumably cannot be rational for us to believe that conjunction. This refutes the supposition that it is rational for us to believe that mm grains make a heap and m−1m-1 grains do not make a heap. We cannot have a rational belief as to the exact location of the cut-off point for “heap”. Although the argument is less crisp than the one to show that we cannot know the exact location, it is nearly as effective.

Inexactness raises almost the same problems for rational belief as for knowledge. The factiveness of knowledge sometimes provides a shortcut in the argument, but there remains a longer way round for non-factive attitudes. Thus a plausible diagnosis of the paradoxes for knowledge should generalize to a diagnosis of the paradoxes for rational belief. §1 blocked the former by denying the KK principle. Technically, it is easy to block the latter by denying the corresponding “RR” principle for rational belief: that if it is rational to believe AA, it is rational to believe that it is rational to believe that AA. The question is whether the explanation of the falsity of the KK principle generalizes to an explanation of the falsity of the RR principle. How, in the relevant cases, can it be rational to believe AA yet not rational to believe that it is rational to believe AA?

The explanation of the falsity of the KK principle in the relevant cases is the margin for error model of inexact knowledge. As it stands, this model is applicable only to factive attitudes. Roughly, SS’s XXing in case cc that AA leaves a margin for error just if AA is true in all cases very like cc; since cc is very like itself, SS’s XXing in case cc that AA leaves a margin for error only if AA is true in case cc. But it can be rational to believe AA even when it is false; some evidence is misleading. As it happens, the non-factiveness of rational belief does not seem to make very much difference to the problems at issue. In the Distant Tree and the Heap, for instance, it may be both rational to believe only what one is in a position to know and rational to believe whatever one is in a position to know. But to find theoretical space for non-factiveness is more interesting.

The simplest way to extend the margin for error model to non-factive attitudes is to allow SS’s XXing in case cc that AA to leave a probabilistic margin for error just if AA holds in most cases very like cc. Even if AA holds in most cases very like cc, it may not hold in cc itself. If XXing leaves a probabilistic margin for error, in place of factiveness one has the weaker principle that if SS XXs that AA, SS does not XX that not AA: for if AA is true in most cases very like cc, it is not true that in most cases very like cc it is false. Of course, one is more likely to be interested in a continuum of possible cases like cc than in the miscellaneous collection of actual ones; “most” of such an infinite set is a measure-theoretic notion. However, one can get a feel for the idea by considering a simple model in which the numbers are finite. It is not intended to be more realistic than the wall in §2.

Let the possible cases form a two-dimensional infinite grid; each case is given by a pair of coordinates ⟨x,y⟩\langle x,y\rangle, where xx and yy are integers. The points “very near” ⟨x,y⟩\langle x,y\rangle are just those within one step on the grid: ⟨x,y⟩\langle x,y\rangle itself, ⟨x+1,y⟩\langle x+1,y\rangle, ⟨x−1,y⟩\langle x-1,y\rangle, ⟨x,y+1⟩\langle x,y+1\rangle and ⟨x,y−1⟩\langle x,y-1\rangle. “Most” of these five points are in a set just in case at least four are. “Propositions” are sets of points; a proposition is true at a point just in case the latter is in the former. If AA is a proposition, the point ⟨x,y⟩\langle x,y\rangle is in the proposition RARA just in case most of the points very near ⟨x,y⟩\langle x,y\rangle are in AA; that is, RARA is true at a point if and only if AA is true at most very near points. RARA represents the proposition that it is rational to believe AA, on the massively idealized assumption that rational belief is governed only by the need to leave probabilistic margins for error. Let AA be the proposition {⟨0,1⟩,⟨1,0⟩,⟨1,2⟩,⟨2,1⟩}\{\langle0,1\rangle,\langle1,0\rangle,\langle1,2\rangle,\langle2,1\rangle\}. The only point very near at least four members of AA is ⟨1,1⟩\langle1,1\rangle, so RARA is {⟨1,1⟩}\{\langle1,1\rangle\}. No point is very near at least four members of RARA, so RRARRA is {}\{\}. Thus ⟨1,1⟩\langle1,1\rangle is a point at which it is rational to believe AA, but AA is not true, nor is it rational to believe that it is rational to believe AA. It illustrates the simultaneous breakdown of factiveness and the RR principle. More generally, RR is subject to erosion effects in the model. For example, if AA is any finite set, RkAR^kA (kk iterations of RR on AA) is empty for some number kk.27

On the present model, what it is rational to believe is not fully closed under logical consequence. For single premise inferences there is no problem. If AA entails BB, whenever AA is true at a point BB is too, so AA is a subset of BB, so RARA is a subset of RBRB, so whenever RARA is true at a point RBRB is too, so RARA entails RBRB. It is rational to believe whatever logically follows from a proposition it is rational to believe.28 However, it may not be rational to believe whatever logically follows from two or more propositions each of which it is rational to believe, for it may not be rational to believe their conjunction. For example, let A1A_1 be {⟨0,1⟩,⟨1,0⟩,⟨1,2⟩,⟨2,1⟩}\{\langle0,1\rangle,\langle1,0\rangle,\langle1,2\rangle,\langle2,1\rangle\} and A2A_2 be {⟨1,0⟩,⟨1,1⟩,⟨1,2⟩,⟨2,1⟩}\{\langle1,0\rangle,\langle1,1\rangle,\langle1,2\rangle,\langle2,1\rangle\}. Both RA1RA_1 and RA2RA_2 are {⟨1,1⟩}\{\langle1,1\rangle\}. The conjunction A1&A2A_1\mathbin{\&}A_2 is the intersection of A1A_1 and A2A2, {⟨1,0⟩,⟨1,2⟩,⟨2,1⟩}\{\langle1,0\rangle,\langle1,2\rangle,\langle2,1\rangle\}; since this has only three members, R(A1&A2)R(A_1\mathbin{\&}A_2) is {}\{\}. Thus it is rational to believe each of A1A_1 and A2A_2 at ⟨1,1⟩\langle1,1\rangle, but it is not rational to believe their conjunction.29 Not surprisingly, the logic of RR is like that of the operator “It is at least 80% probable that”: if AA entails BB, “It is at least 80% probable that AA” entails “It is at least 80% probable that BB”, but “It is at least 80% probable that A1A_1 and it is at least 80% probable that A2A_2” does not entail “It is at least 80% probable that A1A_1 and A2A_2”. Just as RARA entails ¬R¬A\neg R\neg A, so “It is at least 80% probable that AA” entails “It is not at least 80% probable that not AA”.

The non-closure of rational belief opens a new gap in the analogue for it of the paradoxical reasoning about the Distant Tree. Corresponding to (4) is the assumption that what it is rational for me to believe is closed under the inference from “If the tree is nn inches tall, it is not rational for me to believe that the tree is not n−1n-1 inches tall” and “It is rational for me to believe that the tree is not n−1n-1 inches tall” to “The tree is not nn inches tall”; but this is just the kind of two-premise inference for which closure fails. However, this point does not undermine the point that the RR principle fails, for they are consequences of the very same model of rational belief.

What have been offered are models, not analyses, of inexact knowledge and belief. They are intended to prescind from many of the complexities of these notions. What they suggest is that a wide variety of cognitive phenomena can be unified and understood through the concept of a margin for error.

This is not to say that KTB is a plausible logic for knowledge in general (it is not), only that the objections to it stem from features of the concept of knowledge additional to those considered in this paper.

A feature of both KT and KTB is that A→KAA\rightarrow KA is a theorem (if and) only if either ¬A\neg A is a theorem or AA is. This corresponds to the idea mentioned in the text that a proposition is available to be known whenever it is true (if and) only if it is either logically true or logically false. See Williamson (1992a) for further details.
