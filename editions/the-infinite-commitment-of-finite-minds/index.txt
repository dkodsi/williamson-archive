# The Infinite Commitment of Finite Minds

Timothy Williamson

Canadian Journal of Philosophy 14 (2) (1984): 235–255.

Chomsky (in syntax) and Davidson (in semantics) have made much of the constraint that speakers’ competence must have a finite base. This base is often supposed to mean a finite axiomatization of beliefs. Section I shows why this is plausible. Section II shows why it is wrong. Section III shows why the semantic constraint is thereby trivialized.1

# I

‘Finite minds cannot have infinitely many beliefs’ has been taken for a useful truism. A theory of meaning, say, for a language may, for each of its infinitely many sentences, attribute to competent speakers knowledge, in some sense, and so in a corresponding sense belief, about the meaning (or some substitute for it) of that sentence. Our truism seems to force this paradox to immediate and constructive resolution: as finite minds we have a finite view on our language that recognizably entails an infinity of propositions about it. Our beliefs form, as it were, the finitely many axioms of a deductive system with infinitely many theorems. Semantics is helpfully restricted to the narrower class of finitely axiomatized theories, giving a clearer idea of what the true ones must look like. We believe each axiom, but are merely committed to each theorem. Commitment is potential belief. Our beliefs are after all finite, but our commitments are infinite. This obvious line, I shall argue, quite misses the depth of the problems. The view of belief that grounds the truism also makes it useless.

Pretend, for argument’s sake, that ‘finite minds cannot have infinitely many beliefs’ yields a necessary or desirable constraint in linguistics. So it must be truistic, not just true. For if it is not obviously true, linguistic theories flouting it cannot justifiably be ruled, a priori, out of court. What view of belief ensures and does not merely allow that finite minds cannot have infinitely many beliefs?

My having a given belief is (naively) a property of my mind: but a finitely complex object can have infinitely many properties. An indivisible particle is finitely complex if anything is, yet has for each positive number $n$ the property that the number of its divisible parts is not $n$. These infinitely many properties are all distinct from each other in the strong sense, perhaps applicable to beliefs, of logical independence. They all follow from the property of lacking divisible parts: similarly, a finite mind with infinitely many beliefs would doubtless have some (perhaps conjunctive) property from which all the properties of having those beliefs would follow, but this need not be the property of having some (perhaps conjunctive) belief from which all those beliefs would follow. For instance, an infinite conjunction of dispositions to judge true is not a disposition to judge an infinite conjunction true. However, a finitely complex mind has in some sense just finitely many features: so of infinitely many beliefs, two (indeed infinitely many) are held in virtue of the very same mental feature: which offends any sense that the mind does not just believe, but contains beliefs. If beliefs are contents of the mind in some ontologically robust sense, a mind with infinitely many beliefs is infinitely complex.

Now this view of belief is controversial. Leave aside the analysis of belief as inner or outer disposition to assent, for it is not what such linguistics require: competent lay speakers are disposed to assent neither to the general principles of linguistics nor to such of their instances as involve more than a trillion words – unless after lengthy training in which they acquire the disposition and so the belief.

However, there is a view2 on which, rather than beliefs existing one by one in the mind, a man has a whole system of belief (and desire) in virtue of that system’s making the most sense of his actions (including speech) as those of a rational agent. That those beliefs make more sense of him than any other system is not just evidence that they are his, but what it is for them to be his. If this suffices to ground the attribution of beliefs in reality, no reason seems left to deny the man, and indeed ourselves, infinitely many beliefs (not just commitments). That we cannot find them in ourselves would be mere introspective irrelevance; only a Cartesian would mention it. Why should an infinite system of beliefs not make more sense of my actions than any finite one? Grounds of simplicity cannot rule out the infinite: an infinite rectangular grid of stars seems simpler than the Milky Way. If someone accepts none of the infinitely many sequences of English words with the abstract feature $\mathrm{\phi}$ as grammatical but cannot recognize as true any proposition equivalent to ‘all $\mathrm{\phi}$ strings are ungrammatical,’ what is complicated about the explanation that they believe, of each of the infinitely many $\mathrm{\phi}$ strings, that it is ungrammatical, without believing the general proposition? Indeed, the apparatus needed to form the single belief equivalent to the infinite set itself squanders simplicity: for the single belief, unlike the members of the infinite set, involves the grammatical concept $\mathrm{\phi}$. Perhaps such infinite attributions could be falisified empirically: but of course that would not make the finiteness constraint obvious in the way required. Nor does the finite opportunity to acquire beliefs by learning and reasoning limit their number: for if these processes exist only as the explanation of action, why should not one sensation be brought under infinitely many concepts, or infinitely many reasoning processes coexist?

If one identifies beliefs with some physical entities or others, one may be able to limit their numbers. But this is to retreat from pure holistic interpretation. If reasons are causes they cannot outnumber causes: but then one cannot attribute more reasons than there were causes, however much sense of the man’s actions it would make. If reasons can outnumber causes, why cannot they do so infinitely?

What view of the mind comes nearest to guaranteeing that finite minds cannot have infinitely many beliefs? We still take the fact, if not the explanation, as moderately clear.

Perhaps our notion of actual belief involves the need of a vehicle: a belief must take some particular realization in the mind. The mind’s possession of this vehicle need not constitute belief: the vehicle may rather be a symbolic representation of the belief – in words, images or some internal code – whose meaning (like that of any other sign) is held in its context, not worn on its face. Here ‘meaning’ means both the content of the belief and the mind’s attitude towards it: its being the belief that $\mathrm{P}$ and neither the belief that $\mathrm{Q}$ nor the desire that $\mathrm{P}$. These vehicles might even be interpreted holistically: but the abstract proposition itself might take up residence in the mind, or the representation of the belief might dictate its own meaning. But, whether its interpretation is intrinsic or extrinsic, something mental must represent the belief that $\mathrm{P}$.3

Can two beliefs share a vehicle? A sign may have several meanings, but the closer analogue, what one means by a sign on a particular occasion, seems less subject to ambiguity. I may mean a pun both ways, but then the two meanings are quite distinct to me. So a vehicle may reasonably be assumed to represent just finitely many beliefs. Then the number of our beliefs is arguably finite. The number of relevantly different brain states is surely finite (though this waits on scientific investigation), so if the mental supervenes on the physical, the number of mental states and hence of vehicles is finite. That does not help the Cartesian, but introspection assures him directly that consciousness contains just finitely many vehicles (words, images or whatever). As for the unphysical unconscious (where ideas go when unperceived?), the more like another consciousness of which we are unconscious it is, the more reason we have to suppose it finite, and the less like it is the less reason we have to suppose it the kind of thing that has belief. So on almost any view the number of vehicles is finite. Since each vehicle represents just finitely many beliefs and every belief has some vehicle, beliefs are finite in number.

Such arguments have been contested4 on the grounds that, though momentary brain states may be finite, any length of time contains infinitely many moments, so a man’s life could contain infinitely many sequences of brain states: why should not these, rather than the momentary states, be the vehicles of belief? But if, as relativity theory says, messages in the brain cannot travel faster than the speed of light, mathematical considerations suggest that relevantly different brain states cannot follow each other at arbitrary speeds: hence the number of changes of state in a given brain possible in a thousand years would be limited, and just finitely many could occur in a man’s life. A similar objection in the case of consciousness is countered by the introspective observation that one never entertains a trillion conscious thoughts in a second – or, for the epiphenomenalist if not the Cartesian, by the previous case and the assumed supervenience of the mental on the physical. For if every conscious state corresponds to some brain state and no two conscious states correspond to the same brain state, and a man’s brain states are finite in number, so are his conscious states.

So we have an argument (if not one as convincing as its conclusion) that actual beliefs are finite in number. We can now reexamine our account of how infinite commitment is possible.

My finitely many actual beliefs entail, by laws of logic, infinitely many propositions, thereby committing me to them all. What if I reject one of those laws of logic? I would not think myself committed to a proposition derivable only by that law. If the law were valid, though, would I not simply be thinking falsely that I was not committed? Well, maybe I should have been committed because I should have accepted the law; maybe, even, on some sense I am committed. But a sense also exists on which I am not. For to add the proposition I could not previously have derived to my system seems to fill out what was incomplete, in a sense in which it would not were I already able to derive that proposition. What actions may be expected of me given my beliefs and desires? The practical inferences I might perform are at stake, not those a perfect, classical logician would. Surely we can treat as a thinking agent one who rejects some point of classical logic: we need not regard one who practices his anti-realism in everyday life as a mere creature of impulse. Now this narrower sense of commitment is still wide enough to allow an infinity of commitments. Assume classical logic correct, and consider an intuitionist. He accepts the classical law $\mathrm{A} \vdash \text{-}\text{-} \mathrm{A}$, but rejects the classical law $\text{-}\text{-} \mathrm{A} \vdash \mathrm{A}$. He may then accept some proposition $\text{-}\text{-} \mathrm{P}$ without accepting $\mathrm{P}$. He is committed to the infinity of propositions $\text{-}\text{-}\text{-}\text{-} \mathrm{P}$, $\text{-}\text{-}\text{-}\text{-}\text{-}\text{-} \mathrm{P}$, … in some sense far beyond that which commits him to $\mathrm{P}$. Maybe he should infer that $\mathrm{P}$, but to include it as a commitment would prevent that notion from illuminating his actual thought. An intuitionist will take his example from some yet weaker logic, or from classically invalid laws of intuitionist mathematics. Even if, as some have argued, the attribution of non-standard logic consitutes mistranslation, we shall still sometimes be interested in the classical consequences of a man’s beliefs because that is the logic he uses, not because it is the correct logic. In our sense, one is committed to those propositions one’s actual beliefs entail by rules of inference one actually accepts.

This suggests that if we cannot have infinitely many actual beliefs, we cannot actually accept infinitely many inference rules either. Indeed, one might argue that to accept an inference rule is to believe that its conclusion is true if its premises are: since one can have just finitely many such beliefs, one can accept just finitely many rules. That is fallacious, as Lewis Carroll observed.5 Without inference rules, axioms entail nothing. The belief that the conclusion is true if the premises are, is just another axiom. Given that $\mathrm{P}$, that if $\mathrm{P}$ then $\mathrm{Q}$ and that for any $\mathrm{A}$ and $\mathrm{B}$ if $\mathrm{A}$ and if $\mathrm{A}$ then $\mathrm{B}$, then $\mathrm{B}$, nothing follows without the rule: $\mathrm{A}$, if $\mathrm{A}$ then $\mathrm{B}$, so $\mathrm{B}$. No belief explains how an inference is made. A rule is not the proposition that it is valid. Nor, once they are distinguished, does acceptance of the rule seem to need belief in the sophisticated, metalogical proposition: one cannot even argue that accepted inference rules are finite because they correspond one-to-one with such beliefs. Still, though acceptance of rules is not belief, it should need a vehicle if the latter does and it is to be similarly actual. So one can argue in parallel with, if not as an extension of, the belief case, that we can accept just finitely many inference rules. This completes the picture. A finite mind actually accepts a finite number of beliefs and inference rules, which yield infinitely many derived commitments. What could be less surprising?

Yet odd consequences follow. Take a simple mathematical theory: first-order Peano arithmetic. Most mathematicians would claim to accept it. Yet it can be shown to have no finite axiomatization.6 So no mathematician really believes any basis for it. Presumably he believes both the second-order proposition that all the theorems of first-order Peano arithmetic are true and a finitely axiomatizable second-order version of arithmetic. This has all the first-order theorems as consequences, so he is committed to them – but only by believing other propositions too. The same must go for other standard theories not finitely axiomatizable: Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory, for instance.

Someone might object: this is absurd! Here is the theory in front of me: why should I not believe it as it stands, if I find it convincing? Surely a finite description of the theory is enough – why need it also have a finite set of axioms? Now ‘finite description’ is far too weak. ‘All arithmetic truths’ is a finite description of a set of propositions, but no one can use it to believe all arithmetic truths. To believe that all arithmetic truths are true is not to believe all arithmetic truths. Indeed, given an infinite non-repeating list of propositions (ordered, say, by their expressions in some language) and any physically defined number $k$ (say, the ratio of two lengths), ‘the set which contains the $n$th proposition if and only if the $n$th digit in the binary expansion of $k$ is 0’ is a finite description, but does not thereby enable anyone to believe all the propositions in the set. In both cases, the finite description is useless because to possess it is not to know which propositions it includes. We want rather some finite means – some mechanism – to determine which sentences are in the set. Given Church’s thesis,7 this idea is correctly formalized by that of a Turing machine, or, equivalently, a recursive function. A (complete) recursive function corresponds to a Turing machine that (eventually) outputs a number whatever number is input. Given a mechanical numbering of the sentences of a language, a theory in it is recursively axiomatizable if some Turing machine has, say, output 0 when the number of an axiom is input and output 1 for any other input. One easily shows that all finitely axiomatizable theories are recursively axiomatizable. The distinction between theories which are and are not recursively axiomatizable certainly plays a far greater role in mathematical logic than does that between theories which are and are not finitely axiomatizable. Indeed, recursive axiomatizability tends to be used as the criterion of a theoretically graspable theory – of course, some recursively axiomatized theories are in practice too complex for any human, but so are some finitely axiomatized theories. By Gödel’s theorem, the truths of arithmetic cannot be recursively axiomatized: this sets one of the great problems of the philosophy of mathematics; who worried, before Gödel’s proof, that no finite axiomatization had been found? The central role so far given here to finite axiomatizability is suspect.

A defence might cite a result of Kleene’s:8 given a recursively axiomatized first order theory, one can extend its language by adding one new primitive predicate and finitely axiomatize a theory whose theorems expressible in the old language are exactly the theorems of the old theory. That is, one can be committed to the theorems of any recursively axiomatized first order theory and to nothing else expressible in their language, by a finite number of beliefs (and rules) in an extended language. Is finite axiomatizability therefore no extra restriction? Hardly, for a recursively but not finitely axiomatizable theory is still incapable, on this view of being all one’s commitments. Belief in an axiom of the extended language needs its own vehicle – one cannot attribute the extra without psychological implication – which contradicts the sense that a recursively axiomatized theory is believable as it stands.

What could it be, to accept a recursively but not finitely axiomatized theory as it stands? Presumably the abstract Turing machine corresponds to some physical mechanism that, presented with a sentence, accepts or rejects it according as it is or is not an axiom (where rejecting $\mathrm{P}$ implies not immediately believing $\mathrm{P}$, rather than immediately believing $\text{-} \mathrm{P}$). The mechanism may cause belief but its possession need not involve belief – though one may happen to have beliefs about the mechanism. To believe that everything the mechanism accepts is true is not to believe everything the mechanism accepts, nor need the mechanism accept that everything it accepts is true. Rather, the mechanism is a form of intuition, by which we find certain propositions just striking us as true. For one does not operate the mechanism by propositional thought. If one did, the propositions accepted would have been derived and so would not be axioms of one’s system: it would have been misdescribed. So even if the operation were conscious, we could introspect only, as it were, the clanking machinery, issuing at last in acceptance or rejection. Such thoughts as this spectacle might prompt would be about, not part of, the mechanism.

I do not deny that such mechanisms operate in our brains. But have they much to do with the problem, how our actual, conscious belief system commits us to infinitely many propositions? No doubt such mechanisms represent a commitment of our nature to the propositions they would accept (and their consequences): we find ourselves the kind of being that accepts them when they are proposed. But we can also consciously take up a view in its entirety that carries infinite commitment, rather than finding ourselves implicated in it item by item. Here non-propositional mechanisms are irrelevant. The axioms of the system the mechanism represents are not all axioms of my belief system, for an axiom of my belief system is a proposition I actually accept: since the system the mechanism represents has infinitely many axioms, at any time just finitely many will have been presented for acceptance, so infinitely many will not have been accepted (though they would be if presented): these are not yet axioms of my system (though they could become so). So we remain with the view that our system comprises a finite number of axioms and rules yielding infinitely many consequences. I shall now show that this is utterly mistaken.

# II

What is an inference rule? At its simplest, a licence immediately to infer a proposition of a certain form from a set of propositions of related forms. All such licences are inference rules: inference rules of more elaborate form are mentioned later. Modus ponens, for instance, is the licence immediately to infer a proposition $\mathrm{Q}$ from the set of propositions $\{\mathrm{P}, \mathrm{P} \mathbin{\to} \mathrm{Q}\}$. So inference rules with empty premise sets form a special case. Such rules licence the immediate inference of propositions of certain forms from sets of no propositions at all: these propositions may simply be asserted regardless of what has gone before. But, as logicians have long seen, that is just to say that they are axioms. So axioms form a special case of inference rules: does not the restriction to finitely many rules entail the restriction to finitely many axioms?

Quite the opposite. For an inference rule is generally an inference schema: a licence immediately to infer any proposition of a certain form from a set of propositions of related forms. A rule could licence only the inference of one particular proposition from a non-empty set of other propositions – a one-instance schema – but I know of no logician bothering with such a thing. An inference schema with no premises is an axiom schema: a licence to assert any proposition of a certain form. But an axiom schema typically contains a schematic letter for which any proposition (or predicate, or term, depending on its syntactic category) of the language is substitutable: since each of these categories is typically infinite, the schema typically has infinitely many instances – here, infinitely many axioms. For instance, the axiom schema $\mathrm{A} \to \text{-}\text{-} \mathrm{A}$ has infinitely many instances, one for each sentence of the language. Hence, given that the mind can accept finitely many inference rules, the subsumption of axioms under rules refutes rather than proves the restriction to finitely many axioms.

Could one stipulate that inference rules have at least one premise? That would achieve nothing, for an axiom schema, though not itself an inference schema, would remain deeply analogous to one. Could one claim that, since acceptance of an inference schema has yet to be analysed, it might turn out somehow disanalogous to acceptance of an axiom schema – that for some reason the latter’s instances must be taken individually whereas the former’s may be taken collectively? That is quite implausible. For the instance $\mathrm{P}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{P}_n \vdash \mathrm{Q}$ of an inference schema becomes the instance $\vdash \mathrm{P}_1 \to (\ldots(\mathrm{P}_n \to \mathrm{Q})\ldots)$ of an axiom schema, the two being equivalent given standard rules for $\to$. The rule $\mathrm{A}, \mathrm{B} \vdash \mathrm{A} \mathbin{\&} \mathrm{B}$ becomes the axiom schema $\vdash \mathrm{A} \to (\mathrm{B} \to (\mathrm{A} \mathbin{\&} \mathrm{B}))$. Conversely, the instance $\vdash \mathrm{P}$ of an axiom schema becomes the instance $\mathrm{P} \to \mathrm{P} \vdash \mathrm{P}$ of an inference schema, the two again being equivalent given standard rules for $\to$. In particular, first order arithmetic cannot be finitely axiomatized because of its induction schema $\vdash \mathrm{F}(0) \to (\forall x\,(\mathrm{F}(x) \to \mathrm{F}(x+1)) \to \forall x\,\mathrm{F}(x))$: to suggest that a switch to the inference schema $\mathrm{F}(0), \forall x\,(\mathrm{F}(x) \to \mathrm{F}(x+1)) \vdash \forall x\,\mathrm{F}(x)$ effects a profound change in the system’s cognitive status, seems absurd.

Given the rules for $\to$ the difference between other corresponding inference and axiom schemata is merely stylistic. If the mind can accept inference schemata, it can accept axiom schemata. Now if axiom schemata correspond to inference schemata, axioms – that is, possibly unique instances of axiom schemata – correspond to instances of inference rules, not (as we shallowly thought) to the rules themselves. So conversely, if the mind can accept just finitely many axioms, it can accept just finitely many instances of inference rules. Then if the mind accepts just finitely many axioms, it is committed to just finitely many propositions. For the conclusions of the inferences that commit the mind are either axioms or the conclusions of instances of inference rules: since there are just finitely many of each (an instance of an inference rule having a unique conclusion), just finitely many conclusions are possible. By rearrangement of the material infinitely many inferences might be constructible, but these would fall into finitely many groups, all members of each having the same conclusion. So the demand that the system have just finitely many axioms prevents all explanation of its infinite commitment. For acceptance of an inference schema is just as puzzling as belief in infinitely many propositions.

Surely our actual belief systems do commit us infinitely. So we do accept inference schemata. Then, whether we actually accept axiom schemata or not, we certainly could do so, for they form the simplest case of inference rules with infinitely many instances – if we can accept any such rule, we can accept some axiom schema (naturally some axiom schemata are too complex for us to accept).

What is it to accept an axiom schema? Consider the schema $\text{-}\text{-} \mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{A}$. Its acceptance is not belief in the proposition $\forall \mathrm{P}\,(\text{-}\text{-} \mathrm{P} \to \mathrm{P})$: that would confuse the second order proposition with the first order schema. To infer a particular instance of the axiom schema – say $\text{-}\text{-} 0=1 \to 0=1$ – from such a proposition would take, say, the inference schema for eliminating a second order universal quantifier: $\forall \mathrm{P}\,\mathrm{\phi}(\mathrm{P}) \vdash \mathrm{\phi}(\mathrm{Q})$ (where $\mathrm{Q}$ is free for $\mathrm{P}$ in $\mathrm{\phi}(\mathrm{P})$). Acceptance of an inference schema has been substituted for acceptance of an axiom schema; nothing is gained.

Recall the original argument that a human belief system has finitely many axioms. It claimed that every belief has a vehicle, that a man has just finitely many vehicles and that a vehicle carries just finitely many beliefs: the conclusion, that his beliefs are finite, follows logically. Now that the vehicles of belief are finite in number seems scarcely contestible, while denial that belief needs a vehicle would wreck the picture of belief we need to explain our limitations. So we should drop the third premise: that a vehicle carries just finitely many beliefs. Some vehicle carries infinitely many beliefs. Now what is this but the psychologization of the very notion of a schema? The axiom schema $\mathrm{A} \to (\mathrm{B} \to \mathrm{A})$ carries, as it were, its infinity of instances. The schema itself is, no more than a vehicle, the object of belief: for it is not the corresponding second order proposition; its instances are the beliefs. A sentence schema, as well as a sentence, can carry belief (by ‘sentence’ I always mean ‘indicative sentence’). Now the instances are not inferred from the schema, for a belief is not inferred from its vehicle. Yet belief in a proposition $(0=1 \to 0=1) \to (0=1 \to (0=1 \to 0=1))$ expressed by a sentence present to me, does seem more direct than that belief when only the schema $\mathrm{A} \to (\mathrm{B} \to \mathrm{A})$ is present to me: I may never have thought of that sentence, let alone as instantiating that schema. To call the latter ‘belief that $(0=1 \to 0=1) \to (0=1 \to (0=1 \to 0=1))$’ at all, seems odd. Say rather that a schema commits us directly to its instances and indirectly to their consequences. Thus commitment bypasses belief, which seems to require private vehicles. If a sentential vehicle carries many passengers, they are direct commitments: if one, it is a belief. Hence if the axioms of a system include the instances of its axiom schemata (call these, underived theorems), they are a false analogy of belief. These immediately acceptable propositions are rather our direct commitments: the derived theorems are our indirect commitments. We can still say that our beliefs are the axioms of the system, when ‘axiom’ is restricted to propositions accepted in their own right, not just implicitly, through a schema.

This analogy with deductive systems must be handled with care. In a formal system derived and underived theorems are sharply distinguished: a mathematician may use a derived theorem of a formal system as thought it were an axiom, but the result is not a strictly formal proof, just a guarantee that one exists. No such distinction binds the mind: once a proposition is derived, all trace of its derivation may disappear, it may operate autonomously. A belief $\mathrm{B}$ need not advertise its origin as the consequence of a belief $\mathrm{A}$, and subsequent disbelief in $\mathrm{A}$ may not be felt as grounds for suspicion of $\mathrm{B}$. To insist that $\mathrm{B}$ is not an axiom, because it was derived in the forgotten past, is to impose a complicating classification quite external to the present belief system: in Saussurean terms, it confuses synchronic and diachronic analysis. We must not expect too much from the application of the deductive model to the development of belief systems in time: what sense can it make of beliefs gained in perception or lost to alcohol? But that does not undermine the present discussion, aimed at the problem: how can a finite mind, at one time, have infinite commitment? A man’s belief system at each time may be like a deductive system: but its theorems, once derived, become the axioms of the systems that replace it.

So far only linguistic schemata have been mentioned. Yet surely beliefs can have non-linguistic vehicles (say, mental images) – we do not always think in words. Linguistic schemata suffice to explain infinite commitment, but non-linguistic schemata seem worth canvassing. If a non-linguistic vehicle has anything like syntax, schemata may be abstractible from it. For another vehicle could be like it in form but lack some of the content a belief needs: this would be a schema, with as instances the vehicles of direct commitments related to it as the original vehicle was.

In the verbal case, sentences carry belief in, and sentence schemata direct commitment to, propositions. What carries direct commitment to the instances of an inference rule? Not a sentence saying that the rule is valid, for that carries belief in that proposition, nor the sentence schema whose instances say that corresponding instances of the rule preserve truth, for that carries direct commitment to those propositions: propositions are no substitute for inferences (an axiom schema may replace a particular inference schema only because the rules for, say, $\to$ remain). However, the ‘$\vdash$’ in ‘$\vdash \mathrm{A}$’ can be read as, not the metalinguistic provability predicate, but an object language sign of force: that is, as a Fregean assertion sign. Then ‘$\vdash \mathrm{A}$’ does not say that $\mathrm{A}$ is assertible: it actually asserts $\mathrm{A}$. Thus $\mathrm{A}$ must be read as a sentence radical, not an assertive sentence: the shared propositional content of the assertion that $\mathrm{A}$, the command to bring it about that $\mathrm{A}$ and the question whether or not $\mathrm{A}$. In ‘$\mathrm{A}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{A}_n \vdash \mathrm{B}$’ too, ‘$\vdash$’ need not be a metalinguistic consequence relation: instead, one can read the whole as asserting $\mathrm{B}$ conditional on $A_1,\ldots,A_n$. $\mathrm{B}$ becomes the sentence radical to which a complex force, dependent on the premise set, attaches: when the set is empty, this force reduces to assertion tout court. Hence ‘$\mathrm{P}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{P}_n \vdash \mathrm{Q}$’ as a conditional assertion, not the assertion of a conditional, seems able to carry an instance of an inference schema: so the substitution of schematic variables for parts of the radicals $\mathrm{P}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{P}_n$ and $\mathrm{Q}$ must give the vehicle of an inference rule. The same should, as before, go for non-linguistic vehicles.

Now Michael Dummett’s article ‘Truth’9 seems to challenge the distinction of a conditional assertion from the assertion of a conditional. But I take the upshot of his argument to be that such a distinction can be made out, in terms of negation. For negation must attach to a proposition: since a conditional assertion does not as a whole express a proposition, negation must attach to the proposition conditionally asserted. But the assertion of a conditional can be negated as a whole, with results quite different from those of negating its consequent. For the material conditional, when $\mathrm{P}$ is false $\mathrm{P} \to \text{-} \mathrm{Q}$ is true but $\text{-}(\mathrm{P} \to \mathrm{Q})$ is false; if ‘if $\mathrm{P}$ then $\mathrm{Q}$’ entails some connection between $\mathrm{P}$ and $\mathrm{Q}$, when $\mathrm{P}$ and $\mathrm{Q}$ are independent ‘if $\mathrm{P}$ then $\text{-} \mathrm{Q}$’ is false but ‘$\text{-}$(if $\mathrm{P}$ then $\mathrm{Q}$)’ is true. This is not quite enough, for the material biconditional too has no effective distinction between external and internal negation: $\mathrm{P} \equiv \text{-} \mathrm{Q}$ and $\text{-}(\mathrm{P} \equiv \mathrm{Q})$ have the same truth table. Perhaps though, conditional assertion can be distinguished from the assertion of a material biconditional on the grounds that, if its conditions are not met, the assertion is ‘null and void’, hence independent of the truth value of the conditionally asserted proposition, whereas if $\mathrm{P}$ is false $\mathrm{P} \equiv \mathrm{Q}$ is materially equivalent to $\text{-} \mathrm{Q}$. Is that technical distinction enough to block the following reply? $\mathrm{P} \vdash \mathrm{Q}$ is not $\vdash \mathrm{P} \to \mathrm{Q}$, but it is in just the same position. Just as we need modus ponens to advance from $\vdash \mathrm{P}$ and $\vdash \mathrm{P} \to \mathrm{Q}$ to $\vdash \mathrm{Q}$, we need another rule to advance from $\vdash \mathrm{P}$ and $\mathrm{P} \vdash \mathrm{Q}$ to $\vdash \mathrm{Q}$. So we are back on Achilles’ regress: to express inferences we need more rules to go from the expression to the inference, to make the inferences without expressing them, we need a mechanism (as with recursive axiomatization) and conscious commitment is inexplicable. Now the mistake here is a capricious insistence on an intrinsic link between commitment and vehicle, if not a confusion between the two. ‘$\mathrm{P} \vdash \mathrm{Q}$’ carries the licence of the inference, not a premise; why should it not? What forces us to take it that way, not another? That opens the vast issue of how vehicles operate – of how, in general, signs have meanings. One should ask the prior question, what forces us to take $\mathrm{P}$ as expressing one proposition rather than another? That is: what is the correct theory of meaning? These are hardly questions to embark on now. One can presuppose that vehicles carry commitments while presupposing nothing as to how they do so. I suggested a candidate vehicle for the licence to infer $\mathrm{Q}$ from $\mathrm{P}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{P}_n$, to make the discussion concrete: I no more undertake here to show how it carries the licence than to show how sentences express propositions. The candidate should not be an assertive sentence, since these are already booked to carry beliefs; although some vehicles (schemata) carry infinitely many commitments of the same kind, clarity is hardly served by vehicles unnecessarily carrying commitments of two different kinds. Why then should some vehicle not carry each member of a merely recursive axiom set? We do not know enough to call such vehicles impossible. I would like, though, to know what they are.

The mind’s capacity has extended from a finite number of axioms to a finite number of axiom schemata. Is this a return to the criterion favoured by mathematical logic, recursive axiomatizability? Not quite. Here is a counterexample. Add two new primitive monadic predicates, $\mathrm{F}$ and $\mathrm{T}$, to (the language of) first order arithmetic formulated without constant or function symbols (its axioms will not be part of the underlying logic). For each natural number $\mathrm{n}$, standardly formalize the proposition that at least $n$ things (in the domain) are $\mathrm{F}$:

$\exists x\,\mathrm{F}x$, $\exists x\,\exists y\,((\mathrm{F}x \mathbin{\&} \mathrm{F}y) \mathbin{\&} \text{-} x=y))$, $\exists x\,\exists y\,\exists z\,((\mathrm{F}x \mathbin{\&} \mathrm{F}y \mathbin{\&} \mathrm{F}z) \mathbin{\&} (\text{-} x=y \mathbin{\&} \text{-} x=z \mathbin{\&} \text{-} y=z))$, …

Call the set of all such sentences $\mathrm{S} = \mathrm{F}_1, \mathrm{F}_2, \mathrm{F}_3,\ldots$. One can mechanically check whether a given sentence is in $\mathrm{S}$: hence $\mathrm{S}$ is a recursive axiom set whose members collectively assert that infinitely many things are $\mathrm{F}$. Suppose that $\mathrm{S}$ can be axiomatized by a finite number of schemata $\mathrm{A}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{A}_n$: that is, all members of $\mathrm{S}$ are true in a model if and only if each instance of each $\mathrm{A}_1$ is. These schemata are equivalent to their conjunction $\mathrm{B}$. If $\mathrm{T}$ or arithmetic predicates appear in $\mathrm{B}$, replace them by new schematic variables: the result $\mathrm{B}'$ will still be equivalent to $\mathrm{S}$, for each instance of $\mathrm{B}$ is an instance of $\mathrm{B}'$, so any model of $\mathrm{B}'$ is a model of $\mathrm{S}$, while if a model of $\mathrm{S}$ falsified an instance of $\mathrm{B}'$, $\mathrm{T}$ and the arithmetic predicates (which do not appear in $\mathrm{S}$) could be reinterpreted by the predicates substituted for the corresponding schematic variables, giving a model of $\mathrm{S}$ falsifying $\mathrm{B}$, contrary to hypothesis. Reinterpreting the schematic variables in $\mathrm{B}'$ as predicate letters gives a first order sentence $\mathrm{P}$ in an extended language (they cannot be substituted for the schematic variables in $\mathrm{B}$). Now each finite subset $\mathrm{S}'$ of $\mathrm{S}$ has a model in which $\mathrm{P}$ is false: for some number $n$ will be the largest such that $\mathrm{S}'$ contains $\mathrm{F}_n$, so in a model with exactly $n$ $\mathrm{F}$ things, each member of $\mathrm{S}'$ is true. But $\mathrm{F}_{n+1}$ is false, so by hypothesis some instance of $\mathrm{B}'$ is false: its substitutions for the schematic variables in $\mathrm{B}'$ can be used to interpret the corresponding predicate letters in $\mathrm{P}$, which are otherwise unconstrained. Hence $\mathrm{P}$ is false in the model. Thus every finite subset of $\mathrm{S} \cup \{\text{-} \mathrm{P}\}$ has a model. So by the Compactness Theorem $\mathrm{S} \cup \{\text{-} \mathrm{P}\}$ has a (countable) model, $\mathrm{M}$.10 $\mathrm{T}$ and the arithmetic predicates do not appear in $\mathrm{S} \cup \{\text{-} \mathrm{P}\}$; hence the latter can be given standard interpretations and the former can be interpreted as coding the extensions in $\mathrm{M}$ of the new predicates by standard Gödelian means (for instance, if the $i$th new predicate is the dyadic $\mathrm{Q}$, one could have $\mathrm{Q}(m,n)$ equivalent to $\mathrm{T}(2^i3^m5^n)$). Then formulae in the original language have the extensions of the new predicates in $\mathrm{M}$, so a model of $\mathrm{S}$ falsifies an instance of $\mathrm{B}'$, contradicting the hypothesis that $\mathrm{S}$ and $\mathrm{B}'$ are equivalent. So $\mathrm{S}$ is a recursive axiomatization not axiomatizable by finitely many schemata.

This argument is, of course, restricted to schematic variables over predicates. If one took the quantifiers ‘for at least $n$ values of $x\ldots$’, for $n$ a natural number, as a substitution class, the schema $\mathrm{Q}x\mathrm{F}x$ would axiomatize $\mathrm{S}$, where ‘$\mathrm{Q}$’ is a schematic variable over that class. But those quantifiers are no syntactic category if they are of the same category as the universal quantifier, which is not one of them. Is an appropriate substitution class that of all quantifiers, or of all forms taking predicates to sentences? In general, the permissible range for a schematic variable over anything but predicates (or terms) raises so many problems that one should be prepared to abandon $\mathrm{S}$ as not finitely schematizable. I restricted substitution to predicates already in the language, although the argument would be much simpler without the restriction. But a schema hardly commits one to instances involving predicates one cannot understand and need not presume to be coherent: the complication was necessary.

One can believe that all members of $\mathrm{S}$ are true, but cannot even be directly committed to all its members (one could be indirectly committed, since they all follow from arithmetic, which is axiomatizable by a finite number of schemata, with the extra axiom $\forall x\,\mathrm{F}x$). The dots which completed, or rather failed to complete, the listing of $\mathrm{S}$ are, after all, significant of our incapacity: as Wittgenstein realized, recanting Tractatus doctrine, they are not the dots of laziness. But the unbelievability of $\mathrm{S}$ is tolerable, for who wants to believe that theory anyway?

# III

The capacity of finite minds for infinite commitment has turned out to depend not on finitely many axioms having infinitely many consequences but on finitely many schemata having infinitely many instances. What does this imply for semantics, where much has been made of the finitude of our beliefs? A speaker of a language can (in theory) understand each of its infinitely many sentences; if in practice one is too complex for him, he still has the materials for understanding, but as it were lacks space to put them together. For each sentence he knows, potentially and implicitly, a proposition of the form: ‘grass is green’ means that grass is green (non-indicatives and indexicals take more machinery). If speakers could believe any infinite set of sentences, a series of such disquotations might adequately express their knowledge. Semantic theories (as in the Davidsonian style11) often start from the observation that, since we are finite, our knowledge cannot take that form. So one does not seek merely a theory knowledge of which would suffice for knowing what each sentence of the language means, for such theories need not be finite. The restriction to a finite number of axioms, generating an infinity of theorems, is often inferred immediately. But that is sheer error. A finite mind, as noted above, can assent to each sentence in an infinite recursive set without process of reasoning. Such cases were dismissed only as examples of infinite commitment consciously achieved – the recursive mechanism is not itself conscious belief or inference. But we know systematic semantic knowledge not to be conscious in speakers anyway: why should it not be merely recursive?

Now that seems to trivialize semantics. For a recursive mechanism could select all sentences of the disquotational form ‘ “grass is grass” means that grass is green.’ Some presumably recursive condition of well-formedness for object language sentences would be needed, to eliminate, for instance, ‘ “blah blah blah” means that blah blah blah’; the recursion might analyze well-formed sentences, but this would receive no semantic significance. When ‘means’ is replaced by some truth-functional construction, such as ‘is true if and only if’ in the Davidsonian programme, the Kleene method will even expand this theory, by addition of a new predicate, to one finitely axiomatizable. This predicate would have a status such as has Tarski’s satisfaction relation between infinite sequences and open sentences in truth-conditional semantics: unintuitive, yet justified by its role in a theory that yields appropriate truth conditions, nor needing any other grounding in reality. This schematically axiomatized theory would still give no semantic significance to the syntax of the ‘interpreted’ sentences. What then of the doctrine that a semantic theory should exhibit the meanings of wholes as composed of the meanings of their parts?

Now this trivialization of semantics is really the exposure of a shallowness in thinking of a speaker’s understanding of his language as a theory in the sense of a set of sentences. If that were literally, true, if a speaker, each time they understood a sentence, generated another sentence stating its meaning, it would only shift the problem: for in what would their understanding of the second sentence consist? The recursive mechanism depends utterly on the sentence ‘ “grass is green” means that grass is green’ being produced, so that it can verify the type identity of the letters within the quotation marks with those that follow ‘means’ (or something marginally more complex if Tarski’s spelling procedure is adopted, or much more complex if the English sentence were translated into some internal code). If the speaker merely produced the proposition that ‘grass is green’ means that grass is green, no such simplistic test could apply, for to the right-hand side would correspond neither letters nor concepts of letters, but (say) concepts of grass and greenness, which the mechanism would have to see were expressed by the words of the quotation: it is unclear even what ‘recursive’ means here. Yet the speaker cannot merely produce a proposition – what could a mechanism have to do with it if they did? ‘No belief without a vehicle’ has been our slogan. This is just the problem of meaning all over again, or, in our terms: how does a vehicle carry its belief? No translation from one system of vehicles to another is any answer. That theories of translation are not theories of meaning has become a platitude: a theory of meaning for a language must say what its sentences mean, not just that they mean the same as some other sentences (or themselves): but the point thus gained is conceded at last, if speakers’ implicit knowledge of the theory is reduced to possession of its sentences. For they do not just know those sentences to express truths: they know the truths those sentences express. Can one secure by other means the constraint on semantic theory that the finiteness of our minds had been hoped to yield?12

Consider a language learner who seems to understand the sentences ‘grass is green’ and ‘snow is white.’ One would expect them also to understand ‘grass is white’ and ‘snow is green.’ This is sometimes presented as an empirical regularity, though the grounds for thinking it such seem themselves to be a priori, not empirical. We might simply not count someone as understanding the first pair who did not understand the second: at most they would know what the first pair meant as one can know, by being told, what a Russian sentence means without knowing which elements contribute what to this meaning. Perhaps a condition of understanding ‘grass is green’ and ‘snow is white’ is understanding ‘grass’, ‘snow’, ‘is’, ‘green’ and ‘white’ in their full generality, so that one can recombine them as ‘grass is white’ and ‘snow is green.’ We may merely be reinsisting that a semantic theory exhibit the meaning of a whole as composed of the meanings of its parts. But to postulate connections, whether empirical or conceptual, between the understanding of different sentences seems reasonable enough. One may then demand that when sentences $\mathrm{S}_1,\ldots.,\mathrm{S}_m$ (say ‘grass is green’ and ‘snow is white’) are so connected to sentences $\mathrm{T}_1,\ldots.,\mathrm{T}_n$ (say ‘grass is white’ and ‘snow is green’), a semantic theory explains this by showing how all the knowledge needed to understand $\mathrm{T}_1,\ldots.,\mathrm{T}_n$ is already needed to understand $\mathrm{S}_1,\ldots.,\mathrm{S}_m$ (say, knowledge of what ‘grass,’ ‘snow,’ ‘is,’ ‘green’ and ‘white’ mean). This clearly tends towards a one-one correspondence between items of semantic knowledge and syntactic atoms. Since the latter are surely finite in number, so are the items of semantic knowledge. Hence, without appeal to limits on the minds of speakers, we seem to have shown that a semantic theory should have just finitely many axioms. From here, some have seen no workable alternative to Davidson’s: finitely many axioms as the basis of a Tarskian truth-definition, meanings as contributions to truth-conditions and English as something like a predicate calculus. I doubt if the restriction to finitely many axioms is really so inhibiting: but the line our considerations already open is that finitely many items of semantic knowledge need not correspond to finitely many axioms of semantic theory. For schemata were seen to be as much units of knowledge as are their instances. Why should not finitely many items of semantic knowledge correspond to, say finitely many rules of inference? And then many of the problems that give truth-conditional semantics its current interest seem to vanish.

Take an intensional context: belief. Why belief sentences do not fit straightforwardly into recursive truth theories may not be just that the truth value of $\mathrm{P}$ does not determine the truth value of ‘$c$ believes that $\mathrm{P}$’ (‘recursive’ here refers to the compositional analysis such theories give sentences: it does not mean ‘recursively axiomatizable’). For the truth value of $\mathrm{P}$ does not determine the truth value of ‘it is causally necessary that $\mathrm{P}$,’ but it remains arguable that, for each English sentence $\mathrm{S}$, ‘it is causally necessary that’$^\frown \mathrm{S}$13 is true if and only if it is causally necessary that $\mathrm{S}$ is true, which is all such theories need (or rather, some version with satisfaction rather than truth – a complication I shall ignore).14 Now the corresponding axiom for belief states that, for each English sentence $\mathrm{S}$, ‘$c$ believes that’$^\frown \mathrm{S}$ is true if and only if $c$ believes that $\mathrm{S}$ is true. Yet if Paul has not heard of Tarski, he may well believe that snow is white without believing that ‘snow is white’ is true. Hence the apparent need to reduce belief to extensional notions. But if one’s understanding of ‘believes’ may be expressed by a schema rather than an axiom, that seems unnecessary. For consider the axiom schema: if $\mathrm{S}$ means that $\mathrm{A}$ then ‘$c$ believes that’$^\frown \mathrm{S}$ means that $c$ believes that $\mathrm{A}$. Substitution of sentence names for $\mathrm{S}$, sentences for $\mathrm{A}$ and personal names for $c$ yields no false instances. If one desires a theory in terms of truth – though this is often motivated by the supposed unworkability of theories in terms of meaning – one needs an inference schema like the modal necessitation rule ($\vdash \mathrm{A}/\vdash □ \mathrm{A}$): if ‘$\mathrm{S}$ is true if and only if $\mathrm{A}$’ is a theorem of the semantic theory, so is ‘“$c$ believes that”$^\frown \mathrm{S}$ is true if and only if $c$ believes that $\mathrm{A}$.’15

Some sentences such as ‘“snow is white” is true if and only if $17.18 = 360$’ are ‘accidentally’ true: Davidson’s approach depends on these not being derivable in the semantic theory. So one also could not derive, by the rule, ‘“Mary believes that grass is green” is true if and only if Mary believes that $17.18 = 360$,’ which is false if Mary knows more about gardening than arithmetic – whereas this example refutes the corresponding axiom schema ‘if $\mathrm{S}$ is true if and only if $\mathrm{A}$ then “$c$ believes that”$^\frown \mathrm{S}$ is true if and only if $c$ believes that $\mathrm{A}$,’ for here it would have a true (though underivable) antecedent and a false (and underivable) consequent. Davidson makes the truth of its axioms a necessary, though it can hardly be a sufficient, constraint on a truth theory qua theory of meaning. Actually, a few substitutions for $\mathrm{A}$ falsify even the rule: for instance, ‘$\mathrm{S}$ is true.’ For ‘$\mathrm{S}$ is true if and only if $\mathrm{S}$ is true’ is derivable by elementary logic: then so would be ‘“Paul believes that”$^\frown \mathrm{S}$ is true if and only if Paul believes that $\mathrm{S}$ is true,’ which falsely imported semantic notions into Paul’s beliefs. However, part of the Davidsonian apparatus must be some device to select that value of $\mathrm{A}$ for which the theorem ‘$\mathrm{S}$ is true if and only if $\mathrm{A}$’ gives the right truth conditions for $\mathrm{S}$: ‘“grass is green” is true if and only if “grass is green” is true’ is derivable in a Davidsonian theory, but for him ‘“grass is green” is true’ does not give the truth conditions of ‘grass is green,’ else his theory would have nothing to do. Whatever selection device is used can equally be applied to the premise of the rule.

So the use of schemata expressing semantic knowledge seems to render even the worst intensional contexts amenable to recursive treatment, without any analysis of logical form beneath surface syntax. If so (and of course far more investigation is needed), this suggests not at all that they are at last understood – we obviously do not grasp our grasp of them – but that the disquotational approach has little to offer but disquotation. Its interest was that of an heroic attempt to overcome quite artificial obstacles.

The medium of thought must constrain what messages it can carry – perhaps, Chomsky suggests, to the point of excluding all true theories on some topics. But such remarks are largely empty while our knowledge of both the medium and the carrying remains so small. We do not know what we cannot know. Not that knowing what we cannot know is outright contradiction: but what ground for confidence have we that no self-referential paradox inherent in us ensures that we cannot know what we cannot know?

October, 1981
