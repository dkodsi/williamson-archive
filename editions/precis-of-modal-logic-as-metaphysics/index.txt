# Précis of Modal Logic as Metaphysics

Timothy Williamson

Philosophy and Phenomenological Research 88 (3) (2014): 713–716.

Necessitism is the thesis that necessarily everything is necessarily something. Contingentism is the negation of necessitism. Here ‘necessarily’ expresses metaphysical necessity, what couldn’t have been otherwise; ‘everything’ and ‘something’ are absolutely unrestricted quantifiers. Necessitism says that ontology is necessary, contingentism that it is contingent. There are similar theses about time too: permanentism says that ontology is permanent, temporaryism that is temporary. If we confuse being something with being something concrete, necessitism (and permanentism) will look obviously false, since it is contingent that we are concrete. On the best necessitist view, however, something concrete could have been something non-concrete (but not abstract). Common sense has no authority to decide between necessitism and contingentism; it is a more theoretical dispute. One strand in the obscure debate between possibilism and actualism is the clearer dispute between necessitism and contingentism. David Lewis’s modal realism makes necessitism trivially true, but necessitism in no way implies modal realism. Nor is necessitism to be confused with Meinongianism. The dispute has consequences for questions about the interests of merely possible persons, just as its temporal analogue has for questions about the badness of death.

In quantified modal logic, necessitism is closely connected to the validity of the Barcan formula and its converse, adumbrated by Avicenna. They appear in proof-theoretic work of Barcan Marcus and are validated by Carnap in his early version of possible world semantics. Their metaphysical interest was at first unclear, because the modalities were read as logical. Prior noticed the metaphysical significance of the analogous formulas in quantified tense logic, which served as a template for a more metaphysical reading of the modal formulas. However, Prior’s attempt to construct a contingentist quantified modal logic based on the principle that non-being induces truth-value gaps was a failure.

Possible world model theory reached maturity in the work of Kripke. However, the model theory is just non-modal mathematics; the challenge is to relate it to modal metaphysics. Here is a way. A modal formula is metaphysically universal just if its universal generalization over all its free variables and non-logical constants is true on its intended interpretation, treating quantifiers as unrestricted and modalities as metaphysical. A model structure is a model minus its interpretations of non-logical constants. A formula is valid on a model structure just if it is true in every model with that model structure. A necessary but insufficient condition for a model structure to be intended is this: the formulas valid on it are exactly the metaphysically universal ones. Given a plausible abstract theory of propositions, a model structure is defined satisfying the condition and reasonably taken as intended, for propositional modal logic. Unfortunately, its definition doesn’t allow one to read off which formulas it validates. Some limited results are obtainable, however: for example, if all theorems of the system S5 are metaphysically universal, only theorems of S5 are metaphysically universal. For first-order modal logic, one can show that if some model structure satisfies the necessary condition for being intended then the Barcan formula is metaphysically universal. Since contingentists deny that it is metaphysically universal, they must take a more instrumental attitude to the model theory. Things are better for necessitists, since the argument for an intended model structure can be extended to the quantified case. However, problems remain, because model structures are sets; arguably, no set is big enough to satisfy our intentions. Nevertheless, necessitists can at least approximate an intended model structure more closely than contingentists can.

One choice point for contingentist modal logic is whether being something is necessary for satisfying predicates. Arguably, purported mythological counterexamples fail, and full-blooded contingentists should treat being something as necessary for satisfying both simple and complex predicates. For complex ones, this requires a predicate abstraction device. Stalnaker’s system of quantified modal logic is built along these lines, but a detailed analysis shows that it has problematic features. Stalnaker’s criterion for identifying representationally significant aspects of a model also has unwanted consequences.

A more revealing comparison between contingentism and necessitism can be drawn in the setting of higher-order modal logic, which permits quantification into predicate and sentence position. Its history goes back to work of Lewis and Langford, Barcan Marcus, Carnap, Montague, Gallin, and others, but the combination of higher-order and modal logic is underdiscussed and under-used by philosophers. A leading interpretation of second-order quantifiers, in terms of plural quantification, is unsuitable because pluralities in the relevant sense are extensional, modally rigid in membership, whereas a more intensional reading is needed. Substitutional readings of the higher-order quantifiers are also unsuitable, owing to their sensitivity to expressive limitations of the language. Nor do higher-order quantifiers have adequate equivalents in natural language. Nevertheless, we can understand them by analogy with, but not as reducible to, first-order quantifiers. A faithful semantics requires a higher-order metalanguage, although for technical purposes an unintended set-theoretic model theory can be provided in a first-order metalanguage. Irreducible higher-order quantification exemplifies the non-exhaustiveness of the Quinean dichotomy between ontology and ideology: it is neither ontological (since ‘What is there?’ is a first-order question) nor merely ideological (since it is just as metaphysically committing as first-order quantification).

A good test of the relative power of contingentist and necessitist higher-order modal logics concerns comprehension principles. Necessitists can have strong comprehension principles, permitting unrestricted generalization into predicate position. If contingentists adopt those comprehension principles too, they are committed to higher-order analogues of necessitism. No plausible metaphysical picture motivates such a hybrid of first-order contingentism and higher-order necessitism. Alternatively, if comprehension principles are restricted in line with contingentist limits on first-order being, they are too weak to yield natural modal analogues of standard forms of higher-order mathematical reasoning; the ability of higher-order logic to capture such mathematical reasoning is one of its main advantages.

Both contingentists and necessitists hope to simulate the other side’s useful distinctions in their own terms. This is not a matter of translating one language into another, because they speak a common language and disagree without equivocation. However, each side hopes to find cash-values of the other’s utterances, sentences neutral in the metaphysical dispute that the other takes as equivalent to the originals. This can be done in both directions for all sentences of first-order modal logic, if each side accepts a modest fleshing-out of its position. Even in higher-order logic, necessitists can simulate contingentists’ distinctions, because contingentists treat quantifiers as equivalent to ones restricted to a realm both sides recognize. However, contingentists cannot simulate all the distinctions necessitists draw with higher-order quantifiers. The best attempt to do so is by Fine; it uses infinite strings of quantifiers and modal operators which can be made sense of on necessitist but not on contingentist terms.

A common charge against necessitism is that in some sense it fails to ground the modal in the non-modal. For example, the modal difference between a merely possible mountain and a merely possible river is not grounded in some non-modal difference. On closer examination, however, the distinction between the modal and the non-modal turns out to be surprisingly elusive. It is not even clear which predicates are supposed to express modal properties. Since contingentists have not succeeded in grounding the modal in the non-modal, or even in explaining what it would be to do so, there is no pressure on necessitists to try to do the same.

Necessitism is inconsistent with full-blooded truthmaker theory. However, the motivation for such truthmaker theory depends on an unwarranted neglect of higher-order quantification, so the inconsistency is no cost for necessitism, and may even be a benefit.

Analyses of ordinary modal operators as quantifiers over worlds are much less problematic for necessitists than for contingentists. However, if they are analysed only as quantifiers binding variables in a tacit extra argument place for a world tacked onto atomic predicates, then those predicates express necessary relations and there is a danger of treating all genuine contingency as illusory, a result unwelcome even to necessitists. But that view of ordinary modal operators is independently implausible, since it makes their application to artificial notations with no extra argument place for a world redundant, which it is not. It is therefore better to analyse ordinary modal operators as combinations of a quantifier binding a world variable ‘$w$’ with a genuine modal operator ‘at $w$’. Similar issues arise for the analysis of tense operators as quantifiers over times, concerning genuine change.

Overall, necessitism beats contingentism as a metaphysically interpreted higher-order modal logic. The comparison between them is fundamentally abductive, on grounds such as strength, non-causal explanatory power (bringing data under illuminating generalizations), simplicity, elegance, and consistency with the evidence. Thus it is made on similar general grounds to those used for comparing theories in other branches of science. The methodology for choosing first principles of mathematics is similarly abductive. Logic and metaphysics are much less exceptional than they look.
