Iterated Attitudes

Timothy Williamson

Proceedings of the British Academy 95 (1998): 85–133.

The aim of this paper is not to advance a thesis but to explore a phenomenon. The phenomenon is an easily overlooked kind of intensionality, whose neglect causes philosophical problems. It can be put like this: sometimes the set of principles satisfied by one sentential operator differs from, and may even be inconsistent with, the set of principles satisfied by another sentential operator coextensive with the first. These notions will be formally defined. The phenomenon arises when an operator is iterated, in the loose sense of being applied to a sentence in which it itself occurs. Examples will be analysed in detail. Several involve operators by which propositional attitudes are attributed to subjects; one involves metaphysical modality. An application will be made to some arguments against mechanistic views of mind. The phenomenon also raises a number of technical questions in bimodal logic, some of which will be answered.

1. Five examples

Example 1. According to what is sometimes known as the KK principle, if one knows something, then one knows that one knows it (in the propositional sense of ‘know’). The principle is open to several cogent objections (for one objection see Williamson 1992). Even so, qualified versions of it may hold when appropriate restrictions or idealizations are introduced. In such cases, the question arises: under what modes of presentation of oneself to oneself does the qualified KK principle hold? Let us use the phrase ‘singular term’ broadly, to cover definite descriptions as well as names, demonstratives and pronouns such as ‘I’, without prejudice to the question whether definite descriptions are to be analysed as quantifiers. For any singular term tt, ‘tt knows that …’ is therefore a singulary sentence operator (interpret quotation marks as corner quotes where appropriate). Let us say, relative to a fixed context, that the term tt fits the KK principle just in case this holds

(KKt)(\mathrm{KK}_t)
For all P\mathrm{P}, if tt knows that P\mathrm{P}, then tt knows that tt knows that P\mathrm{P}.

Suppose (what is not the case) that in the context of its use by me, ‘I’ fits the KK principle. Suppose also that, although I am in fact the man with a hole in his pocket, I do not know that I am the man with a hole in his pocket. In this context, does the definite description ‘the man with a hole in his pocket’ fit the KK principle? Suppose that the man with a hole in his pocket knows that it is sunny. Does it follow that the man with a hole in his pocket knows that the man with a hole in his pocket knows that it is sunny? It certainly follows that I know that it is sunny (‘… knows that P\mathrm{P}’ is an extensional context for ‘…’). Since KKt\mathrm{KK}_t holds for t=‘I’t = \text{‘I’} in this context, it follows that I know that I know that it is sunny. Thus the man with a hole in his pocket knows that I know that it is sunny. But it does not follow that the man with a hole in his pocket knows that the man with a hole in his pocket knows that it is sunny, or that I know that the man with a hole in his pocket knows that it is sunny. For all I know, the man with a hole in his pocket is asleep and has no idea what the weather is like (‘tt knows that … knows that P\mathrm{P}’ is an intensional context for ‘…’). Thus ‘the man with a hole in his pocket’ does not fit the KK principle in this context, even though in this context the definite description picks out the same individual as ‘I’ does, and ‘I’ fits the KK principle in this context.

Even if an individual satisfies the KK principle in the intuitively intended sense, it does not follow that KKt\mathrm{KK}_t holds whenever the term tt picks out that individual. Nor does KKt\mathrm{KK}_t specify any property of the individual picked out by tt. To do that, one would also need to specify how the knower is to be specified in the content of the knowledge, in a way that depends only on who the knower is. For example, one might stipulate that an individual xx satisfies the KK principle just in case KKt\mathrm{KK}_t holds for t=‘I’t = \text{‘I’} in a context in which xx is the speaker. This comes close to the intuitively intended sense of the principle. Sophisticated defenders of the KK principle entered just such qualifications, which its Cartesian spirit seems to demand (see Hintikka 1962, pp. 158–9, and Castañeda 1970). Failures of KKt\mathrm{KK}_t are not failures of the KK principle when tt does not contribute what ‘I’ would have contributed to the proposition expressed. For present purposes what matters is that although the two sentence operators ‘I know that …’ and ‘The man with a hole in his trousers knows that …’ are coextensive in the present context, in the sense that one yields a truth when applied to a given sentence if and only if the other does, they do not satisfy the same principles: one iterates in the manner of KKt\mathrm{KK}_t, but the other does not.

Example 2. Switch from the subject of the propositional attitude to the attitude itself. On a cartoon version of the Stoic idea of wisdom, xx is wise if and only if xx believes only what xx knows, i.e.

(B/Kx)(\mathrm{B/K}_x)
For all P\mathrm{P}, if xx believes that P\mathrm{P}, xx knows that P\mathrm{P}.

Given the simplifying assumption that knowledge entails belief, the converse of B/Kx\mathrm{B/K}_x holds automatically. Now suppose that Socrates is wise. To ensure that the case is genuinely different from Example 1, assume that Socrates knows perfectly well that he is Socrates. The operators ‘Socrates knows that …’ and ‘Socrates believes that …’ are coextensive, but they need not satisfy the same principles. For example, Socrates knows that if Socrates knows that there is a form of mud then there is a form of mud, simply because Socrates knows that knowledge is factive, and applies that knowledge to the present case. It does not follow that Socrates believes that if Socrates believes that there is a form of mud then there is a form of mud. For although Socrates is wise, he may not believe that he is wise. More generally, the correctness of the schema O[Op⊃p]\mathrm{O}[\mathrm{O}p \supset p] for the operator ‘Socrates knows that’ in place of O\mathrm{O} does not imply its correctness for ‘Socrates believes that’ in place of O\mathrm{O}, even granted the coextensiveness of the two operators.

Matters are slightly more complicated than has yet been indicated. For suppose that Socrates always knows by introspection whether he believes something; suppose also that he believes all the logical consequences of his beliefs. Then if he does not believe that P\mathrm{P}, he believes that Socrates does not believe that P\mathrm{P}. Since ‘Socrates does not believe that P\mathrm{P}’ entails ‘If Socrates believes that P\mathrm{P} then P\mathrm{P}’ (on a truth-functional reading of the conditional), Socrates believes that if Socrates believes that P\mathrm{P} then P\mathrm{P}. The same conclusion follows from the assumption that Socrates does believe that P\mathrm{P}, for P\mathrm{P} entails ‘If Socrates believes that P\mathrm{P} then P\mathrm{P}’. Thus if the schema O[Op⊃p]\mathrm{O}[\mathrm{O}p \supset p] fails for ‘Socrates believes that’ in place of O\mathrm{O}, Socrates fails either to know whether he believes something or to believe the logical consequences of what he believes; both kinds of failure are consistent with wisdom as defined above. In particular, the technique of possible worlds semantics can be used to provide a model in which Socrates is wise, and knows that if he knows that P\mathrm{P} then P\mathrm{P}, but believes neither that P\mathrm{P} nor that if he believes that P\mathrm{P} then P\mathrm{P}.

For the proof of this, consider a possible worlds model based on a set of three worlds {a,b,c}\{a,b,c\}. Interpret K\mathrm{K} (‘Socrates knows that’) and B\mathrm{B} (‘Socrates believes that’) like necessity operators whose accessibility relations are RK\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{K}} and RB\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{B}} respectively, where wRKxw\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{K}}x for all worlds ww and xx, and wRBxw\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{B}}x if and only if either w=aw = a or x=cx = c. Since aa has both RK\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{K}} and RB\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{B}} to all worlds, Kα≡Bα\mathrm{K}\mathrm{\alpha} \equiv \mathrm{B}\mathrm{\alpha} is true at aa for any formula α\mathrm{\alpha}. However, if pp is true at cc but not at bb, then Bp⊃p\mathrm{B}p \supset p is false at bb, so B(Bp⊃p)\mathrm{B}(\mathrm{B}p \supset p) is false at aa (as is ∼Bp⊃B∼Bp\mathord{\sim} \mathrm{B}p \supset \mathrm{B}\mathord{\sim} \mathrm{B}p). Since RK\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{K}} is an equivalence relation, K\mathrm{K} satisfies all the principles of the modal system S5. Since RB\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{B}} is serial, connected and transitive, B\mathrm{B} satisfies all the principles of KDG14\mathrm{KDG}_1 4. See Hughes and Cresswell 1984 for the relevant background in modal logic.

Example 3 involves metamathematical provability operators, and their relevance to John Lucas’s celebrated attempt to enlist Gödel’s work to disprove mechanism. Put racily, Gödel’s second incompleteness theorem says that Peano Arithmetic (PA)—or any recursively axiomatizable system extending it—can prove its own consistency only at the price of inconsistency. A little more formally: if it is provable in PA that it is not provable in PA that 0=10 = 1 then it is provable in PA that 0=10 = 1. But this formulation still has one occurrence of the informal sentence operator ‘It is provable in PA that’ inside the scope of another; it therefore treats the operator as coding something in the first-order language of PA, whose intended interpretation concerns numbers, not proofs. Gödel codes each formula α\mathrm{\alpha} of LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}}, the language of PA, by a numeral ⌜α⌝\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner of LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}}, and provability in PA by a formula Bew⁡(x)\operatorname{Bew}(x) of LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}} with just one free variable. Thus the informal sentence operator is formalized by Bew⁡(⌜…⌝)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner) in LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}}. The second incompleteness theorem says that ⊢PA∼Bew⁡(⌜0=1⌝)\vdash_{\mathrm{PA}}\mathord{\sim}\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner) only if ⊢PA0=1\vdash_{\mathrm{PA}}0=1.

Gödel could have used a different coding. What features of his coding justify the informal reading of Bew⁡(⌜…⌝)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner) as ‘It is provable in PA that’? If LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}} had no intended interpretation, the informal reading would be hard to justify. But LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}} does have an intended interpretation, on which a sentence is true if and only if it is true in all standard models of PA. Since PA has standard models, and they are all isomorphic, exactly one of α\mathrm{\alpha} and ∼α\mathord{\sim}\mathrm{\alpha} is true (given a classical metalogic). Since we have the notion of truth for LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}}, we can stipulate that a one-place sentential operator O\mathrm{O} in LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}} is a provability operator for PA just in case for each sentence α\mathrm{\alpha} of LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}}, Oα\mathrm{O}\mathrm{\alpha} is true if and only if ⊢PAα\vdash_{\mathrm{PA}}\mathrm{\alpha}. Bew⁡(⌜…⌝)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner) is a provability operator for PA in this sense (if we count it an operator).

Any two provability operators O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 for PA are coextensive: O1α≡O2α\mathrm{O}_1\mathrm{\alpha} \equiv \mathrm{O}_2\mathrm{\alpha} is true for each sentence α\mathrm{\alpha} of LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}}. But it does not follow that the biconditionals are provable in PA. The unprovability in PA of ∼Bew⁡(⌜0=1⌝)\mathord{\sim}\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner) does not imply the unprovability in PA of ∼O(0=1)\mathord{\sim} \mathrm{O}(0=1) for every provability operator O\mathrm{O} for PA. For example, we can define an operator O◇\mathrm{O}^◇ in LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}} thus:

O◇α=dfBew⁡(⌜α⌝)&∼Bew⁡(⌜∼α⌝)\mathrm{O}^◇\mathrm{\alpha} =_{\mathrm{df}} \operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner) \mathbin{\&} \mathord{\sim}\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\mathord{\sim}\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner)

Since PA is in fact consistent, the second conjunct of O◇α\mathrm{O}^◇\mathrm{\alpha} is true whenever the first is, so O◇α\mathrm{O}^◇\mathrm{\alpha} is true if and only if Bew⁡(⌜α⌝)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner) is true. Thus O◇\mathrm{O}^◇ is a provability operator for PA. Yet ⊢PA∼O◇(0=1)\vdash_{\mathrm{PA}}\mathord{\sim} \mathrm{O}^◇(0=1), for it is easily checked that ⊢PABew⁡(⌜∼0=1⌝)\vdash_{\mathrm{PA}}\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\mathord{\sim} 0=1\urcorner). Thus, if ⊥\bot is the negation of a theorem of PA, the result of replacing O…\mathrm{O}\ldots throughout the principle O∼O⊥\mathrm{O}\mathord{\sim} \mathrm{O}\bot by O◇…\mathrm{O}^◇\ldots is true, but the result of replacing it by Bew⁡(⌜…⌝)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner) is false. Coextensive provability operators satisfy different principles in PA.

One might hope to exclude unwanted provability operators by analysing ‘It is provable that’ as the existential generalization of ‘xx is a proof that’. Since the latter notion is effectively decidable, it can be subjected to tighter controls. More specifically, say that Pf⁡[…](x)\operatorname{Pf}[\ldots](x) is a proof operator for PA just in case there is an effective procedure for assigning to each closed formula α\mathrm{\alpha} of LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}} a formula Pf⁡[α](x)\operatorname{Pf}[\mathrm{\alpha}](x) of LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}} with just one free variable and coding each proof in an axiomatization of PA by a natural number such that for each natural number nn, if nn codes a proof of α\mathrm{\alpha} then ⊢PAPf⁡[α](𝐧)\vdash_{\mathrm{PA}}\operatorname{Pf}[\mathrm{\alpha}]({𝐧}) (where 𝐧{𝐧} is the LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}} numeral for nn) and otherwise ⊢PA∼Pf⁡[α](𝐧)\vdash_{\mathrm{PA}}\mathord{\sim}\operatorname{Pf}[\mathrm{\alpha}]({𝐧}). The operator of which Bew⁡(⌜…⌝)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner) is the existential generalization is a proof operator in this sense. The definition ensures that the existential generalization of a proof operator is a provability operator. For let Pf⁡[…](x)\operatorname{Pf}[\ldots](x) be a proof operator. If (∃x)Pf⁡[α](x)(\exists x)\operatorname{Pf}[\mathrm{\alpha}](x) is true then Pf⁡[α](𝐧)\operatorname{Pf}[\mathrm{\alpha}]({𝐧}) is true for some nn, so not ⊢PA∼Pf⁡[α](𝐧)\vdash_{\mathrm{PA}}\mathord{\sim}\operatorname{Pf}[\mathrm{\alpha}]({𝐧}) (since everything provable in PA is true), so nn codes a proof of α\mathrm{\alpha} (by definition of a proof operator), so ⊢PAα\vdash_{\mathrm{PA}}\mathrm{\alpha}. Conversely, if ⊢PAα\vdash_{\mathrm{PA}}\mathrm{\alpha}, then some number nn codes a proof of α\mathrm{\alpha}, so ⊢PAPf⁡[α](𝐧)\vdash_{\mathrm{PA}}\operatorname{Pf}[\mathrm{\alpha}]({𝐧}) (by definition of a proof operator), so Pf⁡[α](𝐧)\operatorname{Pf}[\mathrm{\alpha}]({𝐧}) is true, so (∃x)Pf⁡[α](x)(\exists x)\operatorname{Pf}[\mathrm{\alpha}](x) is true.

It turns out that not even the existential generalization of a proof operator need satisfy the same principles as Bew⁡(⌜…⌝)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner). Example: let Bew⁡(⌜…⌝)=(∃x)B(⌜…⌝,x)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner)=\allowbreak(\exists x)\mathrm{B}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner,x). B(⌜…⌝,x)\mathrm{B}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner,x) is a proof operator. Define a new operator Pf⁡+[…](x)\operatorname{Pf}^+[\ldots](x) thus

Pf⁡+[α](x)=dfB(⌜α⌝,x)&α\operatorname{Pf}^+[\mathrm{\alpha}](x) =_{\mathrm{df}} \mathrm{B}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner,x) \mathbin{\&} \mathrm{\alpha}

Pf⁡+[…](x)\operatorname{Pf}^+[\ldots](x) is a proof operator, for if nn codes a proof of α\mathrm{\alpha} in PA then ⊢PAα\vdash_{\mathrm{PA}}\mathrm{\alpha} and ⊢PAB(⌜α⌝,𝐧)\vdash_{\mathrm{PA}}\mathrm{B}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner,{𝐧}) (since B(⌜…⌝,x)\mathrm{B}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner,x) is a proof operator), so ⊢PAB(⌜α⌝,𝐧)&α\vdash_{\mathrm{PA}}\mathrm{B}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner,{𝐧})\mathbin{\&}\mathrm{\alpha}; while if nn does not code a proof of α\mathrm{\alpha} in PA then ⊢PA∼B(⌜α⌝,𝐧)\vdash_{\mathrm{PA}}\mathord{\sim} \mathrm{B}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner,{𝐧}), so ⊢PA∼(B(⌜α⌝,𝐧)&α)\vdash_{\mathrm{PA}}\mathord{\sim}(\mathrm{B}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner,{𝐧})\mathbin{\&}\mathrm{\alpha}). Now define a new provability operator O+\mathrm{O}^+ as the existential generalization of Pf⁡+[…](x)\operatorname{Pf}^+[\ldots](x). Thus where xx is not free in α\mathrm{\alpha}

O+α=df(∃x)(B(⌜α⌝,x)&α)\mathrm{O}^+\mathrm{\alpha} =_{\mathrm{df}} (\exists x)(\mathrm{B}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner,x)\mathbin{\&}\mathrm{\alpha})

Since xx is not free in α\mathrm{\alpha}, O+α\mathrm{O}^+\mathrm{\alpha} is provably equivalent in PA to Bew⁡(⌜α⌝)&α)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner)\mathbin{\&}\mathrm{\alpha}). Trivially, ⊢PA∼(Bew⁡(⌜0=1⌝)&0=1)\vdash_{\mathrm{PA}}\mathord{\sim}(\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner)\mathbin{\&} 0=1), i.e. ⊢PA∼O+(0=1)\vdash_{\mathrm{PA}}\mathord{\sim} \mathrm{O}^+(0=1). Thus the second incompleteness theorem does not apply to O+\mathrm{O}^+, even though O+\mathrm{O}^+ is the existential generalization of a proof operator. Like O◇\mathrm{O}^◇, O+\mathrm{O}^+ satisfies the principle O∼O⊥\mathrm{O}\mathord{\sim} \mathrm{O}\bot, which is not satisfied by the coextensive operator Bew⁡(⌜…⌝)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner).

The existence of deviant metamathematical codings was recognized early on. Kreisel (1953) showed that a problem of Henkin’s could be resolved either way by alternative codings. Takeuti (1955) gave a Gentzen-style cut-free formulation of arithmetic in which the natural coding of the unprovability of 0=10=1 is provable. Feferman (1960) discussed the problem of alternative presentations of formal systems in detail, and demonstrated the provability in PA of a deviant coding of consistency for the usual formulation of PA. Kreisel (1965, pp. 153–154) advocated the use of a notion of the canonical presentation of a formal system, a programme developed by Feferman (1982). If one can isolate the features of a coding relevant to the derivation of the second incompleteness theorem for PA, that analysis will carry with it a generalization of the theorem to other systems (the latter is the aspect of the problem with which Gödel was concerned in the footnote he added to the translation of Gödel 1932 in van Heijenoort 1967, discussed in Feferman 1990).

The usual contemporary response appeals to the Hilbert-Bernays-Löb derivability conditions, first isolated by Löb (1955; see e.g. Boolos 1993, p. 16). These are conditions on a predicate (as contrasted with an operator) F\mathrm{F} in the language L\mathrm{L} of a theory S\mathrm{S}, given a quotation functor ⌜…⌝\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner in L\mathrm{L} from sentences to singular terms. They state that for all sentences α\mathrm{\alpha} and β\mathrm{\beta} of L\mathrm{L}:

(1)(1)
If ⊢Sα\vdash_{\mathrm{S}}\mathrm{\alpha} then ⊢SF(⌜α⌝)\vdash_{\mathrm{S}} \mathrm{F}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner)
(2)(2)
⊢SF(⌜α⊃β⌝)⊃(F(⌜α⌝)⊃F(⌜β⌝))\vdash_{\mathrm{S}} \mathrm{F}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\supset\mathrm{\beta}\urcorner)\supset(\mathrm{F}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner)\supset \mathrm{F}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\beta}\urcorner))
(3)(3)
⊢S(F(⌜α⌝)⊃F(⌜F(⌜α⌝)⌝)\vdash_{\mathrm{S}} (\mathrm{F}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner)\supset \mathrm{F}(\ulcorner \mathrm{F}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner)\urcorner)

If S\mathrm{S} satisfies (1)–(3) and a specifiable modicum of arithmetic can be embedded in S\mathrm{S}, then ⊢S∼F(⌜⊥⌝)\vdash_{\mathrm{S}}\mathord{\sim} \mathrm{F}(\ulcorner\bot\urcorner) only if ⊢S⊥\vdash_{\mathrm{S}}\bot. This account lacks generality in at least one respect, because it does not make explicit the relevant features of the quotation functor ⌜…⌝\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner. What is really required is just that L\mathrm{L} should contain a function symbol #\# such that for all formulas α\mathrm{\alpha}, β\mathrm{\beta} and γ\mathrm{\gamma} of L\mathrm{L}:

(4)(4)
If the result of substituting ⌜β⌝\ulcorner\mathrm{\beta}\urcorner for all free variables in α\mathrm{\alpha} is γ\mathrm{\gamma} then ⊢S⌜α⌝#⌜β⌝=⌜γ⌝\vdash_{\mathrm{S}}\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner\mathbin{\#}\ulcorner\mathrm{\beta}\urcorner=\ulcorner\mathrm{\gamma}\urcorner

No more arithmetic is needed in S\mathrm{S}. The required background logic is simply:

(5)(5)
If α\mathrm{\alpha} is a truth-functional tautology then ⊢Sα\vdash_{\mathrm{S}}\mathrm{\alpha}
(6)(6)
If ⊢Sα⊃β\vdash_{\mathrm{S}}\mathrm{\alpha}\supset\mathrm{\beta} and ⊢Sα\vdash_{\mathrm{S}}\mathrm{\alpha} then ⊢Sβ\vdash_{\mathrm{S}}\mathrm{\beta}
(7)(7)
⊢Ss=t⊃(α(s)≡α(t))\vdash_{\mathrm{S}} s=t\supset(\mathrm{\alpha}(s)\equiv\mathrm{\alpha}(t))

In (7), ss and tt are singular terms and the formula α(t)\mathrm{\alpha}(t) differs from α(s)\mathrm{\alpha}(s) only in having tt somewhere where the latter has ss. If S\mathrm{S} satisfies (1)–(7), then ⊢S∼F(⌜⊥⌝)\vdash_{\mathrm{S}}\mathord{\sim} \mathrm{F}(\ulcorner\bot\urcorner) only if ⊢S⊥\vdash_{\mathrm{S}}\bot.

The crucial point is that (4)–(7) permit one to derive Gödel’s diagonal lemma for S\mathrm{S}, that for every one-place predicate F(…)\mathrm{F}(\ldots) in L\mathrm{L}, there is a closed formula α\mathrm{\alpha} of L\mathrm{L} such that ⊢Sα≡F(⌜α⌝)\vdash_{\mathrm{S}}\mathrm{\alpha}\equiv \mathrm{F}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner). For the result of substituting the term ⌜F(x#x)⌝\ulcorner \mathrm{F}(x\mathbin{\#}x)\urcorner for the free variable in F(x#x)\mathrm{F}(x\mathbin{\#}x) is F(⌜F(x#x)⌝#⌜F(x#x)⌝)\mathrm{F}(\ulcorner \mathrm{F}(x\mathbin{\#}x)\urcorner\mathbin{\#}\ulcorner \mathrm{F}(x\mathbin{\#}x)\urcorner). Thus by (4)

⊢S⌜F(x#x)⌝#⌜F(x#x)⌝=⌜F(⌜F(x#x)⌝#⌜F(x#x)⌝)⌝\vdash_{\mathrm{S}}\ulcorner \mathrm{F}(x\mathbin{\#}x)\urcorner\mathbin{\#}\ulcorner \mathrm{F}(x\mathbin{\#}x)\urcorner=\ulcorner \mathrm{F}(\ulcorner \mathrm{F}(x\mathbin{\#}x)\urcorner\mathbin{\#}\ulcorner \mathrm{F}(x\mathbin{\#}x)\urcorner)\urcorner.

(7) then gives the required biconditional:

⊢SF(⌜F(x#x)⌝#⌜F(x#x)⌝)≡F(⌜F(⌜F(x#x)⌝#⌜F(x#x)⌝)⌝)\vdash_{\mathrm{S}} \mathrm{F}(\ulcorner \mathrm{F}(x\mathbin{\#}x)\urcorner\mathbin{\#}\ulcorner \mathrm{F}(x\mathbin{\#}x)\urcorner)\equiv \mathrm{F}(\ulcorner \mathrm{F}(\ulcorner \mathrm{F}(x\mathbin{\#}x)\urcorner\mathbin{\#}\ulcorner \mathrm{F}(x\mathbin{\#}x)\urcorner)\urcorner).

From the diagonal lemma and (1)–(3) (and, of course, (5)–(6)) one can prove Löb’s theorem, that ⊢SF(⌜α⌝)⊃α\vdash_{\mathrm{S}} \mathrm{F}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner)\supset\mathrm{\alpha} only if ⊢Sα\vdash_{\mathrm{S}}\mathrm{\alpha} (for a clear presentation see Boolos 1993, pp. 56–57). The second incompleteness theorem is just the special case of Löb’s theorem where α=⊥\mathrm{\alpha}=\bot.

What (1)–(7) do not provide are general sufficient conditions on an arbitrary sentential operator O\mathrm{O} for the derivability of the second incompleteness theorem. It would be naive simply to replace the operator F(⌜…⌝)\mathrm{F}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner) by O…\mathrm{O}\ldots in (1)–(3), giving:

(1′)(1′)
If ⊢Sα\vdash_{\mathrm{S}}\mathrm{\alpha} then ⊢SOα\vdash_{\mathrm{S}} \mathrm{O}\mathrm{\alpha}
(2′)(2′)
⊢SO(α⊃β)⊃(Oα⊃Oβ)\vdash_{\mathrm{S}} \mathrm{O}(\mathrm{\alpha}\supset\mathrm{\beta})\supset(\mathrm{O}\mathrm{\alpha}\supset \mathrm{O}\mathrm{\beta})
(3′)(3′)
⊢SOα⊃OOα\vdash_{\mathrm{S}} \mathrm{O}\mathrm{\alpha}\supset \mathrm{OO}\mathrm{\alpha}

The claim is false that whenever S\mathrm{S} satisfies (1′)–(3′) and (4)–(7), ⊢S∼O⊥\vdash_{\mathrm{S}}\mathord{\sim} \mathrm{O}\bot only if ⊢S⊥\vdash_{\mathrm{S}}\bot. For if O\mathrm{O} is a redundant operator, so that Oα\mathrm{O}\mathrm{\alpha} is provably equivalent to α\mathrm{\alpha}, then (5)–(6) imply both (1′)–(3′) and the condition that ⊢S∼O⊥\vdash_{\mathrm{S}}\mathord{\sim} \mathrm{O}\bot; thus the claim implies that S\mathrm{S} satisfies (4)–(7) only if S\mathrm{S} is inconsistent, to which PA is an obvious counterexample. The attempt to prove the claim breaks down at the application of the diagonal lemma. Although the lemma itself is still derivable, it does not provide the required formula α\mathrm{\alpha} such that ⊢Sα≡∼Oα\vdash_{\mathrm{S}}\mathrm{\alpha}\equiv\mathord{\sim} \mathrm{O}\mathrm{\alpha} (corresponding to the α\mathrm{\alpha} such that ⊢Sα≡∼F(⌜α⌝)\vdash_{\mathrm{S}}\mathrm{\alpha}\equiv\mathord{\sim} \mathrm{F}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner) in the proof of the incompleteness theorem), and when O\mathrm{O} is redundant such an α\mathrm{\alpha} exists only if S\mathrm{S} is inconsistent. The redundant operator cannot be factorized into any predicate T\mathrm{T} and the quotation functor ⌜…⌝\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner in PA, for otherwise α≡T(⌜α⌝)\mathrm{\alpha}\equiv \mathrm{T}(\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner) would be true for each formula α\mathrm{\alpha} in LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}}, contrary to Tarski’s theorem on the undefinability of truth in LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}}.

Even when O…\mathrm{O}\ldots is coextensive with Bew⁡(⌜…⌝)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner), (1′)–(3′) are not sufficient for O…\mathrm{O}\ldots to satisfy the second incompleteness theorem when S=PA\mathrm{S}=\mathrm{PA} (which satisfies (4)–(7)). The operator O+\mathrm{O}^+ defined above is a counterexample. That it satisfies (1′)–(3′) is an easy corollary of the fact that Bew⁡(⌜…⌝)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner) does. Thus O+α\mathrm{O}^+\mathrm{\alpha} is not equivalent in PA to the application of a predicate to ⌜α⌝\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner. The source of the trouble is obviously the second conjunct of O+α\mathrm{O}^+\mathrm{\alpha}, namely α\mathrm{\alpha}. In contrast, O◇α\mathrm{O}^◇\mathrm{\alpha} is equivalent in PA to the application of a predicate to ⌜α⌝\ulcorner\mathrm{\alpha}\urcorner, but O◇\mathrm{O}^◇ does not satisfy (1′).

A systematic account of the principles satisfied by the various provability operators can be given within the framework of propositional modal logic (see Boolos 1993 for the relevant background and results on the modal logic of provability). Its language L□\mathrm{L}_□ has infinitely many propositional variables p0,p1,p2,…p_0,p_1,p_2,\ldots, with material implication ⊃\supset, a falsity constant ⊥\bot and the box □□ as the only primitive operators; pp and qq will be used as metalinguistic variables ranging over distinct propositional variables of the object-language. For simplicity, assume that ⊃\supset and ⊥\bot are also in LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}}. Given a sentential operator O\mathrm{O} in LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}}, define an O\mathrm{O}-translation to be any mapping *\text{*} of formulas of L□\mathrm{L}_□ to closed formulas of LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}} such that for all formulas α\mathrm{\alpha} and β\mathrm{\beta} of L□\mathrm{L}_□, *⊥=⊥\text{*}\bot=\bot, *(α⊃β)=*α⊃*β\text{*}(\mathrm{\alpha}\supset\mathrm{\beta})=\text{*}\mathrm{\alpha}\supset\text{*}\mathrm{\beta} and *□α=O*α\text{*}□\mathrm{\alpha}=\mathrm{O}\text{*}\mathrm{\alpha}. Intuitively, *\text{*} replaces □□ by O\mathrm{O} and propositional variables by sentences of LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}} in a uniform way. For any formula α\mathrm{\alpha} of L□\mathrm{L}_□, say that O\mathrm{O} satisfies α\mathrm{\alpha} just in case *α\text{*}\mathrm{\alpha} is true (in a standard model of PA) for each O\mathrm{O}-translation *\text{*}. In effect, the propositional variables of L□\mathrm{L}_□ are universally quantified over all closed formulas of LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}}.

It is convenient to begin by considering provable satisfaction, where O\mathrm{O} provably satisfies α\mathrm{\alpha} just in case *α\text{*}\mathrm{\alpha} is provable in PA for each O\mathrm{O}-translation *\text{*}. By (1)–(3) for Bew\operatorname{Bew} and Löb’s theorem, Bew⁡(⌜…⌝)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner) provably satisfies all theorems of the system GL (= KW) of provability logic (‘G’ for Gödel, ‘L’ for Löb); likewise for any system satisfying (1)–(7) in place of PA. To axiomatize GL, take all truth-functional tautologies and all formulas of the forms □(α⊃β)⊃(□α⊃□β)□(\mathrm{\alpha}\supset\mathrm{\beta})\supset(□\mathrm{\alpha}\supset□\mathrm{\beta}) and □(□α⊃α)⊃□α□(□\mathrm{\alpha}\supset\mathrm{\alpha})\supset□\mathrm{\alpha} (Löb’s axiom) as the axioms and modus ponens (MP) and necessitation (i.e. the rule that if α\mathrm{\alpha} is a theorem then so is □α□\mathrm{\alpha}) as the rules of inference. Solovay (1976) proved the converse for PA: if Bew⁡(⌜…⌝)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner) provably satisfies α\mathrm{\alpha}, then ⊢GLα\vdash_{\mathrm{GL}}\mathrm{\alpha}.

Now axiomatize an extension GLS of GL (‘S’ for Solovay) by taking all theorems of GL and all formulas of the form □α⊃α□\mathrm{\alpha}\supset\mathrm{\alpha} as the axioms and MP as the only rule of inference (since GL is decidable, the axiomatization of GLS is recursive). Since everything provable in PA is true, Bew⁡(⌜…⌝)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner) satisfies every theorem of GLS. GLS is not closed under necessitation, for ∼□⊥\mathord{\sim}□\bot is a theorem but □∼□⊥□\mathord{\sim}□\bot is not (its negation is); although ∼Bew⁡(⌜⊥⌝)\mathord{\sim}\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\bot\urcorner) is true, it is not provable in PA. Again, Solovay (1976) proved the converse result: if Bew⁡(⌜…⌝)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner) satisfies α\mathrm{\alpha}, then ⊢GLSα\vdash_{\mathrm{GLS}}\mathrm{\alpha}. Thus the theorems of GLS are exactly the principles that Bew⁡(⌜…⌝)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner) satisfies.

As for the deviant provability operator O+\mathrm{O}^+, the principles that it satisfies constitute the modal logic Grz (= S4Grz = KGrz, named after Andrzej Grzegorczyk), which can be axiomatized by taking all truth-functional tautologies and all formulas of the forms □(α⊃β)⊃(□α⊃□β)□(\mathrm{\alpha}\supset\mathrm{\beta})\supset(□\mathrm{\alpha}\supset□\mathrm{\beta}) and □(□(α⊃□α)⊃α)⊃α□(□(\mathrm{\alpha}\supset□\mathrm{\alpha})\supset\mathrm{\alpha})\supset\mathrm{\alpha} (Grzegorczyk’s axiom) as the axioms and MP and necessitation as the rules of inference (see Boolos 1993, pp. 155–164, for the relevant results about Grz). Every formula of the form □α⊃α□\mathrm{\alpha}\supset\mathrm{\alpha} is derivable in Grz, and is subject to necessitation. In particular, □(□⊥⊃⊥)□(□\bot\supset\bot) is derivable. Thus □∼□⊥□\mathord{\sim}□\bot is a theorem of Grz but not of GLS, while ∼□∼□⊥\mathord{\sim}□\mathord{\sim}□\bot is a theorem of GLS but not of Grz. O+…\mathrm{O}^+\ldots but not Bew⁡(⌜…⌝)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner) satisfies □∼□⊥□\mathord{\sim}□\bot; Bew⁡(⌜…⌝)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\ldots\urcorner) but not O+…\mathrm{O}^+\ldots satisfies ∼□∼□⊥\mathord{\sim}□\mathord{\sim}□\bot.

The phenomenon is observable within GLS itself. Let ◼α\blacksquare\mathrm{\alpha} abbreviate □α&α□\mathrm{\alpha}\mathbin{\&}\mathrm{\alpha}. Then ◼α≡□α\blacksquare\mathrm{\alpha}\equiv□\mathrm{\alpha} is a theorem of GLS for each formula α\mathrm{\alpha} of L□\mathrm{L}_□. Nevertheless, ◼∼◼⊥\blacksquare\mathord{\sim}\blacksquare\bot but not □∼□⊥□\mathord{\sim}□\bot is a theorem of GLS, as is ∼□∼□⊥\mathord{\sim}□\mathord{\sim}□\bot but not ∼◼∼◼⊥\mathord{\sim}\blacksquare\mathord{\sim}\blacksquare\bot. Unless α\mathrm{\alpha} is a theorem of GLS (indeed, of GL), □(◼α≡□α)□(\blacksquare\mathrm{\alpha}\equiv□\mathrm{\alpha}) is not a theorem of GLS, so ◼\blacksquare and □□ are not interchangeable within modal contexts in GLS.

Such possibilities cast doubt on attempts to use Gödel’s incompleteness theorems to show that humans are not Turing machines (see Lucas 1961 and Penrose 1989, 1994). One argument runs something like this: if I am a Turing machine, then the set of mathematical and metamathematical sentences to which I eventually assent (given enough time and paper) is recursively enumerable, and therefore (it can be shown) recursively axiomatizable; since I assent to a modicum of logic and arithmetic, the second incompleteness theorem applies; but I assent to my own consistency, so it would follow that I am inconsistent, which I am not; therefore I am not a Turing machine. The argument is obviously very loose as it stands. The supposition that I am a Turing machine is quite unclear until my relevant states have been specified; the inference to the recursive enumerability of the set of sentences to which I eventually assent is also dubious until the notion of assent (is it revocable?) and the role of perceptual input have been clarified (a Turing machine that does not halt can write an infinite sequence of symbols that is not recursively enumerable on its tape, if such a sequence is provided for it to read). For the sake of argument, however, grant that these loose ends have been tidied up, and that a sense has been given to ‘I am a Turing machine’ from which it does follow that the set of sentences to which I eventually assent is recursively enumerable. Call the set ‘my system’. It can be recursively axiomatized; note that there is no automatic guarantee that everything provable in my system is true. A standard objection is then that no good reason has been provided to believe that my system is consistent (see Putnam 1975, p. 366). This objection is unsatisfying, however, for the argument from the incompleteness theorem seems to show that I must be inconsistent if I eventually assent to my own consistency; yet it seems that even if I do so I might still be consistent.

A more significant fallacy in the argument is its neglect of the mode of presentation under which I assent to the consistency of my system. The Gödelian consistency sentence makes coded reference to my system by descriptive means: it codes a description of the axioms and inference rules of an axiomatized system that is in fact my own. The consistency sentence to which it seems that I can consistently assent makes reference to my system by indexical means, as my system. There is a corresponding difference between the descriptive provability operator ‘A Turing machine with machine table T\mathrm{T} can prove that …’, where ‘T\mathrm{T}’ abbreviates an appropriate description, and the indexical provability operator ‘I can prove that …’, in a context in which I am (i.e. realize) a Turing machine with machine table T\mathrm{T} (where ‘can prove’ abbreviates something like ‘has a proof in its system’). Although the two operators are extensionally equivalent, they may satisfy different principles under embedding, if I cannot prove that I am a Turing machine with machine table T\mathrm{T}. As Paul Benacerraf pointed out long ago, I might be a Turing machine without knowing which Turing machine I am (Benacerraf 1967). In particular, if I can prove that I cannot prove that 0=10=1, then it follows that a Turing machine with machine table T\mathrm{T} can prove that I cannot prove that 0=10=1, but it does not follow that a Turing machine with machine table T\mathrm{T} can prove that a Turing machine with machine table T\mathrm{T} cannot prove that 0=10=1, just as Bew⁡(⌜∼Bew⁡(⌜⊥⌝)⌝)\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\mathord{\sim}\operatorname{Bew}(\ulcorner\bot\urcorner)\urcorner) was seen above not to follow from O∼O⊥\mathrm{O}\mathord{\sim} \mathrm{O}\bot for an arbitrary provability operator O\mathrm{O} in PA. The present provability operators belong to a more expressive language than LPA\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{PA}}, but the logical point remains the same.

The objection applies even if the argument appeals to the first incompleteness theorem rather than the second. Unlike the latter, the former is not sensitive to differences between coextensive provability operators, because its statement—that in any consistent recursively axiomatizable extension of PA some sentence is neither provable nor disprovable—does not involve embedded occurrences of the provability operator. However, the anti-mechanistic argument claims that I can prove the supposedly undecidable sentence G\mathrm{G} for my system; but the purported proof requires the consistency [ω\mathrm{\omega}-consistency] of my system as a lemma, so the same objection applies.

I might even know that I am a Turing machine without knowing which Turing machine I am (see Reinhardt 1986). In effect, such a possibility is addressed by Penrose (1994, pp. 132–137). Penrose offers only plausibility arguments against it. He finds it unlikely that I could prove each sentence enumerated by a given algorithm without being able to prove the generalization that every sentence enumerated by the algorithm is true, but such claims of likelihood are unreliable in an area so full of surprises. The execution of the algorithm may involve me in a progressively more complex thought procedure for each sentence. There is no guarantee that I can discern the common pattern in these procedures; even if I am told what it is, there is no guarantee that I will be convinced that it always yields correct results, even though, in each particular case, running through the procedure convinces me that that particular result is correct.

The point essentially concerns provability operators, not provability predicates. If Bew⁡I\operatorname{Bew}_{\mathrm{I}} is an indexical provability predicate, S\mathrm{S} is my system, and quotation marks are used in the ordinary way, then Bew⁡I\operatorname{Bew}_{\mathrm{I}} would presumably satisfy the Hilbert-Bernays-Löb derivability conditions (1)–(3). My system presumably satisfies (5)–(7) and provides the elementary syntax needed for (4). However, when diagonalization is applied to formulas involving Bew⁡I\operatorname{Bew}_{\mathrm{I}} in attempt to prove the second incompleteness theorem for Bew⁡I\operatorname{Bew}_{\mathrm{I}}, it yields an intuitively paradoxical sentence whose intended reading is something like ‘I cannot prove this sentence’; in contrast, Gödel’s use of diagonalization yields a sentence whose intended reading makes an ordinary statement about numbers. Not surprisingly, reasoning with self-referential sentences involving Bew⁡I\operatorname{Bew}_{\mathrm{I}} does lead to inconsistency (if consistency is claimed). The use of an indexical provability operator yields no such paradox, because it is not susceptible to diagonalization (these remarks address a worry developed in the appendix to Benacerraf 1967). For example, as noted above, the deviant provability operator O+\mathrm{O}^+ cannot be factorized into a provability predicate and a quotation functor.

The deviant provability operators introduced by Kreisel, Takeuti and Feferman are defined from provability predicates, and so do not satisfy (1)–(3), otherwise the second incompleteness theorem would be derivable for them. They are therefore less threatening than O+\mathrm{O}^+ to the anti-mechanistic argument, since they seem to differ so markedly from the indexical provability operator in the satisfaction of principles. Nevertheless, it would be interesting to know what deviant systems of modal logic are constituted by the principles that they do satisfy.

Example 4 involves metaphysical rather than epistemic or doxastic modalities. Nathan Salmon has argued that there are counterexamples concerning the possible constitution of artifacts to the analogue of the KK principle for metaphysical necessity, i.e. to the 4 axiom □p⊃□□p□ p\supset□□ p (equivalently, ◇◇p⊃◇p◇◇ p\supset◇ p) familiar from the modal system S4 (= KT4; ◇◇ is throughout a metalogical abbreviation for ∼□∼\mathord{\sim}□\mathord{\sim}). The soundness of his argument is not at issue here. (See his 1989 and, for some objections, Williamson 1990, pp. 126–143, to which Salmon 1993 is a reply. A reply to this must await another occasion.) What is relevant is one part of his response to those who find the modal system S5 (= KT5) compelling as a logic for metaphysical necessity. S5 adds to the system S4 the other half of what is required for necessity always to be a non-contingent feature, the 5 (= E) axiom ∼□p⊃□∼□p\mathord{\sim}□ p\supset□\mathord{\sim}□ p (equivalently, ◇□p⊃□p◇□ p\supset□ p). S5 is the logic of necessity as truth in all possible worlds, on the assumption that it is not contingent what worlds are possible. Strictly, S5 also embodies the assumption that the actual world is possible: it contains the T axiom □p⊃p□ p\supset p (equivalently, p⊃◇pp\supset◇ p). A logic stronger than S5 would result if and only if a specific finite upper bound to the number of possible worlds were imposed in addition to the T and 5 axioms (for the ‘only if’ direction, use Scroggs 1951; the ‘if’ direction is routine). Such a bound is surely incorrect when □□ is read as metaphysical necessity: for each natural number nn, it is metaphysically possible for there to be exactly nn planets.

Salmon suggests that those who find S5 compelling as a logic for metaphysical necessity confuse necessity with actual necessity, i.e. with the property of being necessary in this, the actual world (1989, p. 30). For even if it is contingent whether it is necessary that P\mathrm{P}, it is non-contingent whether it is actually necessary that P\mathrm{P}, simply because it is non-contingent whether anything is actually the case. What is the case relative to a given world is not itself a world-relative matter. The logic of actual necessity is, uncontentiously, S5; necessity is easily confused with actual necessity, for the two are in some sense a priori equivalent (see Davies and Humberstone 1980 for some relevant background on ‘actually’).

To be more precise: First expand L□\mathrm{L}_□ to a language L□@\mathrm{L}_{□@} by adding a new primitive operator @@, to be read as ‘actually’. Axiomatize a preliminary bimodal system KT@ for necessity and actuality by taking all truth-functional tautologies and all formulas of the forms □(α⊃β)⊃(□α⊃□β)□(\mathrm{\alpha}\supset\mathrm{\beta})\supset(□\mathrm{\alpha}\supset□\mathrm{\beta}), □α⊃α□\mathrm{\alpha}\supset\mathrm{\alpha}, @(α⊃β)⊃(@α⊃@β)@(\mathrm{\alpha}\supset\mathrm{\beta})\supset(@\mathrm{\alpha}\supset@\mathrm{\beta}), @∼a≡∼@α@\mathord{\sim}\mathrm{a}\equiv\mathord{\sim}@\mathrm{\alpha}, @α⊃@@α@\mathrm{\alpha}\supset@@\mathrm{\alpha} and @α⊃□@α@\mathrm{\alpha}\supset□@\mathrm{\alpha} as the axioms and MP, necessitation and actualization (i.e. the rule that if α\mathrm{\alpha} is a theorem then so is @α@\mathrm{\alpha}) as the rules of inference. KT@ is a conservative extension of the familiar modal system KT (= T), in the sense that the theorems of KT@ in which @@ does not occur are just the theorems of KT. To axiomatize KT, drop all the axiom schemata in which @@ occurs and the rule of actualization from the axiomatization of KT@. KT and KT@ correspond to an interpretation of □□ on which □α□\mathrm{\alpha} is true at a world ww just in case α\mathrm{\alpha} is true at all worlds accessible from ww, where the only constraint on accessibility is that each world is to be accessible from itself. The theorems of KT are uncontentious when □□ is read as metaphysical necessity. KT@ corresponds to an interpretation of @@ on which @α@\mathrm{\alpha} is true at a world ww just in case α\mathrm{\alpha} is true at a fixed world aa. However, its theorems are the formulas guaranteed to be true at all worlds; thus aa is in no way privileged as the actual world in the semantics of KT@. It is privileged in the system KT@S, whose theorems are the formulas guaranteed to be true at aa itself. KT@S is an extension of KT@ (‘S’ for Solovay, since the construction of KT@S from KT@ resembles that of GLS from GL). To axiomatize KT@S, take all theorems of KT@ and all formulas of the form @α⊃α@\mathrm{\alpha}\supset\mathrm{\alpha} as the axioms and MP as the rule of inference (since KT@ is decidable, the axiomatization is recursive). KT@S is not closed under necessitation, for @p⊃p@p\supset p is a theorem but □(@p⊃p)□(@p\supset p) is not. @p⊃p@p\supset p is guaranteed to hold at the actual world, but not at all possible worlds.

A different way to define an operator that satisfies the S4 axiom out of one that does not is to let □*α□\text{*}\mathrm{\alpha} be the infinite conjunction of α,□α,□□α,…\mathrm{\alpha},□\mathrm{\alpha},□□\mathrm{\alpha},\ldots. The accessibility relation for □*□\text{*} is the reflexive ancestral, which is automatically transitive, of the accessibility relation for □□. This construction does not serve Salmon’s purposes, however, for he also denies the logical truth of the B axiom p⊃□◇pp\supset□◇ p, which is derivable in S5 (it is not even entirely clear why Salmon should regard the T axiom as a logical truth; its obviousness alone does not qualify it as such). The B axiom is not guaranteed to hold for □*□\text{*}: it corresponds to the symmetry of the accessibility relation, and the reflexive ancestral of a non-symmetric relation may itself be non-symmetric.

What must be shown is that S5 is the logic of @□@□ in KT@S. More precisely, it must be shown that if α\mathrm{\alpha} is any formula of L□\mathrm{L}_□ and ^α\text{\textasciicircum}\mathrm{\alpha} is the result of replacing each occurrence of □□ in α\mathrm{\alpha} by @□@□, then α\mathrm{\alpha} is a theorem of S5 if and only if ^α\text{\textasciicircum}\mathrm{\alpha} is a theorem of KT@S. (Some related and more general results are discussed in §5.) Although the operators □□ and @□@□ are coextensive in the set of all models of KT@S, because □α≡@□α□\mathrm{\alpha}\equiv@□\mathrm{\alpha} is a theorem for every formula α\mathrm{\alpha}, they satisfy different principles. But in a language in which @@ is the only primitive operator other than the usual truth-functional ones, with a semantics on which @@ is coextensive with the identity operator, the two operators satisfy exactly the same principles. Example 5. A similar phenomenon can arise even when the coextensive operators are in some sense synonymous. Consider a language L\mathrm{L} with atomic sentences q0,q1,q2,…q_0,q_1,q_2,\ldots, the truth-functional operators ⊃\supset and ⊥\bot, and two further operators O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2. ⟨X,Y⟩⊩O1α\langle \mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}\rangle\Vdash \mathrm{O}_1\mathrm{\alpha} just in case α∈Y\mathrm{\alpha}\in \mathrm{Y}
⟨X,Y⟩⊩O2α\langle \mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}\rangle\Vdash \mathrm{O}_2\mathrm{\alpha} just in case α∈Y\mathrm{\alpha}\in \mathrm{Y}

Since the clauses for O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 have exactly the same right-hand side, one might regard O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 as synonymous, and conclude that they are interchangeable in all contexts salva veritate. Any formula of the form O1α≡O2α\mathrm{O}_1\mathrm{\alpha}\equiv \mathrm{O}_2\mathrm{\alpha} is indeed true at any model, so O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 are coextensive. However, if O1q∈Y\mathrm{O}_1q\in \mathrm{Y} but O2q∉Y\mathrm{O}_2q\notin \mathrm{Y}, then ⟨X,Y⟩⊩O1O1q\langle \mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}\rangle\Vdash \mathrm{O}_1\mathrm{O}_1q but not ⟨X,Y⟩⊩O2O2q\langle \mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}\rangle\Vdash \mathrm{O}_2\mathrm{O}_2q. It does not immediately follow that O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 satisfy different principles with respect to the set of all models; indeed, if no further restrictions are imposed on the models, the symmetrical treatment of O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 will ensure that they satisfy the same principles. But if every model ⟨X,Y⟩\langle \mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}\rangle meets the condition that O1α∈Y\mathrm{O}_1\mathrm{\alpha}\in \mathrm{Y} and O2α∉Y\mathrm{O}_2\mathrm{\alpha}\notin \mathrm{Y}, then O1\mathrm{O}_1 will satisfy the principle OOp\mathrm{OO}p while O2\mathrm{O}_2 satisfies the principle ∼OOp\mathord{\sim} \mathrm{OO}p.

Of course, if the condition that O1α∈Y\mathrm{O}_1\mathrm{\alpha}\in \mathrm{Y} and O2α∉Y\mathrm{O}_2\mathrm{\alpha}\notin \mathrm{Y} followed from the semantics of O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2, then they would not be synonymous. But the asymmetry could arise in other ways. For example, if the models are just contexts of utterance in the actual world, then the asymmetry might arise from mundane facts.

For any particular application of the formal semantics, some explanation would naturally be needed of the significance of the second element Y\mathrm{Y} of a model. If the explanation permits a model ⟨X,Y⟩\langle \mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}\rangle for which O1q∈Y\mathrm{O}_1q\in \mathrm{Y} but O2q∉Y\mathrm{O}_2q\notin \mathrm{Y}, so ⟨X,Y⟩⊩O1O1q\langle \mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}\rangle\Vdash \mathrm{O}_1\mathrm{O}_1q and ⟨X,Y⟩⊩O2O1q\langle \mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}\rangle\Vdash \mathrm{O}_2\mathrm{O}_1q but neither ⟨X,Y⟩⊩O1O2q\langle \mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}\rangle\Vdash \mathrm{O}_1\mathrm{O}_2q nor ⟨X,Y⟩⊩O2O2q\langle \mathrm{X},\mathrm{Y}\rangle\Vdash \mathrm{O}_2\mathrm{O}_2q, then it is natural to conclude that O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 have quotational features, since each yields two sentences of different truth-value when applied to the synonymous sentences O1q\mathrm{O}_1q and O2q\mathrm{O}_2q. For example, Y\mathrm{Y} might contain all sentences uttered at a given time and place. Thus O1α\mathrm{O}_1\mathrm{\alpha} and O1β\mathrm{O}_1\mathrm{\beta} may differ in meaning when α\mathrm{\alpha} and β\mathrm{\beta} have the same meaning; likewise for O2\mathrm{O}_2. For this reason, one may be unwilling to class O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 as propositional operators; nevertheless, they remain well-defined operators on sentences (for a related discussion see Cresswell 1983). Note that Examples 1–4 do not depend on quotationality.

2. A generalization

It is time to give a precise general statement of the phenomenon exemplified in the previous section. Start with an interpreted language L\mathrm{L}, assumed to contain at least the truth-functional operators ⊃\supset and ⊥\bot. Suppose that the sentences of L\mathrm{L} are evaluated as true or false at members of a set I\mathrm{I}, known as indices. They can be possible worlds, contexts of utterance, ordered pairs of worlds and contexts, or anything else; their role is simply to give the account generality. When the generality is not wanted, I\mathrm{I} can have just one member. In Examples 1–3 one can take the only index to be, respectively, a context of utterance in which I (the speaker) am the man with a hole in his pocket, a world in which Socrates is wise, and a context of utterance in which I am a Turing machine with machine table T\mathrm{T}. ⊥\bot and ⊃\supset will be assumed to behave according to their usual truth-tables at each index ii: ⊥\bot is not true at ii and α⊃β\mathrm{\alpha}\supset\mathrm{\beta} is true at ii if and only if either α\mathrm{\alpha} is not true at ii or β\mathrm{\beta} is true at ii, for all sentences α\mathrm{\alpha} and β\mathrm{\beta} of L\mathrm{L}.

If O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 are sentence operators of L\mathrm{L}, say that O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 are coextensive just in case, for every sentence α\mathrm{\alpha} of L\mathrm{L} and every index ii, O1α\mathrm{O}_1\mathrm{\alpha} is true at ii if and only if O2α\mathrm{O}_2\mathrm{\alpha} is true at ii (since the indices will sometimes be possible worlds, it would be just as appropriate to speak of cointensiveness). The indices are not required to include all the points at which the semantics of L\mathrm{L} evaluates its sentences; rather, they are a subset of such points in which we happen to be interested. For example, in Example 4 the semantics of the modal language L\mathrm{L} with the ‘actually’ operator may be two-dimensional, evaluating sentences at pairs of worlds ⟨w,x⟩\langle w,x\rangle, of which ww is the context of utterance and xx the context of evaluation proper; thus @α@\mathrm{\alpha} is true at ⟨w,x⟩\langle w,x\rangle if and only if α\mathrm{\alpha} is true at ⟨w,w⟩\langle w,w\rangle. If we are interested in the evaluation of utterances at the context in which they are made, we can take the indices to be the pairs ⟨w,w⟩\langle w,w\rangle ‘on the diagonal’, with respect to which □□ and @□@□ are coextensive. They are not coextensive if all pairs are taken as indices.

As in Example 3, a principle is a formula of L□\mathrm{L}_□. If O\mathrm{O} is a sentence operator of L\mathrm{L}, an O\mathrm{O}-translation is a mapping *\text{*} from formulas of L□\mathrm{L}_□ to sentences of L\mathrm{L} such that for all formulas α\mathrm{\alpha} and β\mathrm{\beta} of L□\mathrm{L}_□, *(α⊃β)=*α⊃*β\text{*}(\mathrm{\alpha}\supset\mathrm{\beta})=\text{*}\mathrm{\alpha}\supset\text{*}\mathrm{\beta}, *⊥=⊥\text{*}\bot=\bot and *□α=O*α\text{*}□\mathrm{\alpha}=\mathrm{O}\text{*}\mathrm{\alpha}. O\mathrm{O} satisfies a principle α\mathrm{\alpha} just in case *α\text{*}\mathrm{\alpha} is true at every index ii for every O\mathrm{O}-translation *\text{*}. The phenomenon of interest is just that not all coextensive operators satisfy the same principles.

The choice of L□\mathrm{L}_□ to record principles is purely notational: it does not assume the operator O\mathrm{O} to have anything in common with a necessity operator beyond being a one-place sentential operator. The modal language has the merit of familiarity; many of its formulas and sets of formulas already have names.

Coextensiveness and satisfaction of the same principles are conditions at the same level of generality; they are defined by quantification over the same set of indices. If coextensiveness had been defined by quantification only over actual items of some kind, and satisfaction of the same principles by quantification over all logically possible items of that kind, then the failure of the former to imply the latter would not have been surprising; but there is no such disparity in the definitions above.

The principles over which coextensive operators can differ always involve some self-embedding of □□. If a first-degree principle is a formula of L□\mathrm{L}_□ in which no occurrence of □□ lies within the scope of another, then coextensive operators O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 satisfy exactly the same first-degree principles. For let *1\text{*}_1 be any O1\mathrm{O}_1-translation, and let *2\text{*}_2 be the O2\mathrm{O}_2-translation that agrees with *1\text{*}_1 on propositional variables. If α\mathrm{\alpha} does not contain □□, then *1α=*2α\text{*}_1\mathrm{\alpha}=\text{*}_2\mathrm{\alpha}, so *1□α\text{*}_1□\mathrm{\alpha} (=O1*1α=\mathrm{O}_1\text{*}_1\mathrm{\alpha}) and *2□α\text{*}_2□\mathrm{\alpha} (=O2*2α=O2*1α=\mathrm{O}_2\text{*}_2\mathrm{\alpha}=\mathrm{O}_2\text{*}_1\mathrm{\alpha}) are true at the same indices by coextensiveness, so *1α\text{*}_1\mathrm{\alpha} is true at the same indices as *2α\text{*}_2\mathrm{\alpha} for any first-degree formula α\mathrm{\alpha}. Thus if O2\mathrm{O}_2 satisfies a first degree formula, then so too does O1\mathrm{O}_1. The converse follows by a parallel argument. But even the simplest kind of self-embedding, as in □□p□□ p, permits coextensive operators to differ in satisfaction of principles.

One limit to the phenomenon of coextensiveness without satisfaction of the same principles is that it cannot involve truth-functional operators. If O\mathrm{O} is truth-functional, then any operator coextensive with O\mathrm{O} satisfies the same principles as O\mathrm{O}. In the present context, it is natural to stipulate that an operator O\mathrm{O} in L\mathrm{L} is truth-functional just in case for all sentences α\mathrm{\alpha} and β\mathrm{\beta} of L\mathrm{L} and all indices ii and jj, if the truth-value of α\mathrm{\alpha} at ii is the truth-value of β\mathrm{\beta} at jj then the truth-value of Oα\mathrm{O}\mathrm{\alpha} at ii is the truth-value of Oβ\mathrm{O}\mathrm{\beta} at jj. However, the limit to the phenomenon is more general than that. Say that O\mathrm{O} is extensional just in case for all sentences α\mathrm{\alpha} and β\mathrm{\beta} of L\mathrm{L} and all indices ii, if the truth-value of α\mathrm{\alpha} at ii is the truth-value of β\mathrm{\beta} at ii then the truth-value of Oα\mathrm{O}\mathrm{\alpha} at ii is the truth-value of Oβ\mathrm{O}\mathrm{\beta} at ii, in other words, just in case O\mathrm{O} satisfies the principle (p≡q)⊃(□p≡□q)(p\equiv q)\supset(□ p\equiv□ q). All truth-functional operators are extensional, but not all extensional operators are truth-functional. For example, let O…\mathrm{O}\ldots be the material biconditional ‘… if and only if it is raining’, and let I\mathrm{I} contain both an index ii at which it is raining and an index jj at which it is not raining; then, where ⊤\top is ⊥⊃⊥\bot\supset\bot, O⊤\mathrm{O}\top is true at ii and false at jj, even though ⊤\top has the same truth-value at ii and jj, so O\mathrm{O} is not truth-functional; nevertheless, O\mathrm{O} is obviously extensional. The example also shows that no principle characterizes truth-functionality in the way that (p≡q)⊃(□p≡□q)(p\equiv q)\supset(□ p\equiv□ q) characterizes extensionality: if there were such a principle, O\mathrm{O} would satisfy it too. (For a thorough discussion of a distinction closely related to that between truth-functionality and extensionality see Humberstone 1986. The present phenomenon is closely related to the fact, stated in his terminology, that if O1α\mathrm{O}_1\mathrm{\alpha} and O2α\mathrm{O}_2\mathrm{\alpha} always imply each other in a logic, it does not follow that each of O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 is a subconnective of the other. He points out that if O1α\mathrm{O}_1\mathrm{\alpha} always implies O2α\mathrm{O}_2\mathrm{\alpha}, it does not follow that O1\mathrm{O}_1 is a subconnective of O2\mathrm{O}_2 (1986, p. 41). Note that his notions, unlike the present ones, are defined relative to a logic.) One limit to the phenomenon is this: if O\mathrm{O} is extensional, then any operator coextensive with O\mathrm{O} satisfies the same principles as O\mathrm{O}.

To establish the point, argue as follows. Let O\mathrm{O} be any extensional operator in L\mathrm{L}, and O′\mathrm{O}' any operator in L\mathrm{L} coextensive with O\mathrm{O}. Let *\text{*} be any O\mathrm{O}-translation, and *′\text{*}' the O′\mathrm{O}'-translation such that *p=*′p\text{*}p=\text{*}'p for each propositional variable pp. It can be shown by induction on the complexity of α∈L□\mathrm{\alpha}\in \mathrm{L}_□ that for any index ii, the truth-value of *α\text{*}\mathrm{\alpha} at ii is the truth-value of *′α\text{*}'\mathrm{\alpha} at ii. The only interesting case is the induction step for □□. Suppose that the truth-value of *α\text{*}\mathrm{\alpha} at ii is the truth-value of *′α\text{*}'\mathrm{\alpha} at ii (the induction hypothesis). Then the truth-value of *□α\text{*}□\mathrm{\alpha} at ii is the truth-value of O*α\mathrm{O}\text{*}\mathrm{\alpha} at ii (since *\text{*} is an O\mathrm{O}-translation), which is the truth-value of O*′α\mathrm{O}\text{*}'\mathrm{\alpha} at ii (from the induction hypothesis, since O\mathrm{O} is extensional), which is the truth-value of O′*′α\mathrm{O}'\text{*}'\mathrm{\alpha} at ii (since O\mathrm{O} and O′\mathrm{O}' are coextensive), which is the truth-value of *′□α\text{*}'□\mathrm{\alpha} at ii (since *′\text{*}' is a O′\mathrm{O}'-translation), as required. It follows that if O′\mathrm{O}' satisfies the principle α\mathrm{\alpha}, then so too does O′\mathrm{O}'. Conversely, by a parallel argument, if O\mathrm{O} satisfies α\mathrm{\alpha}, then so too does O′\mathrm{O}'. Thus O\mathrm{O} and O′\mathrm{O}' satisfy the same principles, QED.

Perhaps a more intuitive way of making the point is this. Evidently, any operator coextensive with an extensional operator is itself extensional. Now at any given index a (singulary) extensional operator behaves according to one of the four singulary truth-tables; which one is fixed by the truth-values of the results of applying it to ⊤\top and ⊥\bot, which are the same from one of two coextensive operators to the other. Thus coextensive extensional operators behave according to the same truth-table at any given index. The principles that an extensional operator satisfies are fixed by the set of truth-tables according to which it behaves at at least one index; thus coextensive extensional operators satisfy the same principles. Since the set of singulary truth-tables has just 16 subsets, there are just 16 possibilities for the set of principles that an extensional operator satisfies (or 15 if the set of indices is required to be non-empty. Humberstone 1995 arrives at an answer greater than 16 to what may look like the same question by individuating operators according to consequence relations rather than in the present semantic manner.)

The phenomenon of coextensiveness without satisfaction of the same principles can be generalized to nn-ary operators where n>1n>1, although a more complex language than L□\mathrm{L}_□ would be needed to keep track of it. For example, one could seek coextensive conditionals that satisfy different principles. The preceding remarks show that the phenomenon arises only for non-truth-functional conditionals. Example 4 provides an obvious example, for strict implication (□(p⊃q)□(p\supset q)) and actualized strict implication (@□(p⊃q)@□(p\supset q)) are coextensive in KT@S without satisfying the same principles. Since necessity is definable in terms of strict implication, this is just a notational variant of Example 4. Necessity can equally well be defined in terms of many other conditionals ⇒\Rightarrow (e.g. the counterfactual conditional) by an equivalence between □α□\mathrm{\alpha} and ∼α⇒α\mathord{\sim}\mathrm{\alpha}\Rightarrow\mathrm{\alpha}, so similar examples can be manufactured for them too. In such cases, if p⇒qp\Rightarrow q can be interpreted as contingently true (as it can when ⇒\Rightarrow is the counterfactual conditional), then the principle (p⇒q)⊃(∼(p⇒q)⇒(p⇒q))(p\Rightarrow q)\supset(\mathord{\sim}(p\Rightarrow q)\Rightarrow(p\Rightarrow q)) will fail, while the corresponding principle holds for the actualized variant of ⇒\Rightarrow, even if the logic of necessity is assumed to be S5. Similar remarks apply to the principle (p⇒q)⇒(r⇒(p⇒q))(p\Rightarrow q)\Rightarrow(r\Rightarrow(p\Rightarrow q)). It would be interesting to have examples in which the principles at stake have been subject to more controversy in discussion of conditionals (see Dorothy Edgington’s contribution to this volume).

3. Systems of principles

To investigate the phenomenon of coextensiveness without satisfaction of the same principles more deeply, one must look at the systems of modal logic constituted by the principles that given operators satisfy. Some terminology will help. A (mono)modal system is a subset of the language (set of formulas) L□\mathrm{L}_□ containing all truth-functional tautologies and closed under uniform substitution (US) and MP. A normal (mono)modal system is a (mono)modal system containing the formula □(p⊃q)⊃(□p⊃□q)□(p\supset q)\supset(□ p\supset□ q) and closed under necessitation. In effect, normality means that □□ is closed under deductive consequence in the system. These definitions are extended to polymodal languages in the obvious way, e.g. if the language contains the primitive modal operators □1,…,□n□_1,\ldots,□_n, then normality requires the system to contain the formula □j(p⊃q)⊃(□jp⊃□jq)□_j(p\supset q)\supset(□_jp\supset□_jq) and to be closed under necessitation for □j□_j for each jj from 1 to nn. If S\mathrm{S} is a modal system, write ⊢Sα\vdash_{\mathrm{S}}\mathrm{\alpha} for α∈S\mathrm{\alpha}\in \mathrm{S}. A set of formulas in the language of S\mathrm{S} is 𝑆\mathit{S}-consistent just in case it has no finite subset {α1,…,αk}\{\mathrm{\alpha}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{\alpha}_k\} such that ⊢S∼(α1&⋯&αk)\vdash_{\mathrm{S}}\mathord{\sim}(\mathrm{\alpha}_1\mathbin{\&}\cdots\mathbin{\&}\mathrm{\alpha}_k).

The set of principles that an operator O\mathrm{O} satisfies is automatically closed under US. For given any substitution σ\mathrm{\sigma} and O\mathrm{O}-translation *\text{*}, the mapping of each formula α\mathrm{\alpha} of L□\mathrm{L}_□ to *σα\text{*}\mathrm{\sigma}\mathrm{\alpha} is also an O\mathrm{O}-translation; thus if O\mathrm{O} satisfies α\mathrm{\alpha}, then *σα\text{*}\mathrm{\sigma}\mathrm{\alpha} is true at every index; thus every O\mathrm{O}-translation of σα\mathrm{\sigma}\mathrm{\alpha} is true at every index, so O\mathrm{O} satisfies σα\mathrm{\sigma}\mathrm{\alpha}. Since the primitive truth-functional operators of L□\mathrm{L}_□ (⊥\bot and ⊃\supset) behave in L\mathrm{L} according to their usual truth-tables at every index and commute with every O\mathrm{O}-translation, O\mathrm{O} satisfies all truth-functional tautologies in L□\mathrm{L}_□, and if O\mathrm{O} satisfies α⊃β\mathrm{\alpha}\supset\mathrm{\beta} and α\mathrm{\alpha} then it satisfies β\mathrm{\beta}. Thus the principles that an operator satisfies always constitute a modal system.

Conversely, any modal system S\mathrm{S} is the set of all principles satisfied by some operator O\mathrm{O} for some index set I\mathrm{I}. To establish this, let the base language L\mathrm{L} be L□\mathrm{L}_□ itself, O\mathrm{O} be □□, I\mathrm{I} be the set of all maximal S\mathrm{S}-consistent subsets of L□\mathrm{L}_□, and α\mathrm{\alpha} be true at ii (∈I\in \mathrm{I}) just in case α∈i\mathrm{\alpha}\in i. Since S\mathrm{S} contains all truth-functional tautologies and is closed under MP, this gives the right truth-conditions for ⊥\bot and α⊃β\mathrm{\alpha}\supset\mathrm{\beta}. □□-translations are simply uniform substitutions, so every □□-translation of α\mathrm{\alpha} is true at every index if and only if every substitution instance of α\mathrm{\alpha} belongs to every maximal S\mathrm{S}-consistent set, i.e. if and only if every substitution instance of α\mathrm{\alpha} is a theorem of S\mathrm{S}, i.e. if and only if α\mathrm{\alpha} is a theorem of S\mathrm{S} (since S\mathrm{S} is closed under US). Thus the theorems of S\mathrm{S} are exactly the principles that □□ satisfies.

In many cases, the principles that an operator satisfies do not constitute a normal modal system. Normal systems are especially tractable, however, and therefore make a good point of departure.

Some logical questions can now be raised. How widespread is the phenomenon of coextensiveness without satisfaction of the same principles? More specifically: given the principles that two operators O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 satisfy, when is it consistent to suppose that O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 are coextensive? The question can be taken in more than one way. One reading is this: given two monomodal systems S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2, when is there a language L\mathrm{L} with a non-empty index set I\mathrm{I} and coextensive operators O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 such that O1\mathrm{O}_1 satisfies all the principles in S1\mathrm{S}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 satisfies all the principles in S2\mathrm{S}_2? The index set is required to be non-empty in order not to make the answer trivially ‘Always’, since any operator vacuously satisfies any principle if the index set is empty. On this reading of the question, O1\mathrm{O}_1 may also satisfy some principles not in S1\mathrm{S}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 may also satisfy some principles not in S2\mathrm{S}_2. It follows that this reading does not address the phenomenon at issue very well. For even if S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 are distinct systems, and O1\mathrm{O}_1 satisfies every principle in S1\mathrm{S}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 satisfies every principle in S2\mathrm{S}_2, the two operators may satisfy exactly the same principles.

The extent of the problem can be illustrated as follows. Let S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 be any two normal systems. If ◇⊤◇\top (⊤\top a tautology) is a theorem of neither S1\mathrm{S}_1 nor S2\mathrm{S}_2, then every principle of both S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 is satisfied by any operator O\mathrm{O} such that, for each index ii and sentence α\mathrm{\alpha} of L\mathrm{L}, Oα\mathrm{O}\mathrm{\alpha} is true at ii. For example, Oα\mathrm{O}\mathrm{\alpha} could be α⊃α\mathrm{\alpha}\supset\mathrm{\alpha}. Proof: Let Ver be the smallest normal system of which □⊥□\bot is a theorem. If ◇⊤◇\top is not a theorem of a normal system S\mathrm{S}, then Ver is an extension of S\mathrm{S}. For let S+={α∈L□:⊢S□⊥⊃α}\mathrm{S}^+=\{\mathrm{\alpha}\in \mathrm{L}_□:\vdash_{\mathrm{S}}□\bot\supset\mathrm{\alpha}\}. S+\mathrm{S}^+ is easily shown to be a consistent normal extension of Ver, and therefore to be Ver (which is Post-complete). If Oα\mathrm{O}\mathrm{\alpha} is true at every index for every α∈L□\mathrm{\alpha}\in \mathrm{L}_□, then O\mathrm{O} satisfies every principle in Ver, and therefore every principle in S\mathrm{S}, of which S+\mathrm{S}^+ is an extension.

Similarly, if both S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 have consistent normal extensions of which ◇⊤◇\top is a theorem, then every every principle of both S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 is satisfied by any operator O\mathrm{O} such that, for each index ii and sentence α\mathrm{\alpha} of L\mathrm{L}, Oα\mathrm{O}\mathrm{\alpha} is true at ii if and only if α\mathrm{\alpha} is true at ii. For example, Oα\mathrm{O}\mathrm{\alpha} could be α\mathrm{\alpha} itself. Proof: Let Triv be the smallest normal system of which □p≡p□ p\equiv p is a theorem. If a system S\mathrm{S} has a consistent normal extension S′\mathrm{S}' of which ◇⊤◇\top is a theorem, then Triv is an extension of S\mathrm{S}. For let σ\mathrm{\sigma} be the substitution such that σp=⊤\mathrm{\sigma} p=\top for each propositional variable pp, and put S+={α∈L□:⊢Sστα for every substitution τ}\mathrm{S}^+=\{\mathrm{\alpha}\in \mathrm{L}_□:\vdash_{\mathrm{S}}\mathrm{\sigma}\mathrm{\tau}\mathrm{\alpha}\text{ for every substitution }\mathrm{\tau}\}. S+\mathrm{S}^+ is easily shown to be a consistent normal extension of Triv, and therefore to be Triv (which is Post-complete). If Oα\mathrm{O}\mathrm{\alpha} is true at an index if and only if α\mathrm{\alpha} is, for every α∈L□\mathrm{\alpha}\in \mathrm{L}_□, then O\mathrm{O} satisfies every principle in Triv, and therefore every principle in S′\mathrm{S}', of which S+\mathrm{S}^+ is an extension, and therefore every principle in S\mathrm{S}.

Each result covers a wide class of cases, in all of which one can choose O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 to be identical, so that they automatically satisfy the same principles. Thus, on this reading, the question does not elicit coextensiveness without satisfaction of the same principles.

There are, however, some pairs of coextensive operators O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 such that O1\mathrm{O}_1 satisfies every principle in a normal system S1\mathrm{S}_1, O2\mathrm{O}_2 satisfies every principle in a normal system S2\mathrm{S}_2, and no operator could consistently satisfy every principle in both S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2. For example, let S1\mathrm{S}_1 be the smallest normal system of which □□⊥□□\bot is a theorem, and S2\mathrm{S}_2 be KD (= D), the smallest normal system of which ◇⊤◇\top is a theorem. Since ∼□□⊥\mathord{\sim}□□\bot is a theorem of KD, no operator could satisfy every principle in both S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 with respect to a non-empty set of indices. Nevertheless, define two operators as follows. There is only one index, so this parameter can be suppressed. Truth and an auxiliary notion of pseudo-truth are defined by simultaneous recursion. Take the truth or falsity of atomic sentences as given. An atomic sentence is pseudo-true just in case it is true. Both truth and pseudo-truth behave in the usual way with respect to ⊥\bot and ⊃\supset. O1α\mathrm{O}_1\mathrm{\alpha} is true just in case α\mathrm{\alpha} is pseudo-true; O2α\mathrm{O}_2\mathrm{\alpha} is true just in case α\mathrm{\alpha} is pseudo-true; O1α\mathrm{O}_1\mathrm{\alpha} is pseudo-true in any case; O2α\mathrm{O}_2\mathrm{\alpha} is pseudo-true just in case α\mathrm{\alpha} is pseudo-true. The semantics works just like a possible worlds semantics for O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 as necessity-like operators whose accessibility relations are {⟨i,j⟩}\{\langle i,j\rangle\} and {⟨i,j⟩,⟨j,j⟩}\{\langle i,j\rangle,\langle j,j\rangle\} respectively on a set of worlds {i,j}\{i,j\}, where truth is truth at the actual world ii and pseudo-truth is truth at jj. Validity is taken as truth at the actual world for any truth-assignment to atomic formulas at worlds. In this semantics it is stipulated that only the actual world is an index. By induction on the length of proofs in the natural axiomatizations of S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 with MP and necessitation as the only rules of inference, it is easy to check that every O1\mathrm{O}_1-translation of every theorem of S1\mathrm{S}_1 and every O2\mathrm{O}_2-translation of every theorem of S2\mathrm{S}_2 is both true and pseudo-true, and therefore that O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 satisfy every principle of S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 respectively. In fact, O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 satisfy more principles than those of S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 respectively; for example, O1\mathrm{O}_1 satisfies ◇⊤◇\top, which is not a principle of S1\mathrm{S}_1 (but O1\mathrm{O}_1 does not satisfy □◇⊤□◇\top; the principles that it satisfies do not constitute a normal system). Note that truth is all that matters for satisfaction of principles, but pseudo-truth must also be established for the inductive argument to go through. In particular, O1\mathrm{O}_1 satisfies □□⊥□□\bot and O2\mathrm{O}_2 does not. Thus O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 satisfy different principles. Indeed, they satisfy inconsistent principles, for O2\mathrm{O}_2 satisfies ∼□□⊥\mathord{\sim}□□\bot. Nevertheless, O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 are coextensive, for each of O1α\mathrm{O}_1\mathrm{\alpha} and O2α\mathrm{O}_2\mathrm{\alpha} is true just in case α\mathrm{\alpha} is pseudo-true.

A different question needs to be asked. Given two monomodal systems S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2, when is there a language L\mathrm{L} with an index set I\mathrm{I} and coextensive operators O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 such that O1\mathrm{O}_1 satisfies all and only the principles in S1\mathrm{S}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 satisfies all and only the principles in S2\mathrm{S}_2? Note that if S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 are distinct any case in which such operators exist is a case of coextensiveness without satisfaction of the same principles.

The question can be reformulated in terms of bimodal logic. Let L□\mathrm{L}_□ be the result of extending L□◼\mathrm{L}_{□\blacksquare} by a second modal operator ◼\blacksquare. Given an interpreted language L\mathrm{L} with at least the operators ⊥\bot, ⊃\supset, O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2, define an O1,O2\mathrm{O}_1,\mathrm{O}_2-translation to be a mapping *\text{*} from formulas of L□◼\mathrm{L}_{□\blacksquare} to sentences of L\mathrm{L} such that for all formulas α\mathrm{\alpha} and β\mathrm{\beta} of L□◼\mathrm{L}_{□\blacksquare}, *(α⊃β)=*α⊃*β\text{*}(\mathrm{\alpha}\supset\mathrm{\beta})=\text{*}\mathrm{\alpha}\supset\text{*}\mathrm{\beta}, *⊥=⊥\text{*}\bot=\bot, *□α=O1*α\text{*}□\mathrm{\alpha}=\mathrm{O}_1\text{*}\mathrm{\alpha} and *◼α=O2*α\text{*}\blacksquare\mathrm{\alpha}=\mathrm{O}_2\text{*}\mathrm{\alpha}. The ordered pair ⟨O1,O2⟩\langle \mathrm{O}_1,\mathrm{O}_2\rangle satisfies a principle α\mathrm{\alpha} (∈L□◼\in \mathrm{L}_{□\blacksquare}) just in case *α\text{*}\mathrm{\alpha} is true at every index in the relevant set for every O1,O2\mathrm{O}_1,\mathrm{O}_2-translation *\text{*}. If S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 are monomodal systems in L□\mathrm{L}_□, let S1⊚S2\mathrm{S}_1\mathbin{\circledcirc}\mathrm{S}_2 be the smallest bimodal system in L□◼\mathrm{L}_{□\blacksquare} containing every theorem of S1\mathrm{S}_1 and the result of substituting ◼\blacksquare for □□ throughout every theorem of S2\mathrm{S}_2. Let S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2 be the smallest bimodal system containing S1⊚S2\mathrm{S}_1\mathbin{\circledcirc}\mathrm{S}_2 and the formula □p≡◼p□ p\equiv\blacksquare p (by US, S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2 contains every formula of the form □α≡◼α□\mathrm{\alpha}\equiv\blacksquare\mathrm{\alpha}). A bimodal system S\mathrm{S} in L□◼\mathrm{L}_{□\blacksquare} conservatively □□-extends a monomodal system S′\mathrm{S}' in L□\mathrm{L}_□ just in case the ◼\blacksquare-free theorems of S\mathrm{S} are exactly the theorems of S′\mathrm{S}'; S\mathrm{S} conservatively ◼\blacksquare-extends S′\mathrm{S}' just in case the results of substituting □□ for ◼\blacksquare throughout the □□-free theorems of S\mathrm{S} are exactly the theorems of S′\mathrm{S}'. Then there is a language L\mathrm{L} with coextensive operators O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 and an index set I\mathrm{I} with respect to which O1\mathrm{O}_1 satisfies all and only the principles in S1\mathrm{S}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 satisfies all and only the principles in S2\mathrm{S}_2 if and only if S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2 conservatively □□-extends S1\mathrm{S}_1 and conservatively ◼\blacksquare-extends S2\mathrm{S}_2.

The equivalence can be established as follows. Suppose that there is a language L\mathrm{L} with coextensive operators O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 and an index set I\mathrm{I} with respect to which O1\mathrm{O}_1 satisfies all and only the principles in S1\mathrm{S}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 satisfies all and only the principles in S2\mathrm{S}_2. Let S\mathrm{S} be the set of all principles in L□◼\mathrm{L}_{□\blacksquare} that ⟨O1,O2⟩\langle \mathrm{O}_1,\mathrm{O}_2\rangle satisfies. S\mathrm{S} is a bimodal system. S\mathrm{S} extends S1\mathrm{S}_1, for if α\mathrm{\alpha} is a theorem of S1\mathrm{S}_1 then O1\mathrm{O}_1 satisfies α\mathrm{\alpha}, so every O1\mathrm{O}_1-translation of α\mathrm{\alpha} is true at every index; but the restriction of any O1,O2\mathrm{O}_1,\mathrm{O}_2-translation to L□\mathrm{L}_□ is an O1\mathrm{O}_1-translation, so every O1,O2\mathrm{O}_1,\mathrm{O}_2-translation of α\mathrm{\alpha} is true at every index, so α\mathrm{\alpha} is a theorem of S\mathrm{S}. Similarly, S\mathrm{S} contains the result of substituting ◼\blacksquare for □□ throughout every theorem of S2\mathrm{S}_2. Thus S\mathrm{S} extends S1⊚S2\mathrm{S}_1\mathbin{\circledcirc}\mathrm{S}_2. Since O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 are coextensive, S\mathrm{S} contains □p≡◼p□ p\equiv\blacksquare p. Thus S\mathrm{S} extends S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2. Now S\mathrm{S} conservatively □□-extends S1\mathrm{S}_1. For let α\mathrm{\alpha} be a ◼\blacksquare-free theorem of S\mathrm{S}; thus every O1,O2\mathrm{O}_1,\mathrm{O}_2-translation of α\mathrm{\alpha} is true at every index; but every O1\mathrm{O}_1-translation has a (unique) extension to an O1,O2\mathrm{O}_1,\mathrm{O}_2-translation, so every O1\mathrm{O}_1-translation of α\mathrm{\alpha} is true at every index, so α\mathrm{\alpha} is a theorem of S1\mathrm{S}_1. Similarly, S\mathrm{S} conservatively ◼\blacksquare-extends S2\mathrm{S}_2. Since S\mathrm{S} extends S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2, the latter conservatively □□-extends S1\mathrm{S}_1 and conservatively ◼\blacksquare-extends S2\mathrm{S}_2 too. This establishes one half of the equivalence. Conversely, suppose that S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2 conservatively □□-extends S1\mathrm{S}_1 and conservatively ◼\blacksquare-extends S2\mathrm{S}_2. Let the base language L\mathrm{L} be L□◼\mathrm{L}_{□\blacksquare} itself, O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 be □□ and ◼\blacksquare respectively, I\mathrm{I} be the set of all maximal S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2-consistent subsets of L□◼\mathrm{L}_{□\blacksquare}, and α\mathrm{\alpha} be true at ii (∈I\in \mathrm{I}) just in case α∈i\mathrm{\alpha}\in i. By an earlier argument, □□ satisfies a principle α\mathrm{\alpha} of L□\mathrm{L}_□ if and only if α\mathrm{\alpha} is a theorem of S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2; since the latter conservatively □□-extends S1\mathrm{S}_1, □□ satisfies α\mathrm{\alpha} if and only if α\mathrm{\alpha} is a theorem of S1\mathrm{S}_1. Similarly, ◼\blacksquare satisfies α\mathrm{\alpha} if and only if α\mathrm{\alpha} is a theorem of S2\mathrm{S}_2. Since every formula of the form □α≡◼α□\mathrm{\alpha}\equiv\blacksquare\mathrm{\alpha} is a theorem of S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2, □□ and ◼\blacksquare are coextensive. Thus there is a language L\mathrm{L} with coextensive operators O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 and an index set I\mathrm{I} with respect to which O1\mathrm{O}_1 satisfies all and only the principles in S1\mathrm{S}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 satisfies all and only the principles in S2\mathrm{S}_2, as required.

Our question is therefore this: when does S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2 conservatively □□-extend a monomodal system S1\mathrm{S}_1 and conservatively ◼\blacksquare-extend a monomodal system S2\mathrm{S}_2? In brief, when is S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2 a conservative extension? As noted in §2, coextensive operators satisfy the same first-degree principles. Thus a necessary condition for S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2 to be a conservative extension is that for every first-degree formula α\mathrm{\alpha} of L□\mathrm{L}_□, α\mathrm{\alpha} is a theorem of S1\mathrm{S}_1 if and only if α\mathrm{\alpha} is a theorem of S2\mathrm{S}_2. However, the condition is not sufficient. For let S1\mathrm{S}_1 be the smallest normal modal system containing □□⊥□□\bot, and let S2\mathrm{S}_2 be the smallest normal modal system containing (∼p&□p)⊃□□⊥(\mathord{\sim} p\mathbin{\&}□ p)\supset□□\bot. S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 can be shown to have exactly the same first-degree theorems (Appendix, Proposition 1). Nevertheless, S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2 does not conservatively ◼\blacksquare-extend S2\mathrm{S}_2, for it has the following sequence of theorems:

1 (∼p&◼p)⊃◼◼⊥(\mathord{\sim} p\mathbin{\&}\blacksquare p)\supset\blacksquare\blacksquare\bot S2\mathrm{S}_2
2 (∼⊥&◼⊥)⊃◼◼⊥(\mathord{\sim}\bot\mathbin{\&}\blacksquare\bot)\supset\blacksquare\blacksquare\bot 1 US
3 (∼□⊥&◼□⊥)⊃◼◼⊥(\mathord{\sim}□\bot\mathbin{\&}\blacksquare□\bot)\supset\blacksquare\blacksquare\bot 1 US
4 (◼⊥∨(∼□⊥&◼□⊥))⊃◼◼⊥(\blacksquare\bot\vee(\mathord{\sim}□\bot\mathbin{\&}\blacksquare□\bot))\supset\blacksquare\blacksquare\bot 2, 3 PC
5 □p≡◼p□ p\equiv\blacksquare p S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2
6 □⊥≡◼⊥□\bot\equiv\blacksquare\bot 5 US
7 ◼□⊥⊃◼◼⊥\blacksquare□\bot\supset\blacksquare\blacksquare\bot 4, 6 PC
8 □□⊥≡◼□⊥□□\bot\equiv\blacksquare□\bot 5 US
9 □□⊥□□\bot S1\mathrm{S}_1
10 ◼◼⊥\blacksquare\blacksquare\bot 7, 8, 9 PC

Informally, the derivation shows that no operator coextensive with one that satisfies □□⊥□□\bot is truth-entailing, which is enough to discharge the antecedent in the S2\mathrm{S}_2 axiom. Thus if S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2 conservatively ◼\blacksquare-extends S2\mathrm{S}_2, then □□⊥□□\bot is a theorem of S2\mathrm{S}_2. But □□⊥□□\bot is not a theorem of S2\mathrm{S}_2, for, since (∼p&□p)⊃□□⊥(\mathord{\sim} p\mathbin{\&}□ p)\supset□□\bot is a tautological consequence of □p⊃p□ p\supset p, all theorems of S2\mathrm{S}_2 are theorems of KT (=T), which □□⊥□□\bot certainly is not.

In spite of such examples, when S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2 is a conservative extension S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 often agree on little but their first-degree theorems, as earlier examples and later results show. It would be interesting to know whether agreement on first-degree theorems between S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 is sufficient for S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2 to be a conservative extension when S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 are normal extensions of KT, or more generally of KD.

Similar remarks apply to a slightly stronger way of combining monomodal systems. If S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 are systems in L□\mathrm{L}_□, let S1⊕2\mathrm{S}_1\mathbin{\oplus}_2 be the smallest bimodal system in L□◼\mathrm{L}_{□\blacksquare} containing every theorem of S1\mathrm{S}_1 and the result of substituting ◼\blacksquare for □□ throughout every theorem of S2\mathrm{S}_2, and closed under the rules that if α≡β\mathrm{\alpha}\equiv\mathrm{\beta} is a theorem then so are ◼α≡◼β\blacksquare\mathrm{\alpha}\equiv\blacksquare\mathrm{\beta} and □α≡□β□\mathrm{\alpha}\equiv□\mathrm{\beta}. In particular, if S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 are normal systems, then so is S1⊕S2\mathrm{S}_1\mathbin{\oplus}\mathrm{S}_2, because it is closed under necessitation for both □□ and ◼\blacksquare (for if α\mathrm{\alpha} is a theorem of S1⊕S2\mathrm{S}_1\mathbin{\oplus}\mathrm{S}_2, then so is α≡⊤\mathrm{\alpha}\equiv\top, and therefore □α≡□⊤□\mathrm{\alpha}\equiv□\top and ◼α≡◼⊤\blacksquare\mathrm{\alpha}\equiv\blacksquare\top; but since S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 are normal, □⊤□\top is a theorem of each, so both □α□\mathrm{\alpha} and ◼α\blacksquare\mathrm{\alpha} are theorems of S1⊕S2\mathrm{S}_1\mathbin{\oplus}\mathrm{S}_2). In fact S1⊕S2\mathrm{S}_1\mathbin{\oplus}\mathrm{S}_2 is the smallest normal bimodal system to contain every theorem of S1\mathrm{S}_1 and the result of replacing □□ by ◼\blacksquare throughout every theorem of S2\mathrm{S}_2. Very roughly indeed, the difference between ⊕\oplus and ⊚\circledcirc is that in S1⊕S2\mathrm{S}_1\mathbin{\oplus}\mathrm{S}_2, but not in S1⊚S2\mathrm{S}_1\mathbin{\circledcirc}\mathrm{S}_2, each of □□ and ◼\blacksquare knows what the logic of the other is. Let S1‡S2\mathrm{S}_1\ddagger \mathrm{S}_2 be the smallest bimodal system containing S1⊕S2\mathrm{S}_1\mathbin{\oplus}\mathrm{S}_2 and the formula □p≡◼p□ p\equiv\blacksquare p (by US, S1⊕S2\mathrm{S}_1\mathbin{\oplus}\mathrm{S}_2 contains every formula of the form □α≡◼α□\mathrm{\alpha}\equiv\blacksquare\mathrm{\alpha}).

S1‡S2\mathrm{S}_1\ddagger \mathrm{S}_2 will not in general be a normal system, even if S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 are normal, for it will not in general contain □(□p≡◼p)□(□ p\equiv\blacksquare p). Obviously, S1‡S2\mathrm{S}_1\ddagger \mathrm{S}_2 extends S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2. The converse often fails, even when S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 are normal. For example, □◼□⊤□\blacksquare□\top is a theorem of K‡K\mathrm{K}\ddagger \mathrm{K} (indeed of K⊕K\mathrm{K}\mathbin{\oplus}\mathrm{K}), but not of K†K\mathrm{K}\dagger \mathrm{K} (Appendix, Proposition 2). Another example of the difference between †\dagger and ‡\ddagger can be gleaned from Example 4. On a view such as Salmon’s, the pair of operators ⟨□,@□⟩\langle□,@□\rangle satisfies all the principles of KT†S5\mathrm{KT}\dagger \mathrm{S}5, but not all the principles of KT‡S5\mathrm{KT}\ddagger \mathrm{S}5. For □□(◼p⊃p)□□(\blacksquare p\supset p) is a theorem of KT⊕S5\mathrm{KT}\mathbin{\oplus}\mathrm{S}5 (indeed, of K⊕KT\mathrm{K}\mathbin{\oplus}\mathrm{KT}) and therefore of KT‡S5\mathrm{KT}\ddagger \mathrm{S}5, but it is not satisfied by the pair ⟨□,@□⟩\langle□,@□\rangle. If it were, □□(@□p⊃p)□□(@□ p\supset p) would be a theorem of KT@S, in which case □□@□p⊃□□p□□@□ p\supset□□ p would also be a theorem of KT@S; but since □p⊃□□@□p□ p\supset□□@□ p is a theorem of KT@S, so too would be □p⊃□□p□ p\supset□□ p, which it is not (for an easy semantic argument shows KT@S to be a conservative extension of KT). Thus □□(◼p⊃p)□□(\blacksquare p\supset p) is not a theorem of KT†S5\mathrm{KT}\dagger \mathrm{S}5.

From a semantical point of view, S1⊕S2\mathrm{S}_1\mathbin{\oplus}\mathrm{S}_2 is usually a nicer system than S1⊚S2\mathrm{S}_1\mathbin{\circledcirc}\mathrm{S}_2, for it can be hard to give □□ and ◼\blacksquare nice semantics when they do not act congruentially. Correspondingly, one might prefer to work with S1‡S2\mathrm{S}_1\ddagger \mathrm{S}_2 rather than with S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2. However, the latter system is the relevant one in some applications, as has just been noted in respect of Example 4. Again, if the operators O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 satisfy just the principles of the monomodal systems S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 respectively, and S1\mathrm{S}_1 is not closed under the rule that if α≡β\mathrm{\alpha}\equiv\mathrm{\beta} is a theorem then so is □α≡□β□\mathrm{\alpha}\equiv□\mathrm{\beta}, it follows that S1‡S2\mathrm{S}_1\ddagger \mathrm{S}_2 does not conservatively □□-extend S1\mathrm{S}_1, so the only interesting question will be whether the pair of operators ⟨O1,O2⟩\langle \mathrm{O}_1,\mathrm{O}_2\rangle satisfies all the principles of S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2. Such a situation arose in Example 3, for □□ is not congruential in the system GLS, which has (□p⊃p)≡⊤(□ p\supset p)\equiv\top but not □(□p⊃p)≡□⊤□(□ p\supset p)\equiv□\top as a theorem. GLS†Grz\mathrm{GLS}\dagger \mathrm{Grz} conservatively □□-extends GLS and conservatively ◼\blacksquare-extends Grz; GLS‡Grz\mathrm{GLS}\ddagger \mathrm{Grz} is inconsistent, for the closure of GLS under necessitation is inconsistent. More generally, an earlier argument showed that S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2 is a conservative extension if and only if there are coextensive operators O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 such that O1\mathrm{O}_1 satisfies all and only the principles in S1\mathrm{S}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 satisfies all and only the principles in S2\mathrm{S}_2; no parallel result has been proved for S1‡S2\mathrm{S}_1\ddagger \mathrm{S}_2. However, in the cases for which it is proved below that S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2 conservatively □□-extends a normal system S1\mathrm{S}_1 and conservatively ◼\blacksquare-extends a normal system S2\mathrm{S}_2, it is also proved that S1‡S2\mathrm{S}_1\ddagger \mathrm{S}_2 conservatively □□-extends S1\mathrm{S}_1 and conservatively ◼\blacksquare-extends S2\mathrm{S}_2. It would be interesting to know whether there are normal systems S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 such that S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2 but not S1‡S2\mathrm{S}_1\ddagger \mathrm{S}_2 conservatively □□-extends S1\mathrm{S}_1 and conservatively ◼\blacksquare-extends S2\mathrm{S}_2.

It must be checked whether, if S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2 or S1‡S2\mathrm{S}_1\ddagger \mathrm{S}_2 is not a conservative extension, the reason is not simply that S1⊚S2\mathrm{S}_1\mathbin{\circledcirc}\mathrm{S}_2 or S1⊕S2\mathrm{S}_1\mathbin{\oplus}\mathrm{S}_2 is not a conservative extension; if that were the reason, non-conservativeness would show nothing specific about coextensiveness. Call a modal system S\mathrm{S} in L□\mathrm{L}_□ congruential just in case □α≡□β□\mathrm{\alpha}\equiv□\mathrm{\beta} is a theorem of S\mathrm{S} whenever α≡β\mathrm{\alpha}\equiv\mathrm{\beta} is. Thomason (1980) proved that if S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 are consistent congruential systems, then S1⊕S2\mathrm{S}_1\mathbin{\oplus}\mathrm{S}_2 (and a fortiori S1⊚S2\mathrm{S}_1\mathbin{\circledcirc}\mathrm{S}_2) conservatively □□-extends S1\mathrm{S}_1 and conservatively ◼\blacksquare-extends S2\mathrm{S}_2. (Kracht and Wolter 1991 give a different proof for the case in which S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 are normal. See the latter paper and Fine and Schurz 1996 for many results transferring properties of normal S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 to S1⊕S2\mathrm{S}_1\mathbin{\oplus}\mathrm{S}_2.) The result is trivial if S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 are both inconsistent. Since an operator satisfies an inconsistent set of principles if and only if the relevant index set is empty, if O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 are operators in L\mathrm{L}, and S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 are the systems of the principles satisfied by O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 respectively, then S1\mathrm{S}_1 is consistent if and only if S2\mathrm{S}_2 is. Thus if the congruential systems S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 are the systems of the principles satisfied by the operators O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 respectively in L\mathrm{L}, then S1⊕S2\mathrm{S}_1\mathbin{\oplus}\mathrm{S}_2 (and a fortiori S1⊚S2\mathrm{S}_1\mathbin{\circledcirc}\mathrm{S}_2) conservatively □□-extends S1\mathrm{S}_1 and conservatively ◼\blacksquare-extends S2\mathrm{S}_2. If S1\mathrm{S}_1 or S2\mathrm{S}_2 is non-congruential, then S1⊕S2\mathrm{S}_1\mathbin{\oplus}\mathrm{S}_2 is not a conservative extension. But one can still prove that if S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 are consistent modal systems, then S1⊚S2\mathrm{S}_1\mathbin{\circledcirc}\mathrm{S}_2 conservatively □□-extends S1\mathrm{S}_1 and conservatively ◼\blacksquare-extends S2\mathrm{S}_2 (Appendix, Proposition 4). Thus if S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 are the systems of the principles satisfied by the operators O1\mathrm{O}_1 and O2\mathrm{O}_2 respectively in L\mathrm{L}, then S1⊚S2\mathrm{S}_1\mathbin{\circledcirc}\mathrm{S}_2 conservatively □□-extends S1\mathrm{S}_1 and conservatively ◼\blacksquare-extends S2\mathrm{S}_2.

Informally, the condition says that the two classes of frames are locally alike, in the sense that what can be reached in one step of accessibility from a given point is the same. This condition may be compared with the syntactic necessary condition discussed above for S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2 (and therefore S1‡S2\mathrm{S}_1\ddagger \mathrm{S}_2) to be a conservative extension: that S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 should have the same first-degree theorems. The two conditions are closely related, for whether a first-degree formula is true at a world ww depends only on worlds at most one step of accessibility from ww. The two conditions are not equivalent, for the constraints that a system imposes on the one-step environments of worlds are not exhausted by its first-degree theorems. For example, if □□⊥□□\bot is a theorem of S\mathrm{S}, then S\mathrm{S} is valid only on irreflexive frames, even though S\mathrm{S} may have the same first-degree theorems as a system that is valid on some (or even all) reflexive frames. Whereas a frame is reflexive if and only if □p⊃p□ p\supset p is valid on it, there is no formula α∈L□\mathrm{\alpha}\in \mathrm{L}_□ (let alone a first-degree one) such that a frame is irreflexive if and only if α\mathrm{\alpha} is valid on it, for the smallest normal system K is complete both for the class of all irreflexive frames and for the class of all frames. Irreflexivity is however characterized in a weaker sense by the rule that if (∼p&□p)⊃α(\mathord{\sim} p\mathbin{\&}□ p)\supset\mathrm{\alpha} is a theorem then so is α\mathrm{\alpha}, where the propositional variable pp does not occur in α\mathrm{\alpha} (Gabbay 1981). An example of just this kind was used above to show that for S1\mathrm{S}_1 and S2\mathrm{S}_2 to have the same first-degree theorems is not sufficient for S1‡S2\mathrm{S}_1\ddagger \mathrm{S}_2 or even S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2 to be a conservative extension. The semantic sufficient condition above will be applied in §5.

4. Solovay extensions

Examples 2 and 3 (as regards the Solovay logic GLS) can be generalized. Consider an operator O\mathrm{O} in a base language L\mathrm{L} with respect to an index set I\mathrm{I}. Suppose that for each index ii, Oα\mathrm{O}\mathrm{\alpha} is true at ii only if α\mathrm{\alpha} is true at ii (e.g. O\mathrm{O} is ‘Socrates believes that . . .’, if Socrates is in fact a wise man at all indices) . . . Non-congruential systems are often semantically recalcitrant. Sometimes, however, the non-congruential system S′\mathrm{S}' with the theorem □p⊃p□ p\supset p has a congruential subsystem S\mathrm{S} without the theorem □p⊃p□ p\supset p such that S′\mathrm{S}' can be axiomatized with all theorems of S\mathrm{S} and all substitution instances of □p⊃p□ p\supset p as the axioms and MP as the only rule of inference. GL has a nice semantics: it is sound and complete for the class of all possible worlds models based on finite transitive irreflexive trees. In contrast, there is no frame ⟨W,R⟩\langle \mathrm{W},\mathrm{R}\rangle and world w∈Ww\in \mathrm{W} such that all theorems of GLS are true at ww in all models based on the frame ⟨W,R⟩\langle \mathrm{W},\mathrm{R}\rangle. Proof: The theorem □(□p⊃p)⊃□p□(□ p\supset p)\supset□ p of GLS is true at w∈Ww\in \mathrm{W} in all models based on ⟨W,R⟩\langle \mathrm{W},\mathrm{R}\rangle only if not wRww\mathrm{R}w (consider an assignment on which pp is true everywhere except at ww); □p⊃p□ p\supset p is true at ww in all models based on ⟨W,R⟩\langle \mathrm{W},\mathrm{R}\rangle only if wRww\mathrm{R}w.

More generally, let the Solovay extension of a system S\mathrm{S} be the smallest system containing □p⊃p□ p\supset p and every theorem of S\mathrm{S}; since Solovay extensions are systems, they are automatically closed under MP and contain every substitution instance of □p⊃p□ p\supset p. One can reverse the direction of investigation and examine the Solovay extensions of given congruential systems.

Let S\mathrm{S} be a normal system, and S+\mathrm{S}^+ the Solovay extension of S\mathrm{S}. Suppose that S+\mathrm{S}^+ contains all and only the principles satisfied by an operator O\mathrm{O}. Since S+\mathrm{S}^+ contains the principle □p≡(□p&p)□ p\equiv(□ p\mathbin{\&} p), O\mathrm{O} and O+\mathrm{O}^+ are coextensive. What conditions on S\mathrm{S} ensure that O\mathrm{O} and O+\mathrm{O}^+ satisfy different principles? Obviously, if S\mathrm{S} already contains □p⊃p□ p\supset p, then S+\mathrm{S}^+ is S\mathrm{S} itself (and conversely); in that case, S+\mathrm{S}^+ is normal, and therefore congruential, so by remarks above O\mathrm{O} and O+\mathrm{O}^+ satisfy the same principles. More interestingly, suppose that S\mathrm{S} admits the rule of disjunction, in the sense that whenever □α1∨⋯∨□αn□\mathrm{\alpha}_1\vee\cdots\vee□\mathrm{\alpha}_n is a theorem of S\mathrm{S}, so too is αm\mathrm{\alpha}_m for some mm (1≤m≤n1\leq m\leq n). 5. Introspection and ‘actually’ operators

The following are sometimes described as principles of introspection:

4
□p⊃□□p□ p\supset□□ p
5
∼□p⊃□∼□p\mathord{\sim}□ p\supset□\mathord{\sim}□ p

Such a description is given when □□ is interpreted as a knowledge or belief operator. On such an interpretation, 4 and 5 claim that if one knows something, one knows that one knows it, and that if one does not know it, one knows that one does not know it, or that if one believes something, one believes that one believes it, and that if one fails to believe something, one believes that one fails to believe it. Really, the term ‘introspection’ is more naturally associated with the bimodal claims that if one believes something, one knows that one believes it, and that if one fails to believe it, one knows that one fails to believe it, for introspection is supposed to be a faculty of inner perception by which one comes to know one’s internal mental states. The present concern, however, is not with the nature of introspection, nor with the sorts of idealization that would be needed to make 4 or 5 plausible for knowledge or belief (considered as an empirical generalization about ordinary subjects, each of the preceding epistemic and doxastic claims is false). A simple creature that has knowledge and beliefs but lacks the concepts of knowledge and belief would constitute a counter-example to both 4 and 5 on both the epistemic and doxastic interpretations. The argument of Davidson 1984, p. 170, to the effect that no creature can have beliefs without having the concept of belief seems to exploit an equivocation between de re and de dicto readings of ‘understands the possibility of being mistaken’. If one believes that P, one must understand what it would be for it not to be the case that P, which is the condition for one’s belief to be false; but to assume that one must grasp it as the condition for one’s belief to be false is to beg the question.

The question is rather: when is a given operator coextensive with one that satisfies 4 or 5? Such an operator need not be epistemic or doxastic: consider Salmon’s treatment of necessity and actual necessity, discussed in Example 4. Answers to that question may help to explain the deceptive appeal of 4 and 5 in some cases, but the aim of this section is mainly to clarify the formal position.

Not every operator is coextensive with an operator that satisfies 4 and 5. For example, if negation were coextensive with an operator O\mathrm{O} that satisfied 4, then, at any index, since ∼⊥\mathord{\sim}\bot is true, O⊥\mathrm{O}\bot would be true (by coextensiveness), so OO⊥\mathrm{OO}\bot would be true (by 4), so ∼O⊥\mathord{\sim} \mathrm{O}\bot would be true (by coextensiveness), so O⊥\mathrm{O}\bot would be false, so ∼⊥\mathord{\sim}\bot would be false (by coextensiveness yet again), which is impossible. Similarly, if negation were coextensive with an operator O\mathrm{O} that satisfied 5, then, at any index, O⊤\mathrm{O}\top would be false (by coextensiveness), so O∼O⊤\mathrm{O}\mathord{\sim} \mathrm{O}\top would be true (by 5), so ∼∼O⊤\mathord{\sim}\mathord{\sim} \mathrm{O}\top would be true (by coextensiveness), so O⊤\mathrm{O}\top would be true, which is a contradiction. Thus negation is not coextensive with any operator O\mathrm{O} that satisfies either 4 or 5.

Nevertheless, the treatment of the relation between necessity and actual necessity in Example 4 suggested that under fairly general conditions a given operator is coextensive with an operator that satisfies both 4 and 5. Such conditions will now be identified. An obvious strategy is to introduce something like an ‘actually’ operator while assuming as little about the background as possible.

Given an operator O\mathrm{O} in a language L\mathrm{L}, L\mathrm{L} itself will often contain no operator coextensive with O\mathrm{O} that satisfies 4 and 5. The obvious question is whether L\mathrm{L} can be extended to a language that does contain an operator coextensive with O\mathrm{O} that satisfies 4 and 5. But it may be unclear whether O\mathrm{O} in the extended language retains the meaning that it had in the original language, for it applies to sentences of the extended language that are not in the original language. In what follows, a sentence of the original language turns out to be true at an index in the extended language if and only if it is true at that index in the original language; moreover, O\mathrm{O} turns out to satisfy exactly the same principles with respect to the extended language as it did with respect to the original language. It is left to the reader to judge whether O\mathrm{O} retains its original meaning.

For simplicity, consider a language LO\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{O}} made up of atomic sentences, the truth-functional operators ⊥\bot and ⊃\supset and the operator O\mathrm{O}. The arguments below can be generalized to languages with a more extensive vocabulary. Extend LO\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{O}} to a language LO@\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{O}@} by adding the singulary operator @. Many operators that satisfy neither 4 nor 5 are coextensive with operators that satisfy both 4 and 5. By the same token, many operators that satisfy both 4 and 5 are coextensive with operators that satisfy neither 4 nor 5. In such cases, it is impossible to decide whether an operator satisfies one of those principles on the basis of arguments that do not discriminate between coextensive operators. For instance, some kinds of functional consideration about propositional attitudes are insensitive to differences between coextensive operators. Take as an example the principle ‘When one desires that P and one believes that if one does A then P, one usually does A’; if it holds at all, it holds whenever operators coextensive with ‘one desires that’ and ‘one believes that’ respectively are substituted for them. It is therefore impossible to determine on the basis of such considerations whether, if one believes something, one also believes that one believes it. An adequate account of the operators ‘xx believes that’ and ‘xx desires that’ must be based on considerations fine-grained enough to discriminate between them and operators merely coextensive with them.

PROPOSITION 1. The smallest normal modal system containing □□⊥□□\bot has the same first-degree theorems as K; so has the smallest normal modal system containing (p&□∼p)⊃□□⊥(p\mathbin{\&}□\mathord{\sim} p)\supset□□\bot.

ϕp=ψp=pϕ⊥=ψ⊥=⊥ϕ(α⊃β)=ϕα⊃ϕβψ(α⊃β)=ψα⊃ψβϕ□α=⊤ψ□α=□ϕα \begin{gathered} \phi p=\mathrm{\psi} p=p \\ \phi\bot=\mathrm{\psi}\bot=\bot \\ \begin{array}{ll} \phi(\mathrm{\alpha}\supset\mathrm{\beta})=\phi\mathrm{\alpha}\supset\phi\mathrm{\beta} & \mathrm{\psi}(\mathrm{\alpha}\supset\mathrm{\beta})=\mathrm{\psi}\mathrm{\alpha}\supset\mathrm{\psi}\mathrm{\beta} \\ \phi□\mathrm{\alpha}=\top & \mathrm{\psi}□\mathrm{\alpha}=□\phi\mathrm{\alpha} \end{array} \end{gathered}

Suppose that S1\mathrm{S}_1 is the logic of a class of frames Γ1\mathrm{\Gamma}_1, S2\mathrm{S}_2 is the logic of a class of frames Γ2\mathrm{\Gamma}_2, for each ⟨W1,R1⟩∈Γ1\langle \mathrm{W}_1,\mathrm{R}_1\rangle\in\mathrm{\Gamma}_1 and w∈W1w\in \mathrm{W}_1 there is a ⟨W2,R2⟩∈Γ2\langle \mathrm{W}_2,\mathrm{R}_2\rangle\in\mathrm{\Gamma}_2 such that w∈W2w\in \mathrm{W}_2 and {x∈W1:wR1x}={x∈W2:wR2x}\{x\in \mathrm{W}_1:w\mathrm{R}_1x\}=\{x\in \mathrm{W}_2:w\mathrm{R}_2x\}, and for each ⟨W2,R2⟩∈Γ2\langle \mathrm{W}_2,\mathrm{R}_2\rangle\in\mathrm{\Gamma}_2 and w∈W2w\in \mathrm{W}_2 there is a ⟨W1,R1⟩∈Γ1\langle \mathrm{W}_1,\mathrm{R}_1\rangle\in\mathrm{\Gamma}_1 such that w∈W1w\in \mathrm{W}_1 and {x∈W1:wR1x}={x∈W2:wR2x}\{x\in \mathrm{W}_1:w\mathrm{R}_1x\}=\{x\in \mathrm{W}_2:w\mathrm{R}_2x\}. Then S1‡S2\mathrm{S}_1\ddagger \mathrm{S}_2 conservatively □□-extends S1\mathrm{S}_1 and conservatively ◼\blacksquare-extends S2\mathrm{S}_2.

PROOF. That S1‡S2\mathrm{S}_1\ddagger \mathrm{S}_2 conservatively □□-extends S1\mathrm{S}_1 follows from Proposition 5. That it conservatively ◼\blacksquare-extends S2\mathrm{S}_2 is shown by an argument parallel to the proof of Proposition 5.

PROPOSITION 7. Let S\mathrm{S} be a complete system, and S′\mathrm{S}' the smallest extension of K45 containing all first-degree theorems of S\mathrm{S}. Then S‡S′\mathrm{S}\ddagger \mathrm{S}' conservatively □□-extends S\mathrm{S} and conservatively ◼\blacksquare-extends S′\mathrm{S}' (as does S†S′\mathrm{S}\dagger \mathrm{S}').

PROOF. Let S\mathrm{S} be the logic of a class of frames Γ\mathrm{\Gamma}, ⟨W,R⟩∈Γ\langle \mathrm{W},\mathrm{R}\rangle\in\mathrm{\Gamma} and w∈Ww\in \mathrm{W}. Set Ww={w}∪{x:wRx}\mathrm{W}_w=\{w\}\cup\{x:w\mathrm{R}x\} and define Rw\mathrm{R}_w on Ww\mathrm{W}_w by:

xRwyx\mathrm{R}_wy just in case wRyw\mathrm{R}y.

Let S′\mathrm{S}' be the smallest extension of K45 containing all first-degree theorems of S\mathrm{S}.

(i) K45 is valid on ⟨Ww,Rw⟩\langle \mathrm{W}_w,\mathrm{R}_w\rangle. Proof: Rw\mathrm{R}_w is obviously transitive and euclidean.

(ii) If α∈L□\mathrm{\alpha}\in \mathrm{L}_□ is first-degree and ⊢Sα\vdash_{\mathrm{S}}\mathrm{\alpha}, then α\mathrm{\alpha} is valid on ⟨Ww,Rw⟩\langle \mathrm{W}_w,\mathrm{R}_w\rangle. Proof: Let ⟨Ww,Rw,Vw⟩\langle \mathrm{W}_w,\mathrm{R}_w,\mathrm{V}_w\rangle be a model based on ⟨Ww,Rw⟩\langle \mathrm{W}_w,\mathrm{R}_w\rangle, and fix x∈Wwx\in \mathrm{W}_w. πw=xπx=w if wRwπx=x if not wRwπy=y if y≠w and y≠x \begin{array}{l} \mathrm{\pi} w=x \\ \mathrm{\pi} x=w\text{ if }w\mathrm{R}w \\ \mathrm{\pi} x=x\text{ if not }w\mathrm{R}w \\ \mathrm{\pi} y=y\text{ if }y\neq w\text{ and }y\neq x \end{array}

(iii) ⟨Ww,Rw⟩\langle \mathrm{W}_w,\mathrm{R}_w\rangle is a frame for S′\mathrm{S}'. Proof: From (i) and (ii).

(iv) S‡S′\mathrm{S}\ddagger \mathrm{S}' conservatively □□-extends S\mathrm{S}. Proof: By (iii), for each ⟨W,R⟩∈Γ\langle \mathrm{W},\mathrm{R}\rangle\in\mathrm{\Gamma} and w∈Ww\in \mathrm{W} there is a frame ⟨Ww,Rw⟩\langle \mathrm{W}_w,\mathrm{R}_w\rangle for S′\mathrm{S}' such that w∈Www\in \mathrm{W}_w and {x∈W:wRx}={x∈Ww:wRwx}\{x\in \mathrm{W}:w\mathrm{R}x\}=\{x\in \mathrm{W}_w:w\mathrm{R}_wx\}. By Proposition 5, S‡S′\mathrm{S}\ddagger \mathrm{S}' conservatively □□-extends S\mathrm{S}.

(v) For each α∈L□\mathrm{\alpha}\in \mathrm{L}_□ there is a first-degree formula 1α1\mathrm{\alpha} such that ⊢K45α≡1α\vdash_{\mathrm{K}45}\mathrm{\alpha}\equiv1\mathrm{\alpha}. (vi) S‡S′\mathrm{S}\ddagger \mathrm{S}' conservatively ◼\blacksquare-extends S′\mathrm{S}'. Proof: Suppose that α∈L□\mathrm{\alpha}\in \mathrm{L}_□ and ⊢S‡S′Iα\vdash_{\mathrm{S}\ddagger \mathrm{S}'}\mathrm{I}\mathrm{\alpha}. What must be shown is that ⊢S′α\vdash_{\mathrm{S}'}\mathrm{\alpha}. By (v), ⊢K45α≡1α\vdash_{\mathrm{K}45}\mathrm{\alpha}\equiv1\mathrm{\alpha}, so ⊢S′α≡1α\vdash_{\mathrm{S}'}\mathrm{\alpha}\equiv1\mathrm{\alpha}, so ⊢S‡S′Iα≡I1α\vdash_{\mathrm{S}\ddagger \mathrm{S}'}\mathrm{I}\mathrm{\alpha}\equiv \mathrm{I}1\mathrm{\alpha}, so ⊢S‡S′I1α\vdash_{\mathrm{S}\ddagger \mathrm{S}'}\mathrm{I}1\mathrm{\alpha}. Since ⊢S′α≡1α\vdash_{\mathrm{S}'}\mathrm{\alpha}\equiv1\mathrm{\alpha}, ⊢S′α\vdash_{\mathrm{S}'}\mathrm{\alpha}.

If S1⊆S′\mathrm{S}_1\subseteq \mathrm{S}' and S2⊆S′\mathrm{S}_2\subseteq \mathrm{S}' then S1‡S2\mathrm{S}_1\ddagger \mathrm{S}_2 conservatively □□-extends S1\mathrm{S}_1 and conservatively ◼\blacksquare-extends S2\mathrm{S}_2 (as does S1†S2\mathrm{S}_1\dagger \mathrm{S}_2).

Since S2⊆S′\mathrm{S}_2\subseteq \mathrm{S}', S1‡S2\mathrm{S}_1\ddagger \mathrm{S}_2 conservatively □□-extends S1\mathrm{S}_1. By parallel reasoning, S1‡S2\mathrm{S}_1\ddagger \mathrm{S}_2 conservatively ◼\blacksquare-extends S2\mathrm{S}_2.

PROPOSITION 9. Let S\mathrm{S} and S′\mathrm{S}' be systems such that S′\mathrm{S}' extends K45 and S†S′\mathrm{S}\dagger \mathrm{S}' (or S‡S′\mathrm{S}\ddagger \mathrm{S}') conservatively □□-extends S\mathrm{S} and conservatively ◼\blacksquare-extends S′\mathrm{S}'. Then S′\mathrm{S}' is the smallest extension of K45 containing all first-degree theorems of S\mathrm{S}.

PROOF. Suppose that ⊢Sα\vdash_{\mathrm{S}}\mathrm{\alpha} and α\mathrm{\alpha} is first-degree. PROPOSITION 10. If the system S\mathrm{S} admits the rule of disjunction, and □α1∨⋯∨□αn□\mathrm{\alpha}_1\vee\cdots\vee□\mathrm{\alpha}_n is a theorem of the Solovay extension of S\mathrm{S}, then ⊢Sαm\vdash_{\mathrm{S}}\mathrm{\alpha}_m for some mm (1≤m≤n1\leq m\leq n).
