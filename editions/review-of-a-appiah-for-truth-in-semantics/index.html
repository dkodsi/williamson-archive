Review of A. Appiah, For Truth in Semantics

Timothy Williamson

Linguistics and Philosophy 13 (1) (1990): 129–135.

For Truth in Semantics is in the same sense against justified assertion in semantics. Anthony Appiah’s polemic (his word) in Blackwell’s new series Philosophical Theory argues that realist theories of meaning should be preferred to anti-realist ones, in Michael Dummett’s terminology: the key semantic notion should be that of the condition for a declarative sentence to be true, not for its assertion to be justified by evidence. The book should be read by anyone at graduate level or above who is inclined to favour anti-realist semantics; it is not guaranteed to reduce that inclination, but it does present a formidable array of difficulties for the anti-realist to overcome.

Appiah begins by sketching his preferred form of realism, on which sentences have the truth-conditions of the beliefs they express. Conventions linking sentences and beliefs are roughly sketched. As stated (pp. 10–12), they run foul of irony and perhaps metaphor, seeming to require me (as an English speaker) to use the English sentence ‘Brutus is an honourable man’ with its normal truth-conditions only if I intend thereby to give other English speakers who hear me reason to believe that I believe that Brutus is an honourable man. The reader is referred for more details to Appiah’s other book Assertion and Conditionals (Cambridge University Press, Cambridge, 1985), where at p. 150 special provision is made for utterances with an ironic intonation: but only the crassest forms of irony (e.g., sarcasm) require special intonation (one can be ironic in writing). No doubt these cases are difficult for any theorist. There is also a problem about self-contradictory and Moore-paradoxical sentences; they have truth-conditions, but there do not seem to be regularities linking their assertion to the appropriate beliefs. An appeal to the compositional structure of the sentences might help, but its evaluation would require more theoretical context than Appiah provides (there is a similar difficulty on p. 148, where an account of the functionalist individuation of belief uses in effect the simplifying assumption that beliefs are finite in number and unstructured: structure is crucial to the individuation of belief, and the reader needs a more detailed picture of Appiah’s envisaged treatment). It is assumed (p. 17) that competent speakers must actually recognize the conventions of their language as conventions; this ignores Tyler Burge’s plausible argument to the contrary (‘On Knowledge and Convention’, The Philosophical Review, 84 (1975), pp. 249–55).

Appiah divides anti-realism into a negative programme – the critique of realist semantics – and a positive one – the development of anti-realist semantics; his main targets for both are Dummett and Crispin Wright. The negative programme turns on the idea that speakers can associate sentences only with such states of affairs as they can recognise to obtain. E. J. Craig is said to have shown that anti-realists have no argument for this verificationist constraint. However, Appiah goes on to distinguish between two forms of it: strong verificationism requires speakers of a language to be able to verify its true sentences as true in their current circumstances; weak verificationism only requires them to be able to verify those sentences in appropriate, perhaps counterfactual circumstances. He then argues that only weak verificationism is plausible, whilst only strong verificationism threatens realist semantics. The latter contention is doubtful. For example, no sentence of the form ‘PP and it is never verified that PP’ can be verified as true; nevertheless, for a realist some sentences of that form are true, since there are many unimportant facts that nobody will ever bother to ascertain. I have argued elsewhere that Dorothy Edgington’s attempt to resolve this problem, which Appiah endorses (p. 86), by using the idea of knowledge in one situation of what is the case in another yields a form of verificationism too weak to be relevant. Let ‘weakish verificationism’ mean a weak verificationism without Edgington’s modifications. Thus realism is incompatible with weakish verificationism. Conversely, those who find weakish verificationism compelling have reason to avoid realism. I have also argued elsewhere that the case of sentences of the above form can be reconciled with weakish verificationism in the context of an anti-realist theory of meaning that yields a non-classical logic. At this point Appiah may reply that he is free to allow exceptions to weakish verificationism. However, those with anti-realist sympathies may suspect that Appiah’s functionalist theory of belief ultimately commits him to weakish verificationism, on the grounds that absolutely unverifiable states of affairs would have no functional role with respect to belief. Appiah has not yet shown that suspicion to be misplaced.

Appiah usefully emphasizes the fact that many (if not all) empirical claims are to be justified by defeasible evidence, in contrast to Dummett’s paradigm of mathematical proof. Anti-realists cannot easily transfer accounts of the logical constants from mathematical to empirical language. There are, however, at least two senses of ‘defeasible’. An assertion is defeasible1 just in case evidence for it is always consistent with its falsity; it is defeasible2 just in case evidence for it is always controvertible by further evidence. Appiah does not clearly distinguish between these two notions (p. 41). Neither obviously implies the other. On an externalist view of evidence, the fact that I remember eating truffles is evidence for my assertion ‘I have eaten truffles’; since the evidence entails the truth of the assertion, the latter is not defeasible1, but it is defeasible2, since any evidence for it could be controverted by misleading further evidence that what I ate were not truffles. Conversely, on an internalist view of evidence, the assertion ‘We are not brains in vats having experience as though we were not brains in vats’ may be defeasible1 without being defeasible2, if no evidence can either entail or controvert it. The latter case is of course a very special one: an internalist view of evidence would entitle Appiah to assume that almost all empirical assertions are defeasible in both senses, and his anti-realist opponents to seem to have chosen to use such a view in their theorizing. Nevertheless, one might wonder about the prospects for anti-realism based on an externalist view of evidence. The contrast with mathematical language would then be less thorough-going, for although almost all empirical assertions would be defeasible2, the same could be said of mathematical assertions, for even if I have seen a valid proof of a theorem, I should believe the opposite if mathematicians whom I know to be better than I tell me that it has been discovered to contain a subtle fallacy; as already noted, some empirical assertions would be like mathematical ones in not being defeasible1. Appiah is hardly to be blamed for not going into this under-explored territory.

The attack on the positive programme begins with an argument that an anti-realist semantics for a past language will violate the strong verificationism on which the negative programme is supposed to be premissed. The point is roughly that the anti-realist will state our twentieth century AD understanding of first century BC Latin in biconditionals such as:

ASS1:
‘Alea iacta est’ was assertible for Caesar […] iff Caesar had evidence that the die was cast. (p. 93)

Pretend, for the sake of argument, that neither ‘“Alea iacta est” was assertible for Caesar’ nor ‘Caesar had evidence that the die was cast’ is currently verifiable as true or as false. Then ASS1 itself is supposed to be a currently unverifiable anti-realist claim, so that strong verificationism would make as much trouble for anti-realism as for realism. The argument assumes that if ‘PP iff QQ’ is currently verifiable then so are PP and QQ. This requirement is implausible (put P=QP = Q). The anti-realist could allow a current verification of ASS1 to consist in evidence justifying the assertion that if in the future we have evidence justifying the assertion that ‘Alea iacta est’ was assertible for Caesar then we shall have evidence justifying the assertion that Caesar had evidence that the die was cast, and vice versa; this would be quite in the spirit of the intuitionist semantics for mathematical language. Appiah’s objections to such replies (pp. 103–4) depend on his assumption that the anti-realist believes the two halves of ASS1 (or other sentences like them, p. 94) to be verification-transcendent. Perhaps a better view for the anti-realist is that no sentences are verification-transcendent, but that some would be were the realist correct.

Appiah goes on to argue that, whatever the status of strong verificationism, anti-realist theories of meaning are unacceptable, whether they follow a Davidsonian truth theory, but with ‘true’ replaced by ‘assertible’ (as Wright has suggested), or take some other form (Wright has replied in ‘Can a Davidsonian Meaning-theory be Construed in Terms of Assertibility?’, at pp. 287–316 of Realism, Meaning and Truth, Blackwell, Oxford, 1987). The key idea, following Robert Brandom, is that for many sentential operators O\mathrm{O} the assertibility-condition for OP\mathrm{O}P is not a function of the assertibility-condition for PP, since there are co-assertible sentences PP and QQ (i.e., it is necessary that PP is assertible iff QQ is assertible) such that OP\mathrm{O}P and OQ\mathrm{O}Q are not co-assertible; thus an assertibility-conditional theory could not make sense of the compositionality of meaning. For instance, ‘It is assertible that Caesar cast the die’ and ‘Caesar cast the die’ seem to be co-assertible, but, if we have no evidence as to whether Caesar cast the die or not, ‘It is not the case that it is assertible that Caesar cast the die’ is assertible while ‘It is not the case that Caesar cast the die’ is not. Appiah considers the suggestion that the anti-realist might distinguish between the meanings of PP and QQ in such cases by working with deniability- as well as assertibility-conditions; he objects that treating PP and its negation independently would also violate compositionality (pp. 135–7). This reply assumes that the denial of a sentence is conceived simply as the assertion of its negation, but the anti-realist might conceive it as a separate speech act (compare the Quinean scheme of assent, dissent and abstention): the equivalence of the denial of PP with the assertion of its negation would be thought of as defining negation, not denial. Appiah’s discussion of such a possibility assumes that deniability-conditions are still to be explained in terms of assertibility-conditions (p. 121); this leaves open a modified anti-realism on which speakers must know both conditions in order to understand a sentence, neither needing to be explained in terms of the other. However, it can be argued on Appiah’s behalf that ‘It is assertible that PP’ and ‘PP and it is assertible that PP’ are both co-assertible and co-deniable; then if O…=\mathrm{O}\ldots =‘It is possible that (… and it is not the case that PP)’, ‘It is possible that (it is assertible that PP and it is not the case that PP)’ and ‘It is possible that ((PP and it is assertible that PP) and it is not the case that PP)’ should be co-deniable: but the latter is deniable and the former is not, when the assertibility of PP does not guarantee its truth. Another line for the anti-realist is to argue that the realist has a parallel difficulty in explaining how ‘John believes that PP’ and ‘John believes that QQ’ can have different truth-conditions even when PP and QQ do not; since realists may distinguish at this point between PP and QQ on the basis of their internal structure, why may anti-realists not do the same? Appiah objects (pp. 143–4) that it makes sense to think of O\mathrm{O} as structure-sensitive only if O\mathrm{O} itself has to do with states, as of a computational agent, which may depend on structured representations (e.g., ‘John believes that…’), and that many of the operators which give anti-realists this problem are not of that kind (e.g., ‘It is not the case that…’). The point is not decisive, for anti-realists can reply that they are concerned to explain how the assertibility of OP\mathrm{O}P depends on the assertibility of PP, and assertibility does have to do with states, as of a computational agent, which may depend on structured representations. Since they have not actually provided such an explanation, however, the reply is a somewhat hollow one.

Appiah offers an explanation for the anti-realists’ difficulties. It is that they seek to individuate beliefs (as expressed by sentences) solely in terms of ‘input claims’ about the internal states of the individual and the causal impact of the environment on them, whereas a correct functionalist account must individuate beliefs in terms of both input claims and output claims about the causal impact of the internal states on the environment; beliefs have effects on action as well as causes in perception (pp. 152–3; note that the anti-realist does make claims about actions of a special kind, assertions; ‘output claim’ should exclude these). Now, schematically, it is plausible that a belief-individuating functionalist theory must contain output claims as theorems, and it is plausible that assertibility-conditional theories of meaning do not contain output claims as axioms, but what Appiah does not show is that input claims together with some a priori truths linking the concepts of belief, desire, intention and action cannot entail output claims. Some of Dummett’s remarks about the need for harmony between the conditions for an utterance and the consequences of it are suggestive in this respect. It might be objected that no relation between inputs and apparently assertoric noises would suffice to show that one was dealing with a mind at all: but that is not obviously correct where those noises are of sufficiently complex apparent structure, and sensitive to sufficiently complex conditions. Appiah’s diagnosis remains unproven, though promising.

For Truth in Semantics is clearly and unpretentiously written and full of interesting detail. Unfortunately, it is marred by what seems to be over-hasty composition; the reader is often having to work out how much the correction of some oversight would damage Appiah’s overall argument. Many slips are incidental. The negation of a sentence is defined as the result of negating its main verb (p. xiii; consider ‘Some dogs like cats’ and ‘Either he goes or I go’); the phrase ‘We have seen already’ (p. 13) refers to no preceding passage; a quotation is misinterpreted (p. 120); some arguments are confusingly expressed (e.g., at pp. 150–1, 155). The reader may also be confused by statements such as ‘For the realist, however, the beliefs we ascribe are not instrumentalist hypotheses but real states of persons’ (p. 6), where ‘the realist’ refers not to everyone who accepts a realist (i.e., truth-conditional) theory of meaning but only to those who think like Appiah. Other problems are more serious. Intuitionism in mathematics is said to demand that ‘there should actually be some procedure that guarantees that we should eventually come to confirm (or disconfirm) each sentence’ (p. 44); intuitionists reject the Law of Excluded Middle precisely because they do not regard this demand as reasonable. It is assumed that a theory with an unverifiable consequence cannot be confirmed (p. 67); why should it not be confirmed by an inference to the best explanation of some of its verifiable consequences, since confirmation does not entail truth? The reader is informed that ‘the conjunction of all (finitely statable) truths is itself a (finitely statable) truth’ (p. 86); what if there are infinitely many finitely statable truths? Realists are told to acknowledge without controversy that those who understand a sentence know when evidence justifies its assertion and when not (p. 145); since I do not know whether my evidence justifies the assertion ‘Mallory and Irvine died without reaching the summit of Everest’, I presumably do not know what it means. It is said that ‘what we should believe in the face of external evidence, is what we would believe if we were CPAs’ (computationally perfect agents, p. 154); what I should believe when I look at my logic notebook is that I have no idea whether AA is a theorem of arithmetic, but is that what I would believe if I were a CPA?

Anthony Appiah has written a useful book. With more care, it would have merited the phrase ‘state of the art’.
