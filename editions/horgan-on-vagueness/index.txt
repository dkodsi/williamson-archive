# Horgan on vagueness

Timothy Williamson

Grazer Philosophische Studien 63 (2002): 273–285.

Summary

The paper is a critique of Terry Horgan’s transvaluationist theory of vagueness. It argues that Horgan’s formulations equivocate between a semantic ‘ought’ and a semantic ‘is’. On one reading, transvaluationism is trivially inconsistent. On another reading, it is consistent, but also consistent with an epistemic account of vagueness. In addition, the paper criticizes Horgan’s attempt to recruit supervaluationism as a form of transvaluationism and his argument against vagueness in the world.

In a recent series of provocative and wide-ranging papers, Terry Horgan has developed and defended what he calls a ‘transvaluationist’ account of vagueness.1 In Horgan’s words:

Transvaluationism makes two fundamental claims concerning vagueness. First, vagueness is logically incoherent in a certain way: vague discourse is governed by semantic standards that are mutually unsatisfiable. But second, vagueness is viable and legitimate nonetheless; its logical incoherence is benign rather than malevolent. (p. 313)

Horgan argues that the only genuine alternative to transvaluationism is epistemicism, the view according to which vague discourse satisfies the standards of classical logic and truth-conditional semantics in virtue of sharp boundaries of whose location speakers are irremediably ignorant.2 This brief paper will explore some difficulties for transvaluationism. Unfortunately, one of the claims which the paper rejects is that transvaluationism and epistemicism exhaust the options for a theory of vagueness, so the arguments against transvaluationism are not ipso facto arguments for epistemicism.

# 1.

The first challenge is to understand the fundamental claims of transvaluationism. In particular, what does Horgan mean by saying that ‘vague discourse is governed by semantic standards that are mutually unsatisfiable’? More generally, what does it mean to say that some discourse is ‘governed’ by some semantic standard? If being governed by a standard is the same as satisfying it, then the first claim of transvaluationism is that vague discourse satisfies semantic standards that are mutually unsatisfiable. Since that claim involves an immediate inconsistency, it is presumably not what Horgan intends. Although the transvaluationist embraces logical incoherence in some sense, he does not do so with complete abandon; he avowedly retains sufficient logical discipline to avoid making logically incoherent statements (p. 316). More generally, transvaluationism avoids inconsistency only if being governed by a semantic standard does not entail satisfying it. Therefore, Horgan must allow that a discourse can be governed by a standard without satisfying it, perhaps as people may be governed by laws without obeying them. On that analogy, transvaluationism assimilates participants in vague discourse to subjects governed by a legal code such that, whatever they do, they disobey some law or other.

The legal analogy does not suffice to define a notion of governance appropriate to semantics. Legal norms are too distant from semantic norms in the source of their authority. Can we understand the claim that the semantic standards governing vague discourse are jointly unsatisfiable by seeing how Horgan defends it? He attempts to derive it from the premise that (in the terminology of Sainsbury 1996) vagueness is boundarylessness. Since Horgan interprets most non-epistemicists as accepting this premise, he takes them to be implicitly committed to the transvaluationist claim of incoherence.

What is boundarylessness? Consider a sorites sequence of statements such as ‘A man with 0 hairs on his head is bald’, ‘A man with 1 hair on his head is bald’, …, ‘A man with $10^{17}$ hairs on his head is bald’. According to Horgan:

To say that vagueness is boundarylessness is to say that in such a sequence, (1) initially there are true statements (with each predecessor of any true statement being true); (2) later there are false statements (with each successor of a false statement being false); and (3) there is no determinate fact of the matter about the transition from true statements to false statements. (p. 314)

This is puzzling. Conditions (1)-(3) do not use the notion of governing or the notion of a semantic standard. Are (1)-(3) themselves supposed to be the semantic standards in question? If so, Horgan has defined the thesis that vagueness is boundarylessness so that it implies that vague discourse satisfies those standards, rather than merely being governed by them. In particular, by (3), vagueness is boundarylessness only if there is no determinate fact of the matter about the transition from true statements to false ones (whatever that means). If there is such a determinate fact of the matter, even though it contravenes semantic standards, then vagueness is still not boundarylessness.

In arguing that boundarylessness implies logical incoherence, Horgan seems indeed to argue that (1)-(3) are not mutually satisfiable. Here is the core of his argument:

for any pair of adjacent statements, the two statements must have the same semantic status (truth, falsity, indeterminateness, or whatever). Otherwise there would be a determinate semantic transition between them, contrary to the claim that there is no determinate fact of the matter about semantic transitions in the sequence. (p. 315)

In other words, he infers from (3) that adjacent statements have the same semantic status, which involves an obvious conflict with (1) and (2).3^{,}4 Horgan therefore appears to be making all three of the following claims:

(A)
Vagueness is boundarylessness.
(B)
If vagueness is boundarylessness, then vague discourse satisfies (1)-(3).
(C)
(1)-(3) are not mutually satisfiable.

For he endorses (A) in so many words, (B) is in effect one half of the equivalence by which he defines boundarylessness, and (C) is the conclusion of the argument just cited. Since (A)-(C) form a jointly inconsistent triad, something has gone wrong.5

(A) and (C) might be jointly consistent with the following revision of (B), in line with Horgan’s official statement of transvaluationism:

(B*)
If vagueness is boundarylessness, then vague discourse is governed by (1)-(3).

Could Horgan modify his explanation of ‘boundarylessness’ to replace (B) by (B*)? That would involve abjuring the claim that vague discourse satisfies (1)-(3). In particular, since Horgan takes (1) and (2) to dominate (3) in determining truth for vague discourse (pp. 315-16), it would involve abjuring the claim that vague discourse satisfies (3). In that case he would no longer be denying the possibility that there is a determinate fact of the matter about the transition from true statements to false ones in a sorites series: to say that it is forbidden is not to say that it does not occur. But now transvaluationism is beginning to sound less like an alternative to epistemicism than a special case of it, one according to which vague terms flout semantic norms by having hidden sharp boundaries.6

It is hard to know what Horgan has in mind. His transvaluationism appears to involve some confusion between a semantic ‘ought’ and a semantic ‘is’.

# 2.

An additional source of obscurity in Horgan’s explanation of ‘boundarylessness’ is the phrase ‘no determinate fact of the matter’. The notion of determinateness is notoriously unclear, and ‘fact of the matter’ seems merely to rephrase the difficulty. We can address the problem more rigorously by considering Horgan’s attempt to show that ‘iterated supervaluationism does not avoid the logical incoherence of boundarylessness’ (p. 323). On the supervaluationist account of vagueness, a vague statement is true if it comes out true on all permissible interpretation of the language. That is one way of attempting to explicate the notion of a determinate fact of the matter. By ‘iterated supervaluationism’ Horgan means a version of the approach that takes account of higher-order vagueness by applying supervaluationist techniques throughout a hierarchy of metalanguages; thus ‘permissible’ is itself subject to different permissible interpretations.7

Let ‘B(…)’ abbreviate ‘A man with … hairs on his head is bald’. Horgan correctly observes that the supervaluationist will accept this disjunction:

(!)
$[\mathrm{B}(0) \mathbin{\&} \sim\mathrm{B}(1)] \vee [\mathrm{B}(1) \mathbin{\&} \sim\mathrm{B}(2)] \vee \ldots \vee [\mathrm{B}(10^{17}-1) \mathbin{\&} \sim\mathrm{B}(10^{17})]$

For on every permissible interpretation one disjunct of (!) is true, although no disjunct of (!) is true on every permissible interpretation. Thus (!) is true on every permissible interpretation, and therefore true by supervaluationist lights. Horgan then argues:

suppose we do a forced march (as I call it) left to right through the respective disjuncts in statement (!), asking with respect to each disjunct in turn, “Is it true?” Each of these questions is perfectly meaningful. And for none of these questions could the answer be “Yes”, under iterated supervaluationism. For, to say of any disjunct in statement (!) that it is true would be to commit oneself to a sharp semantic transition in the relevant sorites sequence, contrary to the vagueness and boundarylessness of ‘bald’. Thus, an advocate of iterated supervaluationism is committed to affirming statement (!), and under forced-march querying would be forced to say, of each respective disjunct in statement (!), that it is not true. This being so, iterated supervaluationism does not avoid the logical incoherence of boundarylessness. (p. 323, statement label altered)

Supervaluationists have two notions of truth, truth on all permissible interpretations (supertruth) and Tarskian truth ($\mathrm{truth}_{\mathrm{T}}$). Tarskian biconditionals of the form ‘“$\mathrm{S}$” is $\mathrm{true}_{\mathrm{T}} \equiv \mathrm{S}$’ are true on every permissible interpretation; biconditionals of the form ‘“$\mathrm{S}$” is supertrue $\equiv \mathrm{S}$’ are not, for ‘$\mathrm{S}$’ may be true on a permissible interpretation without being supertrue on that interpretation. Supertruth may be equated with determinate $\mathrm{truth}_{\mathrm{T}}$. In the question ‘Is it true?’ on Horgan’s forced march, does he mean supertruth or $\mathrm{truth}_{\mathrm{T}}$ by ‘true’?

If Horgan is asking ‘Is it supertrue?’, then the supervaluationist will happily answer ‘No’ to each question, for no disjunct is true on every permissible interpretation. Thus (!) is a supertrue disjunction with no supertrue disjunct. That is a familiar supervaluationist phenomenon, which can be elicited without appeal to a sorites series. If p is borderline then it is true on some permissible interpretations and false on others, so p is not supertrue and $\sim p$ is not supertrue, but $p \vee \sim p$ is supertrue; that is how supervaluationists can accept the law of excluded middle while rejecting the principle of bivalence. Of course, one might object to the idea that a true disjunction may have no true disjunct, but that is another matter; it is not Horgan’s argument. He has not shown that the supervaluationist’s negative answer concedes that adjacent statements in the sorites series must have the same semantic status. If $\mathrm{B}(n) \mathbin{\&} \sim\mathrm{B}(n+1)$ is not supertrue, it does not follow that $\mathrm{B}(n) \equiv \mathrm{B}(n+1)$ is supertrue. On this reading, Horgan has not forced the supervaluationist into any kind of logical incoherence.

Perhaps Horgan intends to ask ‘Is it $\mathrm{true}_{\mathrm{T}}$?’. Then the answer ‘Yes’ is tantamount to asserting the disjunct in question, and the answer ‘No’ is tantamount to asserting its negation. But in some cases neither the disjunct nor its negation will be supertrue. The iterated supervaluationist may well think that it is correct to give an answer only if it is supertrue (determinately $\mathrm{true}_{\mathrm{T}}$), and therefore that in some cases it is neither correct to answer ‘Yes’ nor correct to answer ‘No’. The supervaluationist can say that by way of reply. Again, this is not to concede that adjacent statements have the same semantic status, and Horgan has still not forced the supervaluationist into any kind of logical incoherence. But even if the supervaluationist chose to answer ‘Yes’ to one of the questions, thereby assenting to $\mathrm{B}(n) \mathbin{\&} \sim\mathrm{B}(n+1)$, that would be on the understanding that the correctness of an answer required $\mathrm{truth}_{\mathrm{T}}$ rather than determinate $\mathrm{truth}_{\mathrm{T}}$; since there would be no commitment to the determinate truth of $\mathrm{B}(n) \mathbin{\&} \sim\mathrm{B}(n+1)$, there would be no commitment to a determinate fact of the matter about the transition, which is what a violation of boundarylessness would require.

Horgan has therefore failed to show that the iterated supervaluationist is implicitly committed to transvaluationism. Although it would be good news for epistemicism if transvaluationism turned out to be the only alternative, it is too good to be true. Moreover, the failure of Horgan’s attempt also casts doubt on his original argument from boundarylessness to logical incoherence, for iterated supervaluationism provides a way of accepting the premise while denying the conclusion. Thus the appeal to boundarylessness does not motivate the drastic claim of transvaluationism as Horgan had hoped.

# 3.

According to Horgan, transvaluationism entails that there is no ontological vagueness, by which he means that there are no vague objects, properties or relations. His key thought is that ontological vagueness would require some item to have ‘features that are ontological analogs of the mutually incompatible semantic requirements that are in force in vague discourse’ (pp. 317-18).8 For instance, if an object such as a mountain is vague in spatial extent, then there is a sorites series of points for which initially there are points within its boundaries (with each predecessor of any point within its boundaries also being within its boundaries); later there are points not within its boundaries (with each successor of any point not within its boundaries also not being within its boundaries); and for each pair of adjacent points, one is within the boundaries if and only if the other is. The first two of these conditions correspond to (1) and (2) respectively in Horgan’s explanation of ‘boundarylessness’; the third condition corresponds to the one that he attempts to derive from (3), although we have just seen that supervaluationists will reject his derivation.

Horgan’s argument is that since vague objects would satisfy ‘ontological analogs of the mutually incompatible semantic requirements that are in force in vague discourse’, there are no vague objects. But, as already noted, vague expressions do not satisfy ‘the mutually incompatible semantic requirements that are in force in vague discourse’; they are merely governed by them. If vague objects, properties and relations were genuine ontological analogues of vague expressions, they would be governed by ‘ontological analogs of the mutually incompatible semantic requirements that are in force in vague discourse’ without satisfying them. Horgan provides no argument to show that objects, properties and relations cannot be governed by mutually incompatible requirements without satisfying them. Of course, it is far from clear what it would mean for mind-independent objects such as mountains to be ‘governed’ by ‘requirements’ in this normative sense, but Horgan does not seem to be appealing to that point. His argument against ontological vagueness trades on the confusion between satisfying and being governed by which we have observed elsewhere in his case for transvaluationism.

The diagnosis of a flaw in Horgan’s reasoning against ontological vagueness is confirmed by his extension of the argument to conclude that truth for vague discourse does not consist in a direct correspondence with the world, since that would require vague objects, properties and relations to correspond to vague expressions:

Truth is semantic correctness under the operative semantic standards; moreover, there are indeed truths involving vague discourse. So, since the semantic standards governing vague discourse exhibit a kind of logical incoherence, truth for vague discourse cannot be a matter of direct language-world correspondence; for if it were, then the world itself would be logically incoherent, which is impossible. (p. 320)

This argument involves the same equivocation as before, now packed into the phrase ‘logical incoherence’. For present purposes, the relevant notion of a vague ontological item is simply the notion of the direct referent (if any) of a vague expression. A vague object, property or relation in that sense need not be governed by ‘ontological analogs of the mutually incompatible semantic requirements that are in force in vague discourse’; there is no need for mountains to be somehow rule-governed. If there are vague objects, properties and relations, they will satisfy whatever consequences for the referents of vague expressions follow from the semantic requirements which those expressions satisfy. That is no problem for ontological vagueness, because vague expressions do not satisfy mutually unsatisfiable semantic requirements.

Truth for vague discourse may after all be ‘a matter of direct language-world correspondence’. Although Horgan argues additionally against such a correspondence by invoking other considerations, such as naturalism and ontological parsimony (p. 321), those further arguments are undeveloped in his work on vagueness. They will not be assessed here.

# 4.

The idea that vagueness involves some kind of logical incoherence is not new. It can be found in Frege, perhaps even in the scepticism of the Academy under Carneades, and certainly in many recent authors.9 Transvaluationism embodies the idea in a comparatively mild form. One of its two fundamental claims is that vague discourse retains a kind of logical discipline; moreover, the view allows that true, informative statements are often made in vague terms. If transvaluationism is interpreted as logically consistent, then its upshot seems to be that speakers of a vague language inevitably violate its semantic norms. They say things which they are semantically required not to say, or fail to say things which they are semantically required to say. Since transvaluationist discourse is itself vague (for example, the phrase ‘semantic requirement’ is extremely vague), transvaluationists in particular inevitably violate the semantic norms of their own discourse. Like everyone else, they are playing a game with inconsistent rules. As already noted, this view is consistent with, although not a consequence of, epistemicism, for epistemicism is a view about which semantic requirements we satisfy, not about which semantic requirements we are governed by. Horgan’s claim of logical incoherence is not as radical as it sounds, if it is consistent with epistemicism.

A striking consequence of transvaluationism is that we cannot change the rules of our game to make them consistent, for transvaluationism acknowledges that vagueness is an inevitable consequence of our cognitive limitations and claims that logical incoherence is an inevitable consequence of vagueness. Whence do these harsh semantic norms gain their unassailable authority? Once speakers realize that the semantic standards are not jointly satisfiable, why must they continue to feel answerable to those standards? Are they somehow hardwired into us? But surely beings of some other species could lack that bit of hardwiring while still thinking and speaking vaguely, in which case transvaluationism would not have captured the essence of vagueness, for it would be insufficiently general.

Semantic normativity is a notoriously obscure topic. Horgan says very little about its nature, certainly not enough to answer the questions just raised. He does make it fairly clear that the source of the normativity cannot derive from distinctively linguistic conventions, for he applies transvaluationism to vague thought as well as vague language: ‘Parallel to talk […] of semantic standards governing correct assertibility, would be to talk about conceptual standards governing the proper application of concepts’ (1995: pp. 122-3). But that only deepens the mystery.

Precision is not the default condition for thought and language, avoidable only by special devices to induce acceptance of inconsistent standards. It is not hard to be sloppy even without such special devices. What the supposed government by inconsistent standards might explain is not the phenomenon of vagueness itself but the human tendency to accept the major premises of sorites paradoxes. Such an explanation would be perfectly consistent with the falsity of those premises. Creatures who thought and talked vaguely without being governed by inconsistent standards might see our tendency to be puzzled by sorites paradoxes as a psychological quirk of our species. They might find epistemicism boringly obvious.

The confusion between satisfying standards and being governed by them leaves the supposed status of those standards unclear. For we have already seen that when Horgan introduces the semantic standards in question, (1)-(3), he treats them as though they were conditions which vague discourse does in fact satisfy, rather than conditions which it somehow ought to satisfy. In particular, he writes as though his point were that there is no determinate fact of the matter about the transition from true statements to false statements, rather than that there ought not to be such a determinate fact of the matter. As a result, the supposed normativity is never properly justified or explained. We have therefore been given no good reason to think that vague discourse is governed by semantic norms of the kind in question.

Of course, another interpretation of transvaluationism is possible, on which normativity is not the issue. That is the interpretation on which the transvaluationist makes the three immediately inconsistent claims (A)-(C) and blames the inconsistency on vagueness. But Horgan seems to concede that epistemicism is a consistent position. If the choice amongst theories of vagueness is between immediate inconsistency and epistemicism, the latter is surely the better alternative.

On the interpretations canvassed here, the transvaluationist claim that vague discourse is logically incoherent is consistent with itself only if it is consistent with epistemicism. Perhaps Horgan has something quite different in mind. If so, we may look forward to finding out what it is.10

# Notes

[1] See Horgan 1994, 1995, 1998. Unless otherwise specified, page references are to Horgan 1998, on which I concentrate as a recent statement of his view.

[2] For an exposition and defence of epistemicism see Williamson 1994; for some further considerations see Andjelković and Williamson 2000. Horgan argues against this form of epistemicism in his 1997, to which Williamson 1997: pp. 255-8 is a reply.

[3] The ‘must’ in the quoted passage cannot sensibly be read as a semantically normative ‘ought to’, for then Horgan would be inferring a determinate fact of the matter about a semantic transition from the mere absence of a norm requiring the two statements to have the same semantic status; that would be an obvious non sequitur. Presumably, ‘must’ simply signals the conclusion of his inference.

[4] As Horgan formulates (1)-(3), his argument involves the assumption that truth and falsity are mutually exclusive, which has been denied for vague discourse (Hyde 1997; see Andjelković and Williamson 2000 for some discussion). Epistemicists and most others are of course happy to grant the assumption. Moreover, even those who think that borderline statements are both true and false do not wish to conclude that all statements in the sorites series are both true and false, which would follow from (1), (2) and the claim that adjacent statements are the same in semantic value.

[5] The inconsistency may depend on the assumption that vague discourse exists. Since transvaluationism is a theory of vague discourse, that assumption is not in question.

[6] Roy Sorensen has explored a similar position in as yet unpublished recent work.

[7] See Fine 1975: pp. 287-98, Williamson 1994: pp. 156-64 and McGee and McLaughlin 1995.

[8] Horgan adds ‘and are all undefeated’; that is, they are not defeasible requirements for which a defeating condition is realized.

[9] See Williamson 1994: pp. 27-8 for Carneades, pp. 37-46 for Frege and pp. 165-84 for more recent authors.

[10] In Horgan’s earlier discussions of vagueness (1994 and to some extent 1995) he makes much of a three-valued logic for vague discourse, which he also applies at the meta-level to take account of higher-order vagueness. The three values are ‘true’, ‘false’ and ‘neither’. Like other many-valued logics, it runs into the problem of penumbral connections (Fine 1975, Williamson 1994: pp. 135-8). For example, suppose that Barry and Harry are identical twins who are balding in perfect parallel; their scalps are in exactly the same state. They are bald to exactly the same degree. Therefore, however far the process has gone, the statement ‘Barry is bald and Harry is not bald’ is false. We can formalize that statement as $Bb \mathbin{\&} \sim Bh$ (Horgan also has a second negation, $\neg$, but since the falsity condition for $\neg Bh$ is the same as for $\sim Bh$ it would make no difference for the following argument). According to Horgan’s rules, $Bb \mathbin{\&} \sim Bh$ is false only if either $Bb$ is false or $\sim Bh$ is false, and $\sim Bh$ is false only if $Bh$ is true. Thus, if $Bb \mathbin{\&} \sim Bh$ is false, then either $Bb$ is false or $Bh$ is true, in which case, since $Bh$ is true only if $Bb$ is true, $Bb$ is either true or false. But Horgan wants $Bb$ to be neither true nor false in a borderline case. To avoid this inconsistency, he must deny that ‘Barry is bald and Harry is not bald’ is false when both men are borderline cases. That denial is counterintuitive. His three-valued logic is not discussed in the main text for two reasons: he does not claim that it avoids logical incoherence, and Horgan 1998 presents transvaluationism as a general view not dependent on any particular form of non-classical logic or semantics.
