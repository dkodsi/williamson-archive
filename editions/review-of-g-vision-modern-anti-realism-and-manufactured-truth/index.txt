# Review of G. Vision, Modern Anti-Realism and Manufactured Truth

Timothy Williamson

The Philosophical Review 101 (2) (1992): 420–422.

Gerald Vision makes the Correspondence theory of truth the core of global realism. On his first shot, the theory says that “what is true is so by virtue of its correspondence with something in the not-essentially-mind-dependent world” (25). This is later replaced by a revision of J. L. Austin’s account.

Correspondence: A statement made with sentence $\mathrm{S}$ is made true by the specific state of affairs which $\mathrm{S}$-as-used demonstrates being of a type discriminated by the type of $\mathrm{S}$, qua sentence. (128)

The denial of essential mind-dependence has dropped out in the official version. However, what matters to Vision is just that Correspondence should be consistent with mind-independence; it is then up to a local realism to assert “that a certain fragment of language has truth-values founded in a distinctive sort of cognition-independent fact” (139). The second half of his book attacks globally antirealist views which rule out all such local realisms by injecting cognition-dependence into the very nature of truth. Michael Dummett, Thomas Kuhn and some readings of Wittgenstein receive special attention.

One antirealist strategy would concede Vision’s Correspondence, but argue that only cognition-dependent types of states of affairs can be discriminated. It may claim, for example, that a sentence type discriminates a state of affairs type only in virtue of its use by speakers of the language, and that their use must be insensitive to states of affairs whose obtaining they cannot recognize. Vision makes some telling points against arguments of this kind. However, antirealists may be motivated by a failure to find any mechanism for discriminating unrecognizable states of affairs. Vision does not attempt to supply such a mechanism, and gives no substantive account of discrimination. “Not much can be said to elucidate further what it is for a sentence-type to discriminate a type of situation” (129; Vision identifies situations and states of affairs). The antirealists’ suspicions are unlikely to be allayed, even though their arguments do not compel realists to share their suspicions.

A distinction can be drawn between recognition-independent and recognition-transcendent types of states of affairs. $\mathrm{X}$ is recognition-immanent iff necessarily, $\mathrm{X}$ obtains only if it can be recognized to obtain; otherwise, $\mathrm{X}$ is recognition-transcendent. $\mathrm{X}$ is recognition-dependent iff necessarily, $\mathrm{X}$ obtains only if it does so because it can be recognized to obtain; otherwise, $\mathrm{X}$ is recognition-independent. Recognition-dependence entails recognition-immanence but not vice versa, so recognition-transcendence entails recognition-independence but not vice versa. Vision could thus claim that Correspondence secures at least recognition-independence, on the simple grounds that it does not include recognition of the token state of affairs as belonging to the type in what makes the statement true (‘$\mathrm{X}$ is made true by ---’ is a hyperintensional context for ---), while the antirealist suspicions point at most to recognition-immanence. Recognition-independence might then be offered to the intuitions of the realist, even if recognition-transcendence must be sacrificed to those of the antirealist (cf. 15–17). However, few realists will be satisfied with a point of such slight metaphysical consequence. The point of substance is that truth has been restricted to recognizable truth, whether or not the restriction is written into the official account of truth-making.

A more promising strategy would use recognition-independence as a base from which to argue for recognition-transcendence, no doubt by consideration of specific kinds of statement (cf. 18–20). Unfortunately, Correspondence is ill fitted to play a role in such an argument. It is embedded in neither systematic theory nor familiar usage; one can give a clear sense to its explanans only through one’s independent understanding of its explanandum; it is not far short of useless.

One problem may be allowed to stand for many. The sentence type ‘This is longer than that’ might be expected to discriminate the type of situation in which something is longer than something else. By ordinary lights, that type of situation is the type of situation in which something is shorter than something else. Given parity of treatment, the sentence types ‘This is longer than that’ and ‘This is shorter than that’ then discriminate the same situation type. But tokens of these types will demonstrate the same situation token, if uttered in the same context with no change in the reference of ‘this’ or of ‘that’. Thus one statement is made true if and only if the other is, according to Correspondence. In order to avoid this result, the concept of a situation must be refined. The realist should not say that the sentence type ‘This is longer than that’ discriminates the type of situation in which something referred to by ‘this’ is longer than something referred to by ‘that’, for this situation type obtains only if someone uses the words ‘this’ and ‘that’, whereas what makes the statement true is intended (here at least) not to involve minds at all. Rather, a situation should be taken as something like an ordered sequence; ‘This is long than that’ could discriminate the type of situation in which the first member is longer than the second—or it could discriminate the type of situation in which the second member is longer than the first. One must choose between these equally good alternatives. The point is that even very simple sentences force the defender of Correspondence not so much to expound the concepts of discrimination and situation as to construct them. Correspondence is not an account of truth; it is a vague gesture in the direction of one.

Modern Anti-Realism and Manufactured Truth has many worthwhile things to say about important and difficult issues. Unfortunately, it is written in so turgid a style that only the most determined reader will find out what they are.
