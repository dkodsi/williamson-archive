# Replies to King, deRosset and Kment

Timothy Williamson

Analysis 76 (2016): 201–222.

It is very gratifying to see what thoughtful, ingenious and expertly worked out responses Modal Logic as Metaphysics has elicited from Jeffrey King, Louis deRosset and Boris Kment. Each of them questions an aspect of my case for necessitism, the view that necessarily everything is necessarily something. King challenges the initial reasons in Section 1.2 for taking necessitism to be so much as a live option in metaphysics. In different ways, both deRosset and Kment criticize the argument in Section 3.6 for taking a realist understanding of the usual model theory for quantified modal languages to favour necessitism over contingentism. None of them will be surprised to see that I have not been convinced. I hope that the robustness of the contested parts of the case for necessitism in the face of such astute critics will itself strengthen that case.

# 1 Reply to King

In ‘Timothy Williamson on the contingently concrete and non-concrete’, Jeffrey King raises various challenges with the aim of provoking me to develop the initial case in Modal Logic as Metaphysics for treating necessitism as a live theoretical option. In that aim, he has succeeded.

King starts with a cluster of methodological challenges. They can be summed up by the question: how far do our pre-theoretical beliefs constrain inquiry? To make progress, any inquiry must circumscribe the theoretical options within manageable limits. We must be able to dismiss some theories as obviously false. As King hints, if only formally inconsistent theories count as obviously false, then although necessitism gets over that bar, it is too low to be useful. There are infinitely many formally self-consistent but mutually inconsistent theories on just about any topic. King wonders what formally consistent ‘substantive philosophical claims’ meet my standard of obvious falsity.

As I understand it, obviousness – unlike formal consistency and inconsistency – depends on the circumstances, more specifically on background knowledge, and so changes over time as background knowledge changes. Thus the claim that the earth is a flat disk is obviously false now, but was not obviously false ten thousand years ago. When I call a claim ‘obviously false’ without qualification, I mean that it is obviously false in our current circumstances, on our current state of knowledge.

Here is an example of an obviously false claim:

(1)
Every blue-eyed human has mental properties irreducible to physical properties; no other human has mental properties irreducible to physical properties.

(1) is a formally consistent substantive philosophical claim, but it is not worth taking seriously. It is obviously false. Blue-eyed people are obviously not different from other humans in any such metaphysically deep way (however exactly we cash out the terms ‘mental’, ‘physical’ and ‘irreducible’). By contrast, neither (2) nor (3) is obviously false:

(2)
Every normal human has mental properties irreducible to physical properties.
(3)
No normal human has mental properties irreducible to physical properties.

A key difference between (1) and both (2) and (3) is that (1) is an ad hoc, gerrymandered hypothesis, whereas (2) and (3) are not. Unlike (2) and (3), (1) resembles ‘All emeralds are grue’. In the natural sciences too, any hypothesis worth taking seriously is surrounded by a cloud of alternative hypotheses too ad hoc and gerrymandered to be worth taking seriously. Example: the formally consistent but obviously false hypothesis that Newton’s laws exactly hold on a monarch’s Silver Jubilee in the UK but at no other time and place. Thus I am setting the bar for not being obviously false much higher than mere formal consistency. Nevertheless, I maintain, the necessitist principle that necessarily everything is necessarily something comfortably gets over the bar. In particular, it is neither ad hoc nor gerrymandered; it is simple and elegant.

However, a formally consistent hypothesis may be obviously false without being either ad hoc or gerrymandered. Example: the hypothesis that all planets are perfect spheres. King wants a clearer independent standard by which to judge whether we can dismiss a given hypothesis at the outset of inquiry. A quick answer is that we can dismiss the hypothesis if and only if we already know it to be false. But, of course, it is sometimes highly controversial whether that criterion rules out a given hypothesis. For instance, at the outset of inquiry some philosophers take themselves to know that necessitism is false.

Another way of posing the question is to ask what evidence we have at the outset of inquiry. Can’t we dismiss any hypothesis inconsistent with our evidence? But it is not plausible that there is a neutral operational standard of evidence, on whose application all sides can agree (Williamson 2007: 210–14). This is not some pathological characteristic distinctive to philosophy. In the natural sciences too, when something e is offered as evidence against a hypothesis h, it is always in principle open to proponents of h to challenge the status of e as evidence, although of course they are expected to support their challenge with arguments that do not simply assume the truth of h. For instance, they may offer reasons to suspect that an experiment may have been done wrong, or misreported, or misinterpreted; in extreme cases, they may even be entitled to allege fraud. No evidence in science is simply immune to such challenges. Of course, we do have plenty of evidence, but its boundaries are contested. In philosophy as in the natural sciences, we should not expect to start with a body of evidence demarcated by a theory-neutral, operationally effective standard immune to subsequent challenge.

Rather than vainly attempt to articulate an operationally effective standard of evidence, I will compare the case of necessitism, the necessity of existence, with that of another contested principle of quantified modal logic, the necessity of identity.1 To keep the two cases independent, we may put the (necessary) necessity of identity in a form neutral between necessitism and contingentism: necessarily, if things are identical, then necessarily if one of them exists they are identical. For comparison, we may put the (necessary) necessity of existence in this form: necessarily, if a thing exists, then necessarily it exists. Marcus (1947) published a proof of the necessity of identity in 1947. Nevertheless, for several decades thereafter many philosophers regarded it as obviously false; a few still do. The work of Saul Kripke (1971, 1980) played a central role in breaking down resistance, by carefully explaining what the principle implies and does not imply, when the modality in it is read as metaphysical.

The necessity of identity is now, for good reason, a widely accepted principle of modal logic and metaphysics. But, within living memory, many philosophers took it to be inconsistent with our pre-theoretic modal knowledge. The alleged counterexamples were of the obvious sort: Hesperus is identical with Phosphorus, but in easily imagined circumstances Hesperus would (according to them) have been distinct from Phosphorus; thus Hesperus could have been distinct from Phosphorus (while both still existed). In effect, they claimed to know on the basis of pre-theoretic reflection that Hesperus could have been distinct from Phosphorus. On the standard view, such philosophers did not know that which they claimed to know, for what they claimed to know was false. Of course, it does not follow that they did not believe that which they claimed to know. There is no need to deny that they were entertaining the contents they took themselves to be entertaining. What they got wrong was which attitude they were taking to those contents: it was belief, not knowledge. They falsely believed that Hesperus could have been distinct from Phosphorus. What they did know was that it could have been that a planet called ‘Hesperus’ appeared at a certain point in the sky at one time and another planet called ‘Phosphorus’ appeared at a certain point in the sky at another time, and so on, but they falsely believed that in those circumstances the former planet would have been Hesperus and the latter Phosphorus.

Similar points apply to the necessity of existence. Like the necessity of identity, it is a principle of modal logic and metaphysics; unlike the necessity of identity, it is not yet widely accepted. Many philosophers take it to be inconsistent with our pre-theoretic modal knowledge. The alleged counterexamples are of the obvious sort: I exist, but in easily imagined circumstances I would (according to them) have not existed; thus I could have not existed. In effect, they claim to know on the basis of pre-theoretic reflection that I could have not existed. On the necessitist view, such philosophers do not know that which they claim to know, for what they claim to know is false. Of course, it does not follow that they do not believe that which they claim to know. There is no need to deny that they are entertaining the contents they take themselves to be entertaining. What they get wrong (according to necessitists) is which attitude they are taking to those contents: it is belief, not knowledge. They falsely believe that I could have not existed. What they do know is that it could have been that my parents never met, I was never born, and so on, but they falsely believe that in those circumstances I would have been nothing at all.

Given these extensive methodological parallels between supposedly common sense denials of the necessity of existence and supposedly common sense denials of the necessity of identity, it is unlikely that any theory-neutral, operationally effective methodological standard supports supposedly common sense dismissals of the necessity of existence but would not have supported supposedly common sense dismissals of the necessity of identity in the 1950s and 1960s. Although someone might claim that the pre-theoretic case against the necessity of existence is more plausible than the pre-theoretic case against the necessity of identity, what theory-neutral, operationally effective way of measuring degrees of plausibility are we supposed to rely on? Thus contingentists face a challenge to say in what way, if any, they take pre-theoretic dismissals of the necessity of existence to be on methodologically firmer ground than pre-theoretic dismissals of the necessity of identity.

In the second half of his paper, King turns to necessitist accounts of various sorts of property. Unlike some contingentists, he issues no general demand for non-modal explanations of objects’ modal properties. Nevertheless, on a necessitist view, contingently nonconcrete objects have many modal properties of a sort that looks far from basic, such as the property of being a possible coin.2 King doubts whether necessitists can explain in more basic terms why a given contingently nonconcrete object has a given non-basic property. If no such explanation is forthcoming, he suggests, that is a bad outcome for necessitism.

In the book, I indicate by example a strategy for meeting an explanatory challenge like King’s (389). The general idea is this. Suppose that the non-basic modal property in question is that of being possibly F. Consider first the presumptively non-modal property of being F; it stands to the modal property as the property of being a coin stands to the property of being a possible coin. Can we explain what it is to be F in more basic terms, presumably by articulating in more basic terms a necessary and sufficient condition for being F? If not, the property of being possibly F may well inherit some explanatory intractability from the property of being F, but since that intractability was not introduced by the modality, it is not to the present purpose. So we may as well assume that we can explain in more basic terms what it is to be F: to be F is to be B, where ‘B’ stands in for a complex expression built up out of more basic terms. As a corollary, being B is necessary and sufficient for being F. But now we can also explain in more basic terms what it is to be possibly F: to be possibly F is to be possibly B. As a corollary, being possibly B is necessary and sufficient for being possibly F. Consequently, we can explain in more basic terms why an object is possibly F: it is possibly F because it is possibly B. The explanation is constitutive rather than causal, but a constitutive explanation is what we were after.

King objects to the strategy that since the book assumes a coarse-grained conception of properties on which necessarily coextensive properties are identical, being possibly B is the same property as being possibly F, so the explanans (the object is possibly B) is the same as the explanandum (the object is possibly F), making the explanation trivially circular. However, when an explanation involves a property, the value of the explanation may depend on how the property is characterized, not just on which property it is. Consider King’s example (in a slightly different connection): ‘To be a chunk of gold is to be a piece of a solid composed of the element whose atomic number is 79.’ One might usefully utter that sentence when asked to explain what the property of being a chunk of gold is. It would be far more explanatory, physically and metaphysically, than just saying ‘To be a chunk of gold is to be a chunk of gold’. But the quoted sentence is a property identity; it identifies the property of being a chunk of gold with the property of being a piece of a solid composed of the element whose atomic number is 79. Although ‘being a chunk of gold’ and ‘being a piece of a solid composed of the element whose atomic number is 79’ denote the same property, on the coarse-grained view of properties, only the latter description does so in a way that locates it within the space of chemical properties. If reductions are identities, it does not follow that they are unexplanatory.

Here is a variant of King’s objection: if being possibly B is the same property as being possibly F, then being possibly B is no more basic than being possibly F. That is true, but irrelevant. The original claim was not that the property of being B was more basic than the property of being F, but that ‘B’ abbreviated a complex expression built up out of terms (such as ‘atomic number’) more basic than the expression ‘F’. Those more basic terms should refer to properties more basic than that of being F; there is no need for the complex expression itself to do so. To vary the example, if we explain a non-basic property as a disjunction of more basic properties, only the disjuncts, not the disjunction, should be more basic than the original property; the disjunction should be exactly as non-basic as the original property, because we are postulating their identity.

Of course, some philosophers reject the coarse-grained conception of properties; they hold that distinct properties may be necessarily coextensive. But since the coarse-grained conception is neutral between necessitism and contingentism, that issue is orthogonal to present concerns. In any case, the identity of being a chunk of gold with being a piece of a solid composed of the element whose atomic number is 79 is plausible even if it is not taken to follow from their mere necessary co-extensiveness.

If one wants, one can do much of the explanatory work with properties different from the original one of being F. For instance, if being B1 is sufficient for being F, then being possibly B1 will be sufficient for being possibly F (where ‘B1’ stands in for a complex expression built up out of more basic terms). If, in addition, being B1 is not necessary for being F, then in many cases (not always) being possibly B1 will not be necessary for being possibly F, so being possibly B1 will be a distinct property from being possibly F. Nevertheless, if some possibly F object is also possibly B1, one may explain why it is possibly F by noting that it is possibly B1, which implies that is possibly F. Similarly, if being B2 is necessary for being F, then being possibly B2 will be necessary for being possibly F (where ‘B2’ stands in for a complex expression built up out of more basic terms). If, in addition, being B2 is not sufficient for being F, then in many cases (not always) being possibly B2 will not be sufficient for being possibly F, so being possibly B2 will be a distinct property from being possibly F. Nevertheless, if some not possibly F object is also not possibly B2, one may explain why it is not possibly F by noting that it is not possibly B2, which implies that it is not possibly F. However, there is no general need to bring in further properties like B1 and B2 in explaining why an object is possibly F or why it is not possibly F.

King has another objection to the strategy. Let o be a contingently nonconcrete object that could have been a chunk of gold. Consider the purported explanation ‘o is a possible chunk of gold because o is a possible piece of a solid composed of the element whose atomic number is 79’. King objects that although it is informative about gold, it does not explain what it purports to explain. His worry seems to be that it gives no further information specifically about the object o beyond what is contained in the explanandum itself, ‘o is a possible chunk of gold’, together with general information about the nature of gold. But that worry does not depend on the modal element in the purported explanation. It remains when the modal element is eliminated. Let o* be a chunk of gold. Consider the purported non-modal explanation ‘o* is a chunk of gold because o* is a piece of a solid composed of the element whose atomic number is 79’. It gives no further information specifically about the object o* beyond what is contained in the explanandum itself, ‘o* is a chunk of gold’, together with the same general information as before about the nature of gold. But it is quite unclear what sort of further information we should expect about o*, especially on essentialist assumptions. For many contingentist essentialists, o* could have failed to be a piece of a solid composed of the element whose atomic number is 79 only by failing to exist. For many necessitist essentialists, o* could have failed to be a piece of a solid composed of the element whose atomic number is 79 only by failing to be concrete. We may be close to bedrock here, for reasons unconnected with necessitism. Likewise for the purported modal explanation about o: by S5 modal logic (which King is not challenging), since o is a piece of a solid composed of the element whose atomic number is 79, o could not have failed to be a possible piece of a solid composed of the element whose atomic number is 79. What further information should we expect about o? If you want to know what the possible worlds are like in which o is a piece of a solid composed of the element whose atomic number is 79, nothing in principle stops a necessitist from giving you detailed further information about that. It is quite unclear what sort of explanatory demand King has in mind that a necessitist cannot supply.

For King, a further concern about the necessitist’s contingently nonconcrete objects is that they are ‘peculiar’, because their natures are almost entirely modal. He thinks that ‘for a lot of philosophers, the more peculiar an entity is seen to be, the harder it is to believe in it without overwhelmingly good reason’. Of course, perceived peculiarity is highly correlated with unfamiliarity. The number 0 and the null set seemed pretty peculiar at first, but mathematicians and many philosophers got used to them after a while. If philosophers are reasonably cautious about accepting new theories with unfamiliar consequences, who can object? However, what King meant to be exploring is not philosophers’ unwillingness to accept unfamiliar theories (such as necessitism) at first sight but rather their willingness to reject them at first sight. The history of physics and mathematics gives little support to the methodological assumption that reality contains no peculiar entities, or at any rate as few of them as we can get away with. At this level of generality, a more open-minded approach seems appropriate.

King’s final source of resistance to necessitism is essentialism. As noted in the book, necessitism requires many essentialist principles to be restricted. Given a tiger t, it is not necessary that if t exists then t is a tiger, although it may be necessary that if t is concrete then t is a tiger. We should not be surprised if the essentialist principles we first thought of do not hold with strict generality. There are arguments quite independent of necessitism for that conclusion (Mackie 2006). Common sense is most at home with easily possible cases; why should it be able to survey in advance all the non-standard ways of conceiving children that scientists can devise, or all the sorites series for composition that metaphysicians can devise? Some watering-down of essentialist principles was what we had anyway to expect. But King has a more general concern: that necessitism undermines the motivation for any essentialist principles at all. If contingency in an object’s properties infects even something as basic as concreteness, why should any of its properties be immune?

Admittedly, by itself the pure modal logic of necessitism provides no argument for non-trivial essentialism. But, equally, by itself nor does the pure modal logic of contingentism. Since King is concerned with pre-theoretic resistance to necessitism, the relevant motivation for essentialist principles should also be of a pre-theoretic kind. Presumably, it concerns our common sense knowledge of various specific counterfactactual scenarios. Necessitism does not mandate scepticism about such alleged knowledge. Although the necessity of existence requires the modification of some specific tempting modal claims, so did the necessity of identity. For the time being, necessitists can draw support for essentialist principles from our pre-theoretic knowledge of modality much as contingentists can. In the long run, one may hope for well-supported, systematic and informative general theories about what goes into the essences of members of various kinds, and why. But at present our understanding of what essences should include is too inchoate to judge what difference it would make to use a necessitist or a contingentist background modal logic.

I close with one comment on King’s characterization of my case for necessitism. He writes: ‘Williamson’s main argument in favour of necessitism over contingentism is that in a variety of ways it offers a superior treatment of quantified modal languages.’ That is not quite right. Necessitism and contingentism differ primarily over logic and metaphysics, not semantics. Of course, in possible worlds model theory, necessitists prefer constant domains while contingentists prefer variable domains, but the model theory is not the same as the intended interpretation. The two sides might describe the intended interpretation of the modal language in the same modal terms, yet disagree on whether a given formula is true on that interpretation. A key argument against one major type of contingentist second-order modal logic is that it is too weak to support modal versions of normal mathematical reasoning (282–288). The problem is not with the contingentist’s treatment of the second-order modal language, but with the theory they formulate in that language. Even logical theories must be distinguished from the languages in which they are formulated.

# 2 Reply to deRosset

In ‘Modal logic and contingentism’, Louis deRosset responds to the discussion in Modal Logic as Metaphysics (Ch.3) of the relationship between the standard mathematical model theory of quantified modal logic and some metaphysical questions raised by quantified modal logic on its standard philosophical interpretation. Although deRosset finds extensive common ground with me, he rejects one of the chapter’s key arguments (in Section 3.6). I will explain why his objections do not move me.

From a technical perspective, the model theory of modal logic in the tradition of Kripke (1963) is an epic success story. It has generated deep understanding and a wide range of applications. Nevertheless, the model theory is in itself just one more branch of mathematics, no more modal in content than any other branch of mathematics. Although each model involves a set $\mathrm{W}$ often informally imagined and described as the set of ‘possible worlds’ of the model, and a designated member $w_0$ of $\mathrm{W}$ often informally imagined and described as the ‘actual world’ of the model, such loose talk plays no official role whatsoever in the model theory. Any old set $\mathrm{W}$ of things of any kind, and any old member $w_0$ of $\mathrm{W}$, will do for a model. The talk of ‘worlds’ here is merely heuristic. It directs our attention to one philosophically salient application of the mathematics, and thereby helps one see the point of the formal definitions. Similarly, although each model for quantified modal logic may have a domain function $\mathrm{D}$ that maps each member w of $\mathrm{W}$ to a set $\mathrm{D}(w)$, often informally imagined and described as the set of ‘inhabitants’ of the ‘world’ w, the things that ‘exist in’ w, that loose talk also plays no official role whatsoever in the model theory. Any old function $\mathrm{D}$ from members of $\mathrm{W}$ to sets will do for a model. The talk of ‘existing in a world’ here is merely heuristic too. When it comes to interpreting formulas of the formal object-language of quantified modal logic over models, the definitions of key model-theoretic notions such as ‘the formula A is true in the model $\mathrm{M}$’ are given in purely non-modal set-theoretic terms.

In metaphysics, however, we want to use the formal object-language of quantified modal logic with a specific interpretation, in order to articulate claims of interest with more precision and clarify their logical interrelations. Typically, we read the modal operators $□$ and $◇$ as expressing metaphysical necessity and metaphysical possibility, and the quantifiers as expressing unrestricted generality. For the book, that is the intended interpretation. Once we have such an interpretation of the object-language in mind, it makes sense for us to classify its formulas as true or false (on the intended interpretation). Of course, the language may contain all sorts of non-logical expressions, such as names and predicates, whose interpretations are of no special metaphysical interest. We may therefore generalize them away. The universal generalization of a formula is the result of replacing all such non-logical constants by variables of the same type and then prefixing universal quantifiers on all free variables (here I pass over various subtleties discussed in the book). A formula is metaphysically universal if and only if its universal generalization is true (on the intended interpretation). The set of metaphysically universal formulas of the language of quantified modal logic constitutes a core metaphysical theory of the structure of modal reality. Unfortunately, it is sometimes hard to know whether a given formula belongs to that theory – whether it is indeed metaphysically universal.

Philosophical discussions of modality are often insufficiently clear about the distinction between the mathematical model theory and the metaphysics. We need to see the gap between them before we start bridging it. What, if anything, corresponds to metaphysical universality in the model theory?

The first step is to generalize on the non-logical constants in the model theory just as we did in defining metaphysical universality. Each model contains not only the set $\mathrm{W}$, its designated member $w_0$ (also an accessibility relation over $\mathrm{W}$, which we can ignore for present purposes), and a domain function $\mathrm{D}$, but also a function $\mathrm{V}$ that maps each non-logical constant of the language to a suitable interpretation, an intension over $\mathrm{D}$ and $\mathrm{W}$. To achieve the desired level of generality, we simply strip out the interpretation function $\mathrm{V}$ from the model, leaving the other features intact. What is left is a frame. It is pointed in the sense that it still includes a designated member $w_0$ of $\mathrm{W}$. A pointed frame is also known as a model structure. This pointed frame or model structure is inhabited in the sense that it includes the domain function $\mathrm{D}$. A formula is valid on an inhabited model structure if and only if it is true in every model based on that inhabited model structure, in other words, true at the relevant $w_0$ under all assignments of values to variables in every such model: informally, it is true at the actual world of the model structure however the variables and non-logical constants are interpreted.

The usual way to build a bridge between an interpreted theory and its models is by specifying an intended model or class of intended models of the theory. For the present case, we have no specific interpretation of the non-logical constants in mind, so the level of models is too specific for our intentions; it makes more sense to work at the more general level of (inhabited) model structures. We might hope to single out an intended model structure. That would involve singling out the intended set of genuine possible worlds $\mathrm{W}$, the intended genuine actual world $w_0$, and the intended genuine function $\mathrm{D}$ from each world to the set of its inhabitants. As discussed in the book, there are severe obstacles to realizing that ambition. We may have to loosen our constraints on what is to count as an intended model structure in the light of what is technically or philosophically feasible. However, we can lay down one useful necessary condition on being intended. An (inhabited) model structure is intended only if it validates all and only the metaphysically universal formulas. That is, it should pick out exactly our target class of formulas. deRosset interprets me as making this a sufficient as well as necessary condition for a model structure to be intended, but it is made clear in the book that I reject the claim of sufficiency (96, 134). For if two model structures are isomorphic to each other, then one of them validates all and only the metaphysically valid formulas if and only if the other does. But a model structure isomorphic to an intended model structure need not itself be intended. For example, given an intended model structure, one could construct another model structure by substituting bananas for some of the genuine possible worlds in the former; but a model structure in which bananas play the role of possible worlds is presumably not intended. Luckily, deRosset’s misinterpretation of me on this point is not crucial to the rest of his argument. Indeed, deRosset seems willing to grant almost everything I have said up to here.

In the book, I use the necessary condition on being intended to show that if there is an intended model structure, then every instance of the Barcan formula (BF) is metaphysically universal, so true on the intended interpretation (135):

$\mathrm{BF} \to ◇\exists x\, A(x) \to \exists x\, ◇ A(x)$

Almost all contingentists hold that BF has false instances, because although there could have been something that in this actual world is nothing, there is not something that could have been nothing in this actual world. Thus the usual form of contingentism is incompatible with there being an intended model structure. If the usual form of necessitism is compatible with there being an intended model structure, then the model theory is friendlier to the usual form of necessitism than to the usual form of contingentism, by permitting an intended interpretation that verifies the former but not permitting one that verifies the latter.

The argument is simple. Suppose that there is an intended model structure IMS. Consider the formula $\exists y\, x = y$. Its universal generalization is $\forall x\, \exists y\, x = y$ (since the identity predicate ‘=’ is treated as a logical constant), which is a trivial truth of first-order logic. Thus $\exists y\, x = y$ is metaphysically universal, and so valid on IMS by the necessary condition on being intended. But if, for some ‘world’ w in IMS, $\mathrm{D}(w)$ contains something o not in $\mathrm{D}(w_0)$, then $\exists y\, x = y$ is not valid on IMS, because it is false at the ‘actual world’ $w_0$ on an assignment that makes o the value of the variable ‘x’ (since the model theory treats the quantifiers as ranging only over the domain of the ‘world’ of evaluation). Thus, for every ‘world’ w in IMS, $\mathrm{D}(w)$ is a subset of $\mathrm{D}(w_0)$. Consequently, as shown by Kripke, every instance of BF is valid on IMS. Therefore, by a second application of the necessary condition on being intended, every instance of BF is metaphysically universal. QED.

As deRosset notes, the argument relies on both the condition for being intended and the model theory. If we redefine truth in a model so that it requires only truth at $w_0$ (the ‘actual world’) under all assignments that assign only members of $\mathrm{D}(w_0)$ (‘inhabitants of the actual world’) as values of variables, then the argument is blocked: $\exists y\, x = y$ will be valid on every model structure. However, $□\exists y\, x = y$ will not be valid on every model structure, so such a stipulation makes the rule of necessitation fail even in the absence of fancy modal operators such as the rigidifying ‘actually’. In any case, deRosset thinks, ‘it’s more natural to leave the model-theoretic semantics alone’, and instead demote open formulas (such as $\exists y\, x = y$) from metaphysical universality, in effect because on their own they do not express propositions. Thus they will cease to figure in the necessary condition on being intended, which again blocks the argument. One concern about such a restriction to closed formulas is that it looks too draconian when generalized to variables of other types. For instance, it is often natural to treat ‘$P$’ as a variable in sentence position; but then the theorem $P \to P$ is an open formula and so to be excluded; only $\forall P\, (P \to P)$ counts. Similarly, it is often natural to treat ‘$F$’ as a variable in monadic predicate position; but then the theorem $\exists x\, Fx \to \exists x\, Fx$ is an open formula and so to be excluded; only $\forall F\, (\exists x\, Fx \to \exists x\, Fx)$ counts.

Fortunately, we can finesse these issues about free variables, because a variant of the original argument uses the closed formula $\exists y\, c = y$, where $c$ is an individual constant (name), in place of $\exists y\, x = y$, since the two formulas have the same universal generalization (136). The argument goes through almost exactly as before, noting that if, for some ‘world’ w in IMS, $\mathrm{D}(w)$ contains something o not in $\mathrm{D}(w_0)$, then $\exists y\, c = y$ is not valid on IMS, for in some model based on IMS the constant ‘$c$’ denotes o. The conclusion is the same.

At the end of his paper, deRosset discusses the variant argument. By contrast with his treatment of individual variables, when it comes to individual constants he does not ‘leave the model-theoretic semantics alone’. Instead, he stipulates that in any model the denotations of individual constants must belong to $\mathrm{D}(w_0)$, the domain of the ‘actual world’ of the model, which blocks the argument. Given his stipulation, $\exists y\, c = y$ becomes valid, but $□\exists y\, c = y$ does not. Thus the rule of necessitation fails again, even in the absence of fancy modal operators such as ‘actually’. deRosset is ‘inclined to agree that, other things being equal, the restriction on the interpretation of individual constants is unmotivated by contingentist lights’. However, he claims that the restriction is motivated by a stipulation that I make, when I say, ‘In a language well designed for expressing good scientific theories, the denotation of any constant is one of the values over which variables of the same type range, and the value of any variable is a legitimate denotation for a constant of the same type’ (131). Since the truth of a formula $\exists y\, A$ in a model with ‘actual world’ $w_0$ requires a value of the variable ‘$y$’ in $\mathrm{D}(w_0)$ to satisfy $A$ at $w_0$, deRosset reasons that by my lights we should similarly require the denotation of the constant ‘$c$’ to be in $\mathrm{D}(w_0)$.

What deRosset’s argument misses is that the point of my stipulation is to make the interpretations of variables and constants of the same type symmetric, in the sense that the semantic values available for the constants are all and only the values over which the variables range (or analogously for higher types). But variables may take values outside the domain of a given restricted quantifier, for instance $\mathrm{D}(w_0)$ in the case of $\exists y$. Indeed, it is crucial to the working of the semantics that in a model structure where BF fails we should be able to assign a value to a variable from outside $\mathrm{D}(w_0)$ (but in $\mathrm{D}(w)$ for some other ‘world’ w), and then evaluate formulas back at $w_0$ with respect to that assignment. In some model structures, one must do just that to falsify the necessitist principle $□\forall x\, □\exists y\, x = y$ at the ‘actual world’. Since deRosset imposes no corresponding restriction on the values of the individual variables, the effect of his restriction on the values of the individual constants is to produce an asymmetry in the interpretations of variables and constants of just the sort my stipulation forbids: $\exists y\, c = y$ becomes valid but $\exists y\, x = y$ does not. Of course, deRosset could always restore some symmetry by imposing a corresponding restriction on the variables in the definition of logical truth, so that $\exists y\, x = y$ becomes valid too – though variables would still need to be assigned values from outside the domain of the ‘actual world’ in the course of evaluating formulas, even at the ‘actual world’. But the standard model theory on which my arguments rely is fully symmetric in its treatment of variables and constants: both the value of an individual variable and the denotation of an individual constant can be any member of the domain of any ‘world’. Thus symmetry does not motivate deRosset’s restrictions; it does not favour restricting both variables and constants over restricting neither.

A more general issue for deRosset’s restrictions is that they have no model-theoretic motivation. As always, I mean the term ‘model theory’ in its usual logical sense, as referring to a mature branch of mathematical logic. As already noted, one of the chief aims of Chapter 3 of the book is to build a bridge for modal logic between the standard model theory (in the tradition of Kripke 1963) and the salient metaphysical interpretation. Much of the literature on modal logic obscures the need for such a bridge, by failing to recognize the purely mathematical nature of model theory. The bridge is desirable independently of the metaphysical dispute between necessitism and contingentism. But contingentism turns out to make a special obstacle to building a good bridge.

The mathematical model theory of quantified modal logic works fine without deRosset’s restrictions. From a model-theoretic perspective, they are just pointless and untidy minor complications. It is also rather suspicious that no corresponding restrictions suggest themselves for variables and constants of other types. Of course, from a metaphysical perspective, contingentists have ample motivation to tinker with the model theory, as my original arguments show. It is natural for them to seek a kind of model theory not susceptible to my arguments, one which holds out hope of intended model structures that do not validate necessitist claims. As noted in the book, it is not hard to devise such variant model theories (136). But they are motivated by contingentist metaphysics, not by the needs of model theory itself.

Why should it matter whether the motivation is model-theoretic or metaphysical? There has been an impression about that the model theory somehow gives contingentism some independent support. Since the model theory has been so successful, such support would carry considerable weight. Of course, the model theory does show that contingentism is formally consistent, by providing it with models. But, equally, it shows that necessitism is formally consistent, by providing it with models too. That does nothing to show which of the pair of contradictories is true. If we let the model theory develop in the way natural to it, the mathematically natural way, with no external interference from metaphysical modal theories such as necessitism and contingentism, it takes a form uncongenial to contingentism, for specific reasons not applicable to necessitism, as the arguments in the book show.

Of course, none of this amounts to anything like a refutation of contingentism. If it did, there would have been much less reason to include Chapters 6 and 7, in which I painfully build an abductive case for necessitism against contingentism. The contingentist can always take a more or less instrumentalist attitude to the model theory, rejecting the identification of interpretations in the informal sense with models in the formal sense, and insisting that contingentism is true on its intended interpretation even though it has no intended model structure. It is just that, if one takes model theory as a guide to what the relevant interpretations of the language of quantified modal logic are like, then that guide recommends scepticism about the supposed truth of contingentist claims on the intended interpretation of the language. Although one can tweak the model theory to be friendlier towards contingentism, that is just choosing a guide because he gives the advice one wants to hear.

For a different reason, necessitists as well as contingentists cannot simply take the model theory at face value. For models and model structures as standardly defined are set-theoretic structures, which assign each ‘world’ a set domain. The model theory evaluates a generalization at a ‘world’ as restricted to the domain of that ‘world’. But standard set theories tell us that no set contains absolutely everything, on pain of Russell’s paradox. Thus the model theory never really interprets the quantifiers as unrestricted. But, as already noted, the intended reading of the quantifiers in metaphysics is the unrestricted one. Thus the model theory never really delivers the intended reading of the quantifiers. This is a notorious limitation of model theory done, as it standardly is, in a set-theoretic framework.

Should we follow deRosset in concluding that, with respect to reading the metaphysics off the model theory, ‘It seems [. . .] that the necessitist is in the same boat’ as the contingentist? That response neglects important distinctions. The cardinality problem is endemic to the use of set-theoretic models. It has nothing to do with metaphysical modality, or with the dispute between necessitism and contingentism. It arises already for the interpretation of the non-modal theory of pure sets, since no set contains all pure sets (if we are doing set-theoretic model theory, we are already committed to the existence of pure sets). Conversely, the dispute between necessitism and contingentism arises even when we drastically restrict the first-order quantifier, for instance by the property of being a possible knife made from a given finite set of actual handles and blades, so that the cardinality problem does not arise. Thus the cardinality problem is orthogonal to the modal issues. We should therefore seek a way of ‘dividing through’ by the cardinality problem, since it constitutes noise rather than signal with respect to the issue of current interest.3

Higher-order logic may provide a better framework than set theory for handling the cardinality problem (Rayo and Uzquiano 1999, Williamson 2003). While noting that point in the book, in order to engage properly with the mathematical tradition I keep the treatment of model theory within the more standard set-theoretic framework by temporarily pretending that a set $\mathrm{D}$ is the intended domain of the first-order quantifiers and leaving the best ways of solving the cardinality problem and adapting the model theory to that solution as matters for informal discussion (140). In particular, under this pretence, I explain a way of faithfully interpreting formulas by mapping them to propositions. deRosset makes rather heavy weather of this procedure. He points out that if $\mathrm{D}$ has only one member, then my definition maps the formula $\forall x\, \forall y\, x = y$ to a tautology, even though the formula is not true on its intended interpretation, and so not metaphysically universal. Of course, but the link I claimed between faithful interpretations and metaphysical universality was made still under the pretence that the first-order quantifiers range only over $\mathrm{D}$ (141; the pretence is finally dropped on 144–5). deRosset has maintained the pretence for faithful interpretations but dropped it for metaphysical universality, so no wonder the link breaks. The point of my procedure is to show that the link holds whatever set $\mathrm{D}$ we restrict both faithful interpretations and metaphysical universality to, and then use that result as defeasible evidence for a link even when the restriction to a set is dropped (by a sort of informal reflection principle). The case when $\mathrm{D}$ is small makes no trouble for this procedure.

Still under the pretence that the first-order quantifiers range only over $\mathrm{D}$, necessitism makes a universal generalization $\forall x\, A$ necessarily equivalent to the conjunction of its instances $A$ as the variable ‘$x$’ is assigned each member of $\mathrm{D}$ in turn. The book exploits this necessary equivalence by identifying the proposition expressed by the universal generalization with the conjunction of the propositions expressed by the instances. deRosset has a further complaint about this identification: ‘the proposed interpretation of quantificational claims makes no contact at all with the logical structure of ostensibly quantificational propositions on which Williamson’s construction is based’ and so the claims that my argument shows to be metaphysically universal ‘are not the claims we intended to express using the quantifiers’ of the object-language. deRosset’s talk of the ‘logical structure of ostensibly quantificational propositions’ suggests that he has in mind a theory of structured propositions, on which (to a first approximation) the constituents of a complex sentence express constituents of the proposition the sentence expresses. The construction in the book employs no such fine-grained structured propositions. The theory of propositions on which it is based explicitly assumes that propositions form a Boolean algebra and so obey the usual Boolean laws. As I note, ‘The laws make the theory of propositions coarse-grained by equating Boolean equivalences’ (102). For instance, the sentence $A$ expresses the same proposition as the sentence $A \wedge (A \vee B)$, by the corresponding Boolean equation, even though the two sentences have quite different constituent structures. Such coarse-grained propositions are quite adequate for the logic of metaphysical modality, even if we want something finer-grained (perhaps of the sort deRosset has in mind) for the semantics of propositional attitude ascriptions. The theory of propositions in the book merely requires them to be fine-grained enough for the proposition that it is necessary that P to be a function $\mathrm{L}$ of the proposition that P. Thus if it is necessary that P but not necessary that Q then the proposition that it is necessary that P is true while the proposition that it is necessary that Q is not true, so the proposition that it is necessary that P is distinct from the proposition that it is necessary that Q; therefore, by the existence of the function $\mathrm{L}$, the proposition that P is distinct from the proposition that Q. The theory of propositions in the book explicitly postulates such a function $\mathrm{L}$ (103). This is what rules out the identification of propositions with truth-values, contrary to deRosset’s complaint that the variant account of $\mathrm{L}$ in terms of the structure of the Boolean algebra is vulnerable to such an identification (see p. 104; on the variant account, $\mathrm{L}1 = 1$ and $\mathrm{L}p = 0$ if $p \ne 1$, where 1 and 0 are respectively the top and bottom elements of the algebra).

With that theory of propositions, and still under the pretence that first-order quantifiers range only over $\mathrm{D}$, we can show that the necessitist has a way of constructing a natural model structure that meets the necessary (though presumably insufficient) condition on being intended, of validating all and only the metaphysically universal formulas, and so is at least a good candidate for being the necessitist’s intended model structure (140–3). deRosset responds that, if the argument works, so does a variant argument to show that the contingentist has a way of constructing a somewhat similar model structure that also meets the necessary condition on being intended, and so is a more or less equally good candidate for being the contingentist’s intended model structure. Thus necessitism supposedly loses its advantage in this respect. But deRosset’s construction satisfies the necessary condition on being intended only if we either exclude individual constants and free individual variables from consideration or give them a non-standard semantic treatment. That follows from my original arguments. Aside from cardinality problems that plague all parties alike, on the standard mathematical model theory, there is no intended model structure for contingentists, while for necessitists there may well be an intended model structure. How much philosophical weight one puts on that difference depends, in part, on how impressed one is by the successes of the standard model theory.

# 3 Reply to Kment

Boris Kment’s ‘Model theory and contingent existence’ has the same target as deRosset’s ‘Modal logic and contingentism’: the argument in Chapter 3.6 that the standard model theory of quantified modal logic makes a better fit with necessitism than with contingentism. Some of my comments on deRosset’s piece are also relevant to Kment’s. Like deRosset, he blocks the argument with a non-standard treatment of individual constants and free individual variables. Unlike deRosset, he achieves that departure from orthodoxy by a global reconception of model theory, rather than local restrictions.

Kment describes what he is doing as presenting ‘a contingentist construal of model theory’ or an ‘interpretation of model theory’ and as explaining ‘the significance of model theory’. These descriptions suggest that he is taking orthodox model theory more or less as it stands but proposing a new way of understanding what it means. That impression is reinforced by his care in making his theory deliver what look very like the usual clauses for a recursive definition of truth in a model, and so on. However, looks can deceive.

As already emphasized, standard model theory is a branch of mathematical logic. It is formulated in ordinary mathematical language, mainly the language of set theory (whether first-order or higher-order is not crucial for present purposes). Admittedly, it helps itself to some syntax, in order to refer to terms and formulas of the object-language, but for most purposes it could get by with their Gödel numbers instead. In any case, the language of standard model theory is up for reinterpretation only if the language of mathematics as a whole is up for reinterpretation. But Kment does not offer to reinterpret mathematical language in general, nor does his account lend itself to any such generalization. For instance, he does not attempt to reinterpret the membership symbol $\in$ of set theory. Rather, Kment is offering a new sort of model theory, some of whose claims can be expressed in visually similar formulas to some which express claims of standard model theory.

Helpfully, Kment introduces his style of model theory by sketching its application to non-modal first-order languages. We already get a feel for the difference between Kment-style model theory and standard model theory in this simpler case. Consider a standard model <D, V>, where D is any set of objects (the domain) and V is any function that provides extensions of the appropriate types over D for the non-logical individual and predicate constants of the given non-modal first-order language. On Kment’s approach, we should treat the members of D as variables and understand <D, V> as a perspicuous form of a ‘Ramsey sentence’ which says, very roughly, that there are variables and properties or relations defined over them such that the individual constants refer to the former, the predicate constants express the latter, and they all pattern according to V. The Ramsey sentence can be fully articulated in a richer meta-language with primitive predicates for reference, expression and truth. Kment uses this framework to state a new criterion for metaphysical universality in my sense: ‘a formula is metaphysically universal iff it is validated by all accurate Ramsey sentences’, where a Ramsey sentence is accurate ‘iff it represents the non-semantic facts correctly in all ways that matter to the truth-values of object-language sentences’.

Standard model theory needs none of Kment’s accretions. The angle brackets in ‘<D, V>’ are just the standard mathematical notation for ordered pairs (triples, . . .). Their meaning is not specific to inputs such as D and V, and is independent of any association with Ramsey sentences. Nor does standard model theory rely on semantic primitives, such as those for reference, expression, and truth. It provides explicit definitions in mathematical terms of reference in a model, truth in a model, and the extensional analogue of expression in a model. It makes no attempt to characterize model-independent reference, truth, or the analogue of expression; those matters fall outside its remit, since they depend on the non-mathematical issue of which interpretation we intend. For instance, one may study the model theory of the theory of a strict linear ordering, even though it has no single intended interpretation – the natural numbers, the integers, the rationals, and the reals, as ordered by < or >, and infinitely many more examples, all give equally good models.

These differences between standard model theory and Kment-style model theory all persist into the model theory of modal first-order languages, with more differences added. A technical difference is that standard model theory permits model structures with ‘worlds’ that cannot be reached by finitely many steps of the accessibility relation from the ‘actual world’, while Kment-style model theory does not. That is comparatively unimportant, because in the standard model theory such ‘worlds’ play no role in the evaluation of formulas of the given modal language at the ‘actual world’ of the model, and so are irrelevant to truth-value in the model. A far more significant difference is this. Kment’s articulated meta-language for such object-languages is rich in vocabulary dedicated to metaphysically modal notions: it has special variables restricted to genuine Possible Worlds (not merely whatever things play the role of worlds in a given model), a predicate ‘expressing actualization (a contingent property possessed by different Possible Worlds at different Possible Worlds)’, and a device enabling one to form modal operators for truth at a given Possible World. Thus Kment’s model theory for modal object-languages relies on a metaphysically modal meta-language. By contrast, as I emphasize in the book, standard model theory for modal languages is done in the non-modal language of mathematics.

Another difference between standard model theory and Kment-style model theory concerns the treatment of non-logical constants. Kment notes: ‘On my account, a model doesn’t determine the meanings of the non-logical constants.’ To my charge (made against Stalnaker) that such an upshot is a defect in a model theory, Kment responds:

If two meaning assignments yield the same distribution of truth-values (given the relevant non-semantic facts), then the ways in which they differ are irrelevant, in which case it can only be advantageous to abstract away from these differences.

This remark would come as a surprise to most model-theorists. In the terminology of the subject, two models are elementarily equivalent if and only if the same first-order sentences are true in them. Part of the fascination of model theory is in studying how different from each other elementarily equivalent models can be. For example, a basic fact about the model theory of first-order arithmetic is that any standard model is elementarily equivalent to a non-standard model, that is, any model with the intended structure makes the same sentences true as some model with an unintended structure (having elements that one cannot reach from 0 by adding 1 finitely many times). Such results illuminate the expressive limitations of first-order languages. To abstract away from the difference between a standard model of arithmetic and a non-standard model is to abstract away from the subject matter of model theory. Similar points apply to the model theory of modal languages. For instance, a standard model-theoretic question about a modal logic is whether it has the finite model property, in the sense that every non-theorem is false in some model with only finitely many ‘worlds’; often the key to proving that a modal logic is decidable is showing that it has the finite model property. Since the same formulas may be true in a model with infinitely many ‘worlds’ as in a model with only finitely many ‘worlds’, a model theorist cannot afford to abstract away from the difference.

One general reason why model theory cannot afford to abstract away from the differences between two models that yield the same distribution of truth-values is that model theory is one of our best means for studying the expressive differences between types of language. Two models may yield the same distribution of truth-values over sentences of a first-order language but not of a second-order language, or over the sentences of a language with the usual modal operators but not of a language with additional modal operators such as ‘actually’.

If two models are isomorphic, not just elementarily equivalent, then the differences between them are less important for standard model theory: they have the same overall structure, which is what matters for most mathematical purposes. Nevertheless, the reason for that unimportance is still not that they yield the same distribution of truth-values.

In the sentence before the one just quoted, Kment gives a clue as to how he envisages model theory:

Model-theoretic semantics for modal logic isn’t studying any specific meaning assignment but certain generalizations about all meaning assignments, namely truths of the form $\forall A$ is true on all meaning assignments.

But such truths are not what model theory mostly studies, as a glance at any textbook treatment of the model theory of modal or non-modal languages will show. Although model theory does not study any one specific meaning assignment, it does study relations between meaning assignments, in particular those that make the same sentences true. Kment drastically underestimates the autonomy of model theory as a branch of mathematical logic. He treats it as a means to a criterion for metaphysical universality, but that non-mathematical end is not on most model theorists’ agenda.

Some philosophers may react: ‘So much the worse for the philosophical interest of model theory.’ But that would be too quick. The same articles by Kripke published in the period 1959–63 played a decisive role in the development of both the technical model theory and the philosophical understanding of modal logic. That is no coincidence. Applications of the possible world framework in philosophy derive much of their utility and prestige from the technical achievements of possible world model theory, even though the latter, unlike the former, employ only a trivial characterization of ‘worlds’ (as the members of an arbitrary set $\mathrm{W}$). In Chapter 3, the main aim is to reconnect the philosophical and mathematical traditions, modal metaphysics with the model theory of modal languages. To achieve that aim, one must respect the aims and methods of each tradition. It is no good viewing the mathematical tradition through the distorting lens of the aims and methods of metaphysics (or vice versa). One must take each side for what it is, and build a bridge that actually reaches both sides – if one can. Insofar as one has to distort the mathematical development to suit one’s metaphysical preferences, one is falling short.

As I emphasize in the book, and Kment also notes, cardinality problems put a significant limit to how closely we can link the standard model theory of modal logic with metaphysicians’ intended interpretation. A standard model structure is set-sized; its ‘worlds’ form a set, as do the inhabitants of any given ‘world’, whereas it is not clear that there are only set-many maximal metaphysical possibilities and it is clear that there are more than set-many individuals (including sets) in a given maximal metaphysical possibility, such as the actual one. But such cardinality limits are effectively orthogonal to the issue between contingentism and necessitism.

Once we prescind from the cardinality limits, the argument in Section 3.6 still shows that the standard model theory of quantified modal logic makes a better fit with necessitism than with contingentism. The argument is not undermined if Kment-style model theory makes as good a fit with contingentism as with necessitism, for that is a radically different enterprise, with no comparable record of explanatory and technical achievements of its own.

The moral drawn in the book from the argument in Section 3.6 is modest: not that contingentism is false, but just that it labours under the disadvantage of having to treat the standard model theory of quantified modal logic in a more instrumentalist way than necessitism can. Of course, instrumentalism is rarely an all-or-nothing matter. Virtually every representation has some representationally insignificant features, and even extreme instrumentalist interpretations of scientific theories usually treat them as making some representationally significant predictions. Both necessitists and contingentists will say that some features of their favourite model structure are representationally significant while others are representationally insignificant. That much is obvious. The question is whether necessitists can treat more features of their favourite model structure as representationally significant than contingentists can.

It is presumably agreed that in any respect in which contingentists can treat their favourite model structure as representationally significant, necessitists can treat their favourite model structure as representationally significant too. The considerations in 3.6 show that the converse is false. From a necessitist standpoint, their favourite model structure can be taken to correctly represent the existential status of each member o of the domain of any world in that structure – it represents o as (necessarily) existing. By contrast, from a contingentist standpoint, their favourite model structure cannot be taken to correctly represent the existential status of each member o of the domain of any world in that structure. For, if it did represent the existential status of such members, it would represent any member of the domain of some world in the structure not in the domain of the actual world of the structure as (contingently) not existing, and so represent falsely because all constituents of the structure do exist. Moreover, on pain of validating the Barcan formula, contingentists must favour model structures in which some member of the domain of some world is not in the domain of the actual world. Thus contingentists cannot treat their favourite model structure as representing the existential status of the particular individuals in it, while necessitists can so treat their favourite model structure. In that way, contingentists are forced to be more instrumentalist about standard model theory than necessitists are.

It is hardly surprising that one can devise less natural gadgets for recursively evaluating formulas of quantified modal logic, which both necessitists and contingentists are forced to treat in as instrumentalist a way as contingentists but not necessitists are forced to treat standard models. That does not counterbalance the asymmetry between necessitism and contingentism noted in 3.6. To restore symmetry, one would need gadgets as natural as standard models for recursively evaluating formulas of quantified modal logic, which necessitists are forced to treat in as instrumentalist a way as contingentists are forced to treat standard models, but which contingentists can treat in a way no more instrumentalist than the way in which necessitists can treat standard models. Neither deRosset nor Kment has offered any prospect of meeting that need. Absent an equally natural alternative to standard model theory that favours contingentism over necessitism as much as standard model theory favours necessitism over contingentism, the asymmetry remains. Of course, it does not pretend to be a decisive argument by itself; rather, it forms part of the cumulative case for necessitism that the book begins to assemble.

# Notes

[1] Throughout these replies, ‘exist’ is used in the bare sense of ‘be something’, where the quantifier is unrestricted.

[2] On the view developed in the book, ‘property’ talk is best replaced by quantification into predicate position. For expository convenience, the ‘property’ talk is left as it stands here.

[3] One approach to the cardinality problem in set theory is to adopt a modal set theory, in which any things have the potential to form a set even if it is not yet actual (for versions of this approach see Parsons 1983; Fine 2006; Linnebo 2013 and Studd 2013). This might give the impression that the two issues interact, and specifically that one approach to the cardinality problem entails contingentism. But it is not so, because proponents of such modal set theories are generally explicit that the relevant modality is not metaphysical. The reason is that the cardinality problem arises even for pure sets, so the approach requires that there are potentially more pure sets than there actually are, or (to use temporal vocabulary non-literally) that there will be more pure sets than there now are. Thus the being of pure sets is contingent in the sense of the relevant modality. But it is generally agreed that the being of pure sets is not metaphysically contingent. Thus the two modalities are different, and these modal set theories are compatible with necessitism. As for what their modality is, it is harder to say.
