Précis of The Philosophy of Philosophy

Timothy Williamson

Philosophical Studies 145 (2009): 431–434.

The book defends two claims about philosophy in apparent tension with each other: that philosophical inquiry is much more like other sorts of inquiry than many philosophers have liked to pretend, and that philosophical inquiry can legitimately be pursued by traditional armchair methods. Of course, the predominance of armchair methods is itself a difference between philosophy and many other sorts of inquiry, although armchair methods are even more predominant in mathematics, and are widespread elsewhere—for instance, in physics, economics and linguistics. But the obvious difference in methodological emphasis has been taken to manifest a deeper, more radical distinctiveness in the nature of philosophy, roughly speaking on the grounds that since one cannot legitimately conduct an ordinary inquiry into some aspect of the world without getting out of one’s armchair to look at that aspect, armchair philosophizing is legitimate only if it is not an ordinary inquiry into some aspect of the world. For those unwilling to give up armchair philosophizing, the pressure was to explain what extraordinary sort of inquiry it constituted.

The phrase ‘linguistic turn’ labels a cluster of such attempts. In some versions, philosophical questions are reinterpreted as really being questions about words or concepts, even when they appear not to be. The hope is that if philosophical questions are not really about the world beyond the armchair, we can answer them without leaving the armchair. But such reinterpretation badly misconceives what many contemporary analytic philosophers are doing. For instance, the philosophy of time is about time itself, not just temporal words or concepts. We should be more willing to take philosophers’ questions at face value.

Other versions of the linguistic or conceptual turn represent philosophical questions as conceptual questions in the sense that they can be answered on the basis of conceptual or analytic truths, available from the armchair. These versions fail too.

According to metaphysical conceptions of analyticity, analytic truths are true in virtue of meaning and consequently insubstantial. Although something can be made of truth in virtue of meaning, it does not deliver the result that analytic truths are insubstantial in any way that would trivialize the task of knowing them. It does not answer the epistemological challenge to armchair methods.

According to epistemological conceptions of analyticity, understanding a word or grasping a concept often requires accepting, or at least being disposed to accept, reasoning by some relevant principles. Thus the mere linguistic or conceptual competence to engage with a philosophical question already makes available a basis for reasoning towards an answer; indeed, philosophical questions might be defined as those that can be answered from such bases. However, such accounts misconstrue the nature of linguistic and conceptual competence. Deviance on even the most elementary logical matters is quite compatible with the highest levels of linguistic competence, as can easily be observed in philosophical disputes. Even the competent speaker’s underlying inferential dispositions may be deviant. Logical competence is acquired in logic classes, not hardwired in a logic module. The idea of assent in virtue of linguistic or conceptual competence misses the holistic nature of such competence. Nothing is epistemologically analytic.

Thus appeals to analyticity fail to answer the epistemological challenge to armchair philosophy.

A case study of the epistemology of metaphysical modality makes a fresh start on the epistemological challenge to armchair methods in philosophy. The starting-point is the logical equivalence of ‘P is impossible’ and the counterfactual conditional ‘If P were the case, a contradiction would be the case’, from which one can deduce corresponding equivalences for possibility and necessity too. This suggests the hypothesis that the cognitive capacities we use in handling claims of metaphysical modality are just those that we already use in handling much more mundane counterfactual conditionals. In particular, the conceiving of which so much is made in discussions of the epistemology of modality is really just a special case of the sort of offline cognitive processing ‘in the imagination’ that is commonplace when we evaluate ordinary contingent counterfactual conditionals. The price of scepticism about claims of metaphysical modality is scepticism about a vast range of ordinary counterfactual conditionals too. For the non-sceptic, we have ways of knowing contingent claims about what would be the case if P were the case without actually making P the case; that already goes some way towards armchair knowledge. Philosophical knowledge of possibility and necessity employs the same repertoire of ways of knowing, even if in a more systematic way. None of this requires a less than robustly realist understanding of metaphysical modality and counterfactual conditionals.

One effect of the account is to undermine the significance of the distinction between a priori and a posteriori knowledge. Standardly, sense experience may play an enabling role in a priori knowledge, notably by enabling one to grasp relevant concepts, but must not play an evidential role. However, in the evaluation of modal claims and counterfactual conditionals, sense experience sometimes plays a role that is neither purely enabling nor strictly evidential. We originally rely on it to hone our skills in applying concepts online, to objects encountered in sense experience—skills that go far beyond what is required merely to possess the concepts. We then often forget the sense experiences but retain the skills, with which we apply the concepts knowledgeably. Later, we apply the same skills offline, in knowledgeably evaluating modal claims and counterfactual conditionals. Sense experiences may play no strictly evidential role; the relevant experiences are forgotten. Yet their role is not purely enabling, since they did far more than enable us to possess the concepts at issue. Although some dichotomy between ‘a priori’ and ‘a posteriori’ could be extended to such cases by brute force of stipulation, it would obscure the underlying similarities rather than cutting at the cognitive joints.

Another application of the account is to thought experiments. They turn out to be fairly straightforward modal arguments, typically valid ones, with counterfactual conditionals and possibility claims as premises. We assess the premises by the same offline methods as for other claims of the same sort. There is no special mystery about philosophical thought experiments. In particular, there is no need to postulate a special phenomenon of ‘intuition’. We need only judgments, and dispositions to judgment.

Much of the talk about intuitions as evidence in philosophy depends on more general misconceptions about the nature of evidence. It is supposed that whether something counts as part of our evidence can be determined by neutral, uncontentious means. But purported non-psychological facts about the world are often contested by revisionary philosophers, and so cannot be determined to count as part of our evidence by neutral, uncontentious means, since the revisionary philosophers would deny them the role of evidence. For instance, they would not treat hypotheses inconsistent with those facts as inconsistent with our evidence, nor would they treat conditionalization on our evidence as giving those facts probability 1. Thus our evidence is treated as consisting of psychological facts, such as the fact that we make or are disposed to make the judgments rather than the non-psychological facts those judgments purport to state. This psychologization of our evidence resembles that which occurs under the pressure of more traditional forms of scepticism, such as scepticism about the external world, although the present form of scepticism focuses on our practices of applying concepts. But the retreat to psychologized evidence hardly helps, since the psychological facts are philosophically contentious too—eliminativists about the mind deny them. The assumption that the extent of our evidence can be uncontentiously determined is a mistake, since it permits a sceptic’s peremptory challenges to wipe out our evidence. On a more realistic account, the extent of our evidence is essentially contested: but our evidence can still include known non-psychological facts about the world.

For similar reasons, accounts of the method of reflective equilibrium are unsatisfactory, since they exclude the very information about the epistemic status of judgments about particular cases that is needed to evaluate the status of their contents as evidence. Internalist assumptions are no more warranted in the epistemology of philosophy than they are in the epistemology of anything else.

Given the contentiousness of our evidence, we may wonder whether philosophy has any tendency at all to track the truth. We can gain limited but not nugatory reassurance from the reflection that the content and therefore truth-conditions of our thoughts is determined in a way that is not neutral between truth and falsity. Rather, subject to constraints of naturalness, it is constitutively determined in a way that maximizes how much knowledge we have—and knowledge entails truth. In any case, the unexceptional nature of philosophical inquiry means that scepticism about it has far more tendency than its proponents realize to generalize to scepticism about most forms of human inquiry.
