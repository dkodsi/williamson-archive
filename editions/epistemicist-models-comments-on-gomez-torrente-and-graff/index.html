Epistemicist Models: Comments on Gómez-Torrente and Graff

Timothy Williamson

Philosophy and Phenomenological Research 64 (1) (2002): 143–150.

Every philosopher should wish for commentators as thoughtful and rigorous as Mario Gómez-Torrente and Delia Graff. Their papers contribute significantly to our understanding of the fine structure of vagueness, but not by undermining its epistemicist interpretation.

According to epistemicism, there is an unknowable truth of the matter in borderline cases. Either Harry is bald or he is not, but we cannot know which. Why not? Suppose that in fact Harry is bald; the argument is similar if he is not. Then we speak truly if we say “Harry is bald”. But since the case is borderline, we might easily have uttered the same words even if our overall pattern of use had been slightly different so as slightly to shift the boundary of ‘bald’ in that context, making ‘Harry is bald’ express a different and false proposition. Thus even if we actually make the true judgement that Harry is bald in this case, we do so on an insufficiently reliable basis to count as knowing that Harry is bald. This is a conceptual analogue of more familiar kinds of ignorance, in which limits on our powers of perceptual discrimination prevent us from knowing the exact values of physical quantities by ordinary perception. Such limits on our knowledge can be expressed as margin for error principles (Williamson 1992, 1994: pp. 216–47).

Gómez-Torrente points out that in perceptual cases we can discover the values of the quantities exactly (or at least far less inexactly) by scientific measurement; the unknowability is relative to limited cognitive means. He suggests that the epistemicist explanation establishes at most a similarly limited ignorance in borderline cases, thereby missing the depth of the phenomenon to be explained.

The epistemicist explanation is as general as its underlying assumptions. In this context the relevant ones are: (a) the boundary of a vague term is unstable with respect to small changes in use because there is no natural line for it to follow; (b) because our use of such a term is not perfectly co-ordinated internally, a small change in one aspect of use (enough to shift the boundary a little) can easily be combined with preservation of another aspect (the utterance of a particular sentence in particular circumstances). Gómez-Torrente’s discussion does not bear on (a). By implication, he questions whether (b) holds independently of the cognitive means of which humans currently avail themselves. Has the epistemicist shown that no cognitive means available to us would enable us to co-ordinate our use of a vague term so that each aspect would be sensitive to every other? Perhaps, given such co-ordination, we should not still utter ‘Harry is bald’ even if other aspects of its use were slightly shifted to make it express a false proposition. Then our judgement would be reliable; we might come to know after all whether Harry was bald. Co-ordination by itself would not suffice; it would have to be achieved in a way that enabled us to know that the reference of the vague term had been preserved over the relevant contexts, otherwise we should not know our judgement to answer the same question as we began with in the original borderline case. We have no idea how to achieve such knowledge of reference preservation across sharpening, not least because the concept of reference is itself vague. What the epistemicist can freely concede is that this explanation of ignorance does not establish its metaphysical necessity for humans. It leaves open the theoretical possibility that some being might know the truth of the matter in a borderline case by cognitive means that we cannot begin to imagine, and somehow communicate that truth to us, or that we might even discover it for ourselves through new methods in semantics of which at present we have no inkling. By their very nature, such theoretical possibilities are hard to rule out. But why should we attempt to do so? It is not a datum that human knowledge is metaphysically impossible in currently borderline cases. The datum is that we cannot now fully imagine how anyone could acquire such knowledge (see also Williamson 1994, p. 212). The epistemicist account explains that datum, for we cannot now fully imagine just how the conditions from which it derives the ignorance could be violated. Nor does the epistemicist need the metaphysical impossibility of knowledge in currently borderline cases to explain the widespread impression that there is no truth of the matter there. The unimaginability of the knowledge does the work (Williamson 1997a). Thus the general considerations in section 2.1 of Gómez-Torrente’s article do not show that the epistemicist’s appeal to margin for error principles explains less than it needs to explain.

In the remainder of his article, Gómez-Torrente develops a different and more specific kind of objection to the epistemicist account of our ignorance (see also his 1997, pp. 243–45). I will assess it in the context of formal epistemicist models, where it takes its most precise form. The models use possible worlds, understood epistemically as interpretations. The relevant epistemic operator KK is modelled as a kind of epistemic necessity. Since the sentence ∼KA&∼K∼A\mathord{\sim} KA \mathbin{\&} \mathord{\sim} K\mathord{\sim} A is to express the borderline status of AA, strictly KK should not be read as “it is known that” or even “it is knowable that”, since AA might be undecidable for some reason unconnected with vagueness. Rather, KAKA means that there are no obstacles of the kind characteristic of borderline cases to knowing the truth of AA. We might therefore read KK as “clearly” or “definitely”. Both Gómez-Torrente and Graff are sensitive to these issues about the proper interpretation of KK. For present purposes it will do no harm to read KK loosely as “it is knowable that”. The model is applied to the term ‘bald’ by taking as the atomic sentences those of the form B(n)B(n), read as “Any man with nn hairs is bald” (understood with modal force) for any numeral in place of ‘nn’. Complex sentences are formed by means of KK and the usual truth-functors. KmK^m abbreviates mm iterations of KK.

Consider first what we may call the ℕ\mathbb{N} model; its worlds are indexed by the set ℕ\mathbb{N} of natural numbers. They vary in their interpretation of ‘bald’. Specifically, the atomic sentence B(n)B(n) is true at the world sis_i if and only if n<in<i; thus sis_i fixes ii as the cut-off point for ‘bald’. The world sjs_j is epistemically accessible from the world sis_i if and only if |i−j|≤1|i-j|\le 1; that is, if and only if the worlds are similar enough in the cut-off points they assign to ‘bald’. KAKA is true at sis_i if and only if AA is true at every world epistemically accessible from sis_i. The truth-functors are treated as usual.

One can easily check that in the ℕ\mathbb{N} model KmB(n)K^mB(n) is true at sis_i if and only if m+n<im+n<i. Thus at any world sis_i, KiB(0)K^iB(0) is false. Whichever world is actual, it excludes some finite number of iterations of knowability of the truth that any man with 0 hairs is bald. Gómez-Torrente finds this result implausible. He argues thus:

I certainly know that B(0)B(0) (it even appears to be part of the meaning of ‘bald’ that ‘B(0)B(0)’ is true). So it is knowable that B(0)B(0). But by reflection on what I just thought, I seem to come to know that it is knowable that B(0)B(0). So it is knowable that it is knowable that B(0)B(0). But by reflection on what I just thought, I seem to come to know that it is knowable that it is knowable that B(0)B(0). And so on, until I run out of resources allowing me to hold sufficiently complicated beliefs of this form. (2002)

The idea seems to be that if I reach the conclusion CC in a knowledgeable way then by reflection on what I just thought I can reach the conclusion that I know CC in an equally knowledgeable way. But such reasoning assumes that the knowledgeability of the way by which I reached the conclusion is somehow fully transparent to me. For otherwise the way in which I reach the conclusion that I know CC (and thereby that CC is knowable) may be less clearly knowledgeable, and its knowledgeability still less transparent. When such a process is iterated, I eventually fail to know altogether. That Gómez-Torrente is in the grip of a Cartesian picture of transparent self-reflection is also suggested by his assertion that if the predicate ‘nn appears immediately to intuition to be such that B(n)B(n)’ has a cut-off point, then:

it is absurd to claim that this cut-off point cannot be known to be such; we know both when some proposition appears immediately to intuition to be true and when it does not appear immediately to intuition to be true. (2002)

On the contrary, when I am struck by a sudden thought, it is often far from clear to me whether it appears immediately to intuition to be true, rather than being a mere passing fancy. Nor is it always clear to me when I am being struck by a thought; perhaps it is not sudden enough to be striking, or not coherent enough to be a thought (Williamson 1994: pp. 18–21). There just is no realm of cognitive transparency of the kind that Gómez-Torrente postulates (Williamson 2000: pp. 93–113). Thus his argument for the truth of all propositions of the form KmB(0)K^mB(0) is far from compelling.

Nevertheless, if someone does want a model in which all those propositions are true, epistemicism can easily provide it (Williamson 1997b: 262). For one can simply delete the world s0s_0 from the ℕ\mathbb{N} model and restrict the other stipulations to the remaining worlds. Call that the ℕ+\mathbb{N}{+} model. In this cropped model, B(0)B(0) is true at every world, so KmB(0)K^mB(0) is true at every world for every natural number mm. However, Gómez-Torrente has a different objection to the ℕ+\mathbb{N}{+} model. For KmB(1)K^mB(1) is true at sis_i if and only if m+1<im+1<i, so KiB(1)K^iB(1) is false at si−1s_{i-1}, sis_i and si+1s_{i+1}, so KiB(0)&∼KiB(1)K^iB(0) \mathbin{\&} \mathord{\sim} K^iB(1) is true at those three worlds, so K(KiB(0)&∼KiB(1))K(K^iB(0) \mathbin{\&} \mathord{\sim} K^iB(1)) is true at sis_i. Thus at any world some formula of that form is true. According to Gómez-Torrente, that is a bad result for epistemicism, because K(KiB(0)&∼KiB(1))K(K^iB(0) \mathbin{\&} \mathord{\sim} K^iB(1)) expresses the knowability of the cut-off point for the predicate KiB(x)K^iB(x). Thus at any world some such predicate has a knowable cut-off point. He generalizes the result to other cropped models in which more worlds are deleted.

Graff provides a further generalization. Consider any model in which every instance of the B (Brouwer) schema ∼A⊃K∼KA\mathord{\sim} A \supset K\mathord{\sim} KA is true at every world. Suppose that KiB(0)K^iB(0) is true at a world ww for all ii while KiB(k)K^iB(k) is false at ww for some ii and kk. Then for some mm and nn, KiB(n)K^iB(n) is true at ww for all ii while KmB(n+1)K^mB(n+1) is false at ww. By B, K∼Km+1B(n+1)K\mathord{\sim} K^{m+1}B(n+1) is true at ww. But Km+2B(n)K^{m+2}B(n) is true at ww, so by the distribution of KK over conjunction nn is a knowable cut-off point for the predicate Km+1B(x)K^{m+1}B(x). Schema B holds in all fixed margin models: those in which epistemic accessibility holds for just those pairs of worlds at most a fixed distance apart. However, that is only the simplest form of semantics for KK envisaged by the epistemicist. In the more complex variable margin models B can fail, and Graff shows with a cunning example that in some worlds in some such models all predicates of the form KmB(x)K^mB(x) have unknowable cut-off points. But she also notes that the latter models violate a natural constraint; in her terminology, they are not stepping-stone models. Graff establishes that in all stepping stone models the weakening B+ of B, ∼A⊃K∼KKA\mathord{\sim} A \supset K\mathord{\sim} KKA, holds, and it suffices for a variant on the previous argument for a knowable cut-off point.

The epistemicist is not restricted even to the wider class of variable margins models. For even they make the requirements on the margin for error independent of the content of the putative knowledge, whereas epistemicism allows propositions about knowledge of baldness to require different margins for error from those required by propositions simply about baldness, for example. The plausible assumption that the epistemicist’s intended models include variable margins models suffices, when combined with other plausible assumptions, to single out a unique logic for the KK operator (Williamson 1994: pp. 274–75). However, a version of the argument for a knowable cut-off point for some predicate of the form KmB(x)K^mB(x) goes through on the even weaker assumption that for each formula AA at each world ∼A⊃K∼KkA\mathord{\sim} A \supset K\mathord{\sim} K^kA is true for some kk. Although nothing in the letter of epistemicism commits one to that weaker assumption, rejecting it would involve a significant departure from the original picture of margins for error. On that picture, for sufficiently high kk, KkAK^kA is true only at worlds very far from those at which AA is false, so ∼KkA\mathord{\sim} K^kA is true at all worlds even fairly close to worlds at which ∼A\mathord{\sim} A is true, so the relevant obstacle to knowing ∼KkA\mathord{\sim} K^kA is absent at the latter worlds. Let us therefore provisionally accept that if KmB(0)K^mB(0) is true for all mm then the predicate KmB(x)K^mB(x) has a knowable cut-off point.

As Graff points out, even if KmB(0)K^mB(0) is true for all mm at all worlds, knowable cut-off points for predicates of the form KmB(x)K^mB(x) are problematic for epistemicism only if such predicates count as vague. Gómez-Torrente is not clearly correct to call them ‘soritical’. Let us postpone this issue for a while to investigate the formal situation further.

The knowable cut-off point for KmB(x)K^mB(x) depends on the cropping of the model to make KmB(0)K^mB(0) true for all mm. In the uncropped ℕ\mathbb{N} model, for all mm, nn and ii, KmB(n)K^mB(n) is true at sis_i if and only if m+n<im+n<i; thus KmB(n)&∼KmB(n+1)K^mB(n) \mathbin{\&} \mathord{\sim} K^mB(n+1) is true only at sm+n+1s_{m+n+1}, so K(KmB(n)&∼KmB(n+1))K(K^mB(n) \mathbin{\&} \mathord{\sim} K^mB(n+1)) at no world, so the cut-off point for KmB(x)K^mB(x) is unknowable. However, more complex predicates have knowable cut-off points in the ℕ\mathbb{N} model. For in it ∼K∼B(n)\mathord{\sim} K\mathord{\sim} B(n) is true at sis_i if and only if n<i+1n<i+1; consequently ∼K∼B(n)\mathord{\sim} K\mathord{\sim} B(n) is true at sis_i in the ℕ\mathbb{N} model if and only if B(n)B(n) is true at si+1s_{i+1} in the ℕ+\mathbb{N}{+} model. But the function that maps each world sis_i to si+1s_{i+1} is an isomorphism from the ℕ\mathbb{N} model to the ℕ+\mathbb{N}{+} model: it maps the worlds of the former one-one onto the worlds of the latter and preserves the relation of epistemic accessibility. Consequently, for any formula AA, if κA\mathrm{\kappa} A is the result of replacing each formula of the form B(n)B(n) by ∼K∼B(n)\mathord{\sim} K\mathord{\sim} B(n) throughout AA, then κA\mathrm{\kappa} A is true at sis_i in the ℕ\mathbb{N} model if and only if AA is true at si+1s_{i+1} in the ℕ+\mathbb{N}{+} model. In particular, since K(Ki+1B(0)&∼Ki+1B(1))K(K^{i+1}B(0) \mathbin{\&} \mathord{\sim} K^{i+1}B(1)) is true at si+1s_{i+1} in the ℕ+\mathbb{N}{+} model, K(Ki+1∼K∼B(0)&∼Ki+1∼K∼B(1))K(K^{i+1}\mathord{\sim} K\mathord{\sim} B(0) \mathbin{\&} \mathord{\sim} K^{i+1}\mathord{\sim} K\mathord{\sim} B(1)) is true at sis_i in the ℕ\mathbb{N} model. Thus at any world in the ℕ\mathbb{N} model some predicate of the form Km+1∼K∼B(x)K^{m+1}\mathord{\sim} K\mathord{\sim} B(x) has a knowable cut-off point. Presumably Gómez-Torrente would regard such a predicate as soritical.

One can avoid even these knowable cut-off points by adding remote worlds to the ℕ\mathbb{N} model. The 𝕀\mathbb{I} model has as worlds all integers, whether positive or negative. As before, B(n)B(n) is true at sis_i if and only if n<in<i, and sjs_j is accessible from sis_i if and only if |i−j|≤1|i-j|\le 1; thus all atomic sentences are false at all the new worlds. Let σA\mathrm{\sigma} A be the result of replacing each sentence of the form B(n)B(n) by B(n+1)B(n+1) throughout the sentence AA. Since the function which takes each world sis_i to si+1s_{i+1} permutes the worlds of the 𝕀\mathbb{I} model and preserves the structure of the accessibility relation, and, for all nn and ii, B(n+1)B(n+1) is true at si+1s_{i+1} if and only if B(n)B(n) is true at sis_i, it follows that σA\mathrm{\sigma} A is true at si+1s_{i+1} if and only if AA is true at sis_i for any sentence AA. Now consider any predicate φ(x)\mathrm{\varphi}(x) defined by an open formula consisting of atomic sentences of the form B(x+k)B(x+k) and occurrences of KK and the truth-functors; thus φ(n+1)\mathrm{\varphi}(n+1) is σφ(n)\mathrm{\sigma}\mathrm{\varphi}(n). By the preceding observation, φ(n+1)\mathrm{\varphi}(n+1) is true at si+1s_{i+1} if and only if φ(n)\mathrm{\varphi}(n) is true at sis_i. Consequently, φ(n)&∼φ(n+1)\mathrm{\varphi}(n) \mathbin{\&} \mathord{\sim}\mathrm{\varphi}(n+1) is true at sis_i only if it is false at si+1s_{i+1}. Therefore K(φ(n)&∼φ(n+1))K(\mathrm{\varphi}(n) \mathbin{\&} \mathord{\sim}\mathrm{\varphi}(n+1)) is not true at any world. Thus no predicate defined by the means envisaged has a knowable cut-off point in the 𝕀\mathbb{I} model. In particular, that includes predicates of the forms KmB(x)K^mB(x) and Km+1∼K∼B(x)K^{m+1}\mathord{\sim} K\mathord{\sim} B(x). Indeed, since such a predicate φ(x)\mathrm{\varphi}(x) can be extracted from any sentence of the language whatsoever, one might suppose that the result covers all the relevant predicates.

That would be premature, for different predicates can be extracted from the same sentence. For example, from the sentence Km(B(0)⊃B(1))K^m(B(0) \supset B(1)) we can extract both the predicate Km(B(x)⊃B(x+1))K^m(B(x) \supset B(x+1)) and the predicate Km(B(0)⊃B(x))K^m(B(0) \supset B(x)); only the former falls under the observation above. In any model, B(0)⊃B(0)B(0) \supset B(0) is true at every world, so Km(B(0)⊃B(0))K^m(B(0) \supset B(0)) is true at every world. In the 𝕀\mathbb{I} model, B(0)⊃B(1)B(0) \supset B(1) is true at every world except s1s_1, so Km(B(0)⊃B(1))K^m(B(0) \supset B(1)) is true at sis_i if and only if |i−1|>m|i-1|>m. Consequently, for any ii, Km(B(0)⊃B(1))K^m(B(0) \supset B(1)) is false at si−1s_{i-1}, sis_i and si+1s_{i+1} for any sufficiently large mm. But then K(Km(B(0)⊃B(0))&∼Km(B(0)⊃B(1))K(K^m(B(0) \supset B(0)) \mathbin{\&} \mathord{\sim} K^m(B(0) \supset B(1)) is true at sis_i, so 0 is a knowable cut-off point for the predicate Km(B(0)⊃B(x))K^m(B(0) \supset B(x)) there. By an obvious extension of the argument, any natural number nn is a knowable cut-off point for the predicate Km(B(n)⊃B(x))K^m(B(n) \supset B(x)) for sufficiently large mm at any world.

Would Gómez-Torrente call all these predicates ‘soritical’? On reflection, their knowable cut-off points are no threat to epistemicism. As a logical truth, B(n)⊃B(n)B(n) \supset B(n) is epistemically unproblematic for present purposes, and so can be allowed arbitrarily many iterations of knowability. By contrast, B(n)⊃B(n+1)B(n) \supset B(n+1) is an instance of the major premise of a sorites paradox; its epistemic status is therefore problematic at best. For sufficiently large mm, we can know that Km(B(n)⊃B(x))K^m(B(n) \supset B(x)) is true of all and only those values of ‘xx’ no greater than nn. Moreover, this reasoning does not essentially depend on epistemicism; anti-epistemicists can agree with them on the extension of the predicate Km(B(n)⊃B(x))K^m(B(n) \supset B(x)), even though they disagree on the underlying interpretation. At least with respect to this context, the predicate is not vague. That it has the vague constituents KK and BB is not to the point; B(x)⊃B(x)B(x) \supset B(x) is a simpler example of a precise predicate with a vague constituent (see Rolf 1980, pp. 322–24). There are no borderline cases here for epistemicism to explain.

If one assigns B(0)B(0) a status similar to logical truth, the predicate KmB(x)K^mB(x) may be in the same position for sufficiently large mm. We might agree, independently of epistemicism, that it applies to 0 and to no higher number. To oversimplify, ‘is bald’ might mean ‘has close to 0 hairs’, and ‘close to 0’ might apply to 0 in an epistemically unproblematic way in which it applied to no higher number.

In summary, Gómez-Torrente and Graff are right that in almost any epistemicist model a predicate with a knowable cut-off point can be constructed truth-functionally out of vague materials, including KK. But since the argument for that conclusion undermines the claim that the predicate in question has borderline cases with respect to that context, epistemicism is not in trouble.

Significantly, Gómez-Torrente and Graff do not attempt to show that any non-epistemic theory of vagueness fares better with the analogous problem in which KK is replaced by the corresponding non-epistemic operator ‘definitely’ (or ‘determinately’). They hardly could, for that would require a well-developed semantics on which that operator can be iterated without untoward implications for higher-order vagueness. Non-epistemic theorists have notably failed to explain the details of their semantics. Supervaluationism has probably come closest, but its models of vagueness are structurally like those of epistemicism, although interpreted non-epistemically (Williamson 1999). Since the present issues arise from the structure of epistemicist models, they can be raised in analogous form for supervaluationism. Of course, the difference in interpretation might somehow yield a difference in structural constraints, but supervaluationists have as yet said far too little about their accessibility relation between worlds to rely on that. As for approaches other than epistemicism and supervaluationism, such as fuzzy logic, their account of the ‘definitely’ operator is either inconsistent with higher-order vagueness or too poorly worked out to provide a clear answer to the question. There is no good reason to think that those theories could treat the structural issues better than epistemicism has done. The issues have arisen first in relation to epistemicism just because it is the most fully developed theory of vagueness. The value of Gómez-Torrente and Graff’s formal work is not that it facilitates the decision between epistemicism and its rivals but that it provides insight into the fine structure of vagueness for epistemicists and anti-epistemicists alike.
