# Never Say Never

Timothy Williamson

Topoi 13 (2) (1994): 135–145.

Abstract

I. An argument is presented for the conclusion that the hypothesis that no one will ever decide a given proposition is intuitionistically inconsistent. II. A distinction between sentences and statements blocks a similar argument for the stronger conclusion that the hypothesis that I have not yet decided a given proposition is intuitionistically inconsistent, but does not block the original argument. III. A distinction between empirical and mathematical negation might block the original argument, and empirical negation might be modelled on Nelson’s strong negation, but only on intuitionistically unacceptable assumptions. IV. Intuitionists may have to accept the original argument, and therefore be committed to a dubious view of time on which there cannot be merely inductive evidence for statements about the infinite future.

# I. A Problem

Consider any unsolved problem in mathematics: Goldbach’s Conjecture will do. We are strongly inclined to think that, for all we now know, the Conjecture will never be decided. This thought does not require it to be undecidable in principle, or even in practice. It may have a humanly feasible proof or counter-example that will never be discovered, because our species becomes prematurely extinct through nuclear war. Perhaps no other species capable of doing mathematics will happen ever to evolve anywhere in the universe. Such possibilities seem banal, if melancholy. Nevertheless, there are reasons to think that they must be denied by those who generalize intuitionism from mathematical to empirical thought. This paper states those reasons. In particular, it suggests that, so generalized, intuitionism embodies a conception of the infinite future radically at odds with our usual conception of it. On the intuitionistic conception, inductive evidence can support predictions about finite periods of the future, but not unrestricted generalizations over all future times. The reader is left to judge whether the result constitutes a reductio ad absurdum of intuitionism.

How is the intuitionist to acknowledge that Goldbach’s Conjecture may never be decided? There are two problems, one of expression and one of consistency. First, a way must be found to express, in terms that have been given an intuitionistic semantics, the claim that Goldbach’s Conjecture may never be decided. Second, that formula must be shown to be consistent with intuitionistic theory. These problems will be elaborated in turn.

The proposition that Goldbach’s Conjecture may never be decided seems to be constructed by the application of a possibility operator to the proposition that Goldbach’s Conjecture will never be decided. The latter proposition is empirical, not mathematical, in content. It concerns the constructions actually carried out by flesh and blood mathematicians in real time, not those notionally carried out by an idealized mathematician (the creative subject) in ideal time. What is at issue is therefore the extension of the language of intuitionistic mathematics by the addition of terms for empirical concepts. Such an extension raises large questions about the philosophy of intuitionism. On a narrow view, it is a philosophy of mathematics as a human construction of a very special kind, with no implications for the nature of human thought in general. If so, the language of intuitionistic mathematics may be sui generis; we simply step outside it, back into ordinary language as classically conceived, when we want to express an empirical proposition. On this view, there is no obstacle to acknowledging in so many words the empirical possibility that Goldbach’s Conjecture will never be decided. However, Brouwer did not always restrict his intuitionism to mathematics (1975, e.g. p. 488). Dummett (1975), Prawitz (1977) and others have argued systematically that the restricted view is too narrow. They conceive the intuitionistic account of the meanings of mathematical and logical symbols as a special case of a much broader constructivist approach to semantics in general. If they are right, a constructivist account must also be given of the meanings of empirical sentences, such as “Goldbach’s Conjecture will never be decided”. That is the issue discussed in this paper. Can the intuitionistic semantics for mathematical language be extended to a semantics for, in particular, the empirical tense operator “never”?

The problem is not simply a formal one. Formally, in the particular case at issue, the Kripke semantics for intuitionistic logic can indeed be extended to a Kripke semantics for intuitionistic tense logic (Ewald, 1986). But a Kripke semantics gives the meanings intended by the intuitionists only to the extent that it can be interpreted within their informal account of those meanings. That account explains the meaning of a mathematical sentence by the condition for something to count as a proof of it. The obvious difficulty in extending such an account to empirical sentences is that they do not have proofs. However, the notion of a proof may itself be taken as the specialization to mathematics of a more general notion of a warrant for assertion of some canonical kind, a notion applicable to empirical sentences as well as mathematical ones. The meaning of a sentence of either kind might then be explained by the condition for its assertive use to be warranted. The details of such an account remain to be worked out; indeed, it is not clear that they can be worked out. Nevertheless, the schematic generalization will tentatively be used as a background assumption in what follows, whose adequacy will be tested by the more specific discussion to come. The generalization will be assumed to be faithful enough to the original to validate intuitionistic logic; the problem concerns views with a serious right to be considered intuitionistic, not all those that take some vague inspiration from intuitionism.

Suppose that an intuitionistic semantics has been given for the sentence “Goldbach’s Conjecture may never be decided”. The second problem is to show that, so construed, it is intuitionistically consistent.

The consistency problem may be simplified slightly, by the removal of the modal element from the sentence. For the word “may” in this context expresses epistemic possibility; for all we know, Goldbach’s Conjecture will never be decided. Thus if the stronger non-modal claim “Goldbach’s Conjecture will never be decided” were intuitionistically inconsistent, then we could prove it to be inconsistent with what we know, thereby refuting the weaker modal claim “Goldbach’s Conjecture may never be decided”. Conversely, if the weaker claim is intuitionistically inconsistent, then so a fortiori is the stronger one. Thus the problem to be addressed is the intuitionistic consistency of the non-modal claim “Goldbach’s Conjecture will never be decided”. Of course, we have at present no positive reason to assert that Goldbach’s Conjecture will never be decided. For all we know, it will be decided tomorrow. The point is that we also seem to have no warrant to deny that it will never be decided. Thus there seems to be no warrant for a theory inconsistent with the supposition that Goldbach’s Conjecture will never be decided.

Let $KA$ mean that at some past, present or future time someone possesses a warrant to assert $A$ (in spite of the use of the letter “$K$”, it will not be taken for granted that such a warrant implies knowledge). Thus $KA$ expresses an empirical proposition, even when $A$ expresses a mathematical one. Suppose that intuitionistic negation $\neg$ may be applied to empirical as well as mathematical propositions, on some appropriate generalization of its usual semantics. Then, if $p$ is Goldbach’s Conjecture, the sentence “Goldbach’s Conjecture will never be decided” may be formalized by $\neg(Kp \vee K\neg p)$, which is equivalent in intuitionistic logic to $\neg Kp \wedge \neg K\neg p$. Why should there be any doubt as to the consistency of $\neg Kp \wedge \neg K\neg p$ with intuitionistic theory?

The problem is that there seems to be an intuitionistically valid argument from $\neg KA$ to $\neg A$. For consider the conjunction $A \wedge \neg KA$. Could anyone ever possess a warrant to assert that conjunction? If so, $K(A \wedge \neg KA)$ would hold. Then $KA$ would be true, for a warrant to assert a conjunction involves warrants to assert its conjuncts, so the second conjunct would be false, so the conjunction as a whole would be false. Thus one can possess a warrant to assert $A \wedge \neg KA$ only if what one asserts is false. Such a self-defeating warrant is evidently not a genuine warrant at all. Thus no one ever could possess a warrant to assert $A \wedge \neg KA$, even in principle.1 Since a warrant is something that someone could in principle possess, there could not be a warrant to assert $A \wedge \neg KA$. Now, intuitionistically, what proves $\neg B$ is what proves that there could not be a proof of $B$. The obvious generalization of this from mathematical to empirical language is that what warrants the assertion of $\neg B$ is what warrants the assertion that there could not be a warrant for asserting $B$. Now a warrant has just been displayed for the assertion that there could not be a warrant for asserting $A \wedge \neg KA$; intuitionistically, it therefore warrants the assertion of $\neg(A \wedge \neg KA)$. But $\neg(A \wedge \neg KA)$ is intuitionistically equivalent to:

$(1)$
$\neg KA \rightarrow \neg A$

By substitution of $p$ and then of $\neg p$ for $A$ in (1), the conjunction $\neg Kp \wedge \neg K\neg p$ therefore entails $\neg p \wedge \neg\neg p$, a contradiction. The argument seems to have demonstrated the intuitionistic inconsistency of the hypothesis that a given proposition will never be decided.2

The argument is not restricted to the case in which $p$ is a mathematical proposition, e.g. Goldbach’s Conjecture. Propositions about the past may never be decided, or so it seems, and the difficulty arises for them too (Percival, 1990). However, the semantics of the past tense raises a tangle of further difficulties for the intuitionist that cannot be addressed here (Dummett, 1969). For present purposes, $p$ is best taken as a mathematical proposition whose intuitionistic semantics is clear, so that attention can be focussed on the problem raised by the foregoing argument. The considerations to follow should generalize to the other cases.

Intuitively, the point might be put like this. The intuitionist equates truth with the existence of a warrant. Since truth is closed under conjunction, warrants for asserting propositions separately must be combined into a warrant for asserting them together. Since there cannot be a warrant to assert Goldbach’s Conjecture together with the proposition that no one will ever have a warrant to assert Goldbach’s Conjecture, the existence of a warrant for the latter proposition entails the nonexistence of a warrant for the former, which is enough intuitionistically to make its negation true (Rabinowicz, 1985, p. 202).

In classical logic, the decisive conditional $\neg KA \rightarrow \neg A$ is equivalent to $A \rightarrow KA$. Although this form of contraposition is not generally valid in intuitionistic logic, it has been suggested that the intuitionistic semantics for $\rightarrow$ might validate $A \rightarrow KA$ directly (Hart, 1979, p. 165; Wright, 1987, p. 314). What proves $A \rightarrow B$, according to the intuitionist, is an operation that provably transforms proofs of $A$ into proofs of $B$. The obvious generalization of this clause to empirical sentences is that a warrant for $A \rightarrow B$ is an operation that is warranted to transform warrants for $A$ into warrants for $B$. The argument is that if one possesses a warrant to assert $A$, then reflection on one’s possession of it immediately gives one a warrant to assert that someone at some time possesses a warrant to assert $A$, i.e. to assert $KA$; this reflection is warranted to transform warrants for $A$ into warrants for $KA$, and therefore constitutes a warrant for $A \rightarrow KA$. However, this argument neglects any distinction between the existence of a warrant and someone’s possession of it. What has to be transformed into a warrant for $KA$ is a warrant for $A$; what the argument transformed into a warrant for $KA$ was someone’s possession of a warrant for $A$. Intuitionists are not compelled to identify the existence of a warrant with someone’s possession of it. They will certainly regard a warrant as something that someone could possess, but it has not been shown that it exists only in actually being possessed. If the intuitionist makes this distinction, the operations on warrants for $A$ to which the intuitionistic semantics of $\rightarrow$ appeals should not rely on someone’s possession of those warrants. Such a conception can be worked out in some detail, and does not require warrants to be treated platonistically in any intuitionistically unacceptable sense (Williamson, 1988). Thus the intuitionist need not assert $A \rightarrow KA$. However, the argument for $\neg KA \rightarrow \neg A$ remains, for it is not even possible to possess a warrant for $A \wedge \neg KA$.

# II. Sentences and Statements

A striking feature of the argument for $\neg KA \rightarrow \neg A$ is that it seems to go through even if $KA$ is given the unintended reading “I now have a warrant to assert that $A$”. How could anyone ever have a warrant to assert “$A$ and I do not now have a warrant to assert that $A$”? Yet if the intuitionist is forced to conclude “If I do not now have a warrant to assert that $A$, then it is not the case that $A$”, such a solipsism of the present moment surely is a reductio ad absurdum of intuitionism.

The nature of the problem may be clarified with a different example. Consider the sentence “On the next toss this fair coin will come up heads”. We may reasonably assume that there cannot be a warrant for using that sentence to make an assertion; one cannot know in advance how a fair coin will land. If the intuitionistic semantics for $\neg$ is taken at face value, it therefore seems to provide a warrant for using the negation of that sentence to make an assertion. That is absurd. One has no more warrant to assert “On the next toss this fair coin will not come up heads” than to assert “On the next toss this fair coin will come up heads”. For all one knows, in a few minutes one will have a warrant to assert “On the last toss this fair coin came up heads”.

The intuitionistic semantics for $\neg$ can be modified. What a warrant to use the sentence “$\neg A$” assertively must exclude is a warrant to use some sentence or other to assert the statement that is expressed in the present context by the sentence “$A$”. The very statement that is expressed in the present context by the sentence “On the next toss this fair coin will come up heads” will be expressed in a few minutes by the sentence “On the last toss this fair coin came up heads”. A warrant to use the negation of the former sentence to deny the statement must exclude a warrant to use the latter sentence to assert it. This point does not depend on an ontology of statements as language-independent entities. The intuitionist who (unlike Brouwer) has taken the linguistic turn may regard sentences and statements as generated by two contrasting ways of classifying utterances into types. Utterances of “Today is fine” on Monday and Tuesday are tokens of the same sentence, of which an utterance of “Yesterday was fine” on Tuesday is not a token. Utterances of “Today is fine” on Monday and of “Yesterday was fine” on Tuesday are tokens of the same statement, of which an utterance of “Today is fine” on Tuesday is not a token. The intuitionist may then be regarded as giving a semantics of statements rather than sentences. The two kinds of semantics are prolegomena to the same end, the assignment of interpretations to individual utterances. An intuitionistic semantics must therefore include an account of the principles by which utterances are classified together as tokens of the same statement. To provide such an account is a non-trivial task.

The distinction between a semantics of statements and a semantics of sentences should not be pushed too far. A semantics of statements may be equivalent to a semantics in which a warrant to use a sentence in a context is explained in terms of warrants to use simpler sentences in related contexts. However, the simpler sentences will not all be constituents of the given sentence; “On the last toss this coin came up heads” is simpler than, but not a constituent of, “It is not the case that on the next toss this coin will come up heads”. Compositionality holds only for statements; a semantics of statements is therefore more perspicuous.

If the intuitionist can provide a semantics of statements, the argument for the conditional “If I do not now have a warrant to assert that $A$, then it is not the case that $A$” lapses. Someone better informed than I am now could very easily have a warrant now to assert the statement that $A$ and I do not now have a warrant to assert that $A$; I could very easily become better informed and acquire a warrant a few minutes from now to assert the same statement. In neither case is it asserted by use of the sentence “$A$ and I do not now know that $A$”, but that fact is irrelevant to a semantics of statements. Thus the intuitionist avoids the immediate threat of reductio ad absurdum.

A further question arises: can the notion of a statement also be used to block the argument for the crucial conditional $\neg KA \rightarrow \neg A$ on its intended reading, “If no one ever has a warrant to assert that $A$, then it is not the case that $A$”? That would require warrant to assert the statement that $A$ and no one ever has a warrant to assert that $A$ by use of a sentence other than “$A$ and no one ever has a warrant to assert that $A$”, indeed not even synonymous with it. The indexicality of “now” and “I” is not present to be exploited in the construction of such a sentence. Nevertheless, it has been suggested, although not in the context of intuitionism, that non-actual possible situations might play the role played above by future times and other persons (Edgington, 1985; Rabinowicz and Segerberg, 1994). In particular, it may be argued, the statement that is actually expressed by the sentence “$C$” could have been expressed in a non-actual situation by the sentence “If it had been that $B$, it would have been that $C$”, where, from the standpoint of that situation, what would have obtained had “$B$” as used there been true is the actual situation. For example, if “$C$” is “$A$ and no one ever has a warrant to assert that $A$”, the non-actual situation might be one in which someone has a warrant to assert that $A$, but things are otherwise much as they are in the actual situation, and “$B$” might be “I did not acquire this warrant”. On this view, someone could have had a warrant to assert the statement that $A$ and no one ever has a warrant to assert that $A$, by use of the sentence “If I had not acquired this warrant, it would have been that $A$ and no one ever had a warrant to assert that $A$” rather than the sentence “$A$ and no one ever has a warrant to assert that $A$”.

One difficulty for the foregoing proposal is that it is threatened by trivialization. If there is no restriction on the way in which the actual situation is described by “$B$”, the antecedent of the counterfactual, then “$B$” can be a conjunction of which one conjunct is “$C$”, the consequent of the counterfactual, whenever “$C$” as used in the relevant non-actual situation correctly describes the actual situation. In that case the counterfactual becomes a trivial logical truth (Williamson, 1987). This is particularly unfortunate for the intuitionist, since it removes the epistemological constraints on warrants; any true statement automatically acquires a trivial warrant when asserted under the guise of a suitably chosen counterfactual. Thus some restriction is needed on the way in which the actual situation is described by the antecedent of the counterfactual. No adequate restriction has been suggested.3

On the foregoing grounds, the proposal will be assumed to fail. An intuitionistic semantics of statements does not invalidate the conditional $\neg KA \rightarrow \neg A$. The formula $\neg(Kp \vee K\neg p)$ therefore remains intuitionistically inconsistent. If intuitionistic theory is to be consistent with “Goldbach’s Conjecture will never be decided”, as it apparently should be, then that sentence must be formalized in some other way. Section III considers one such attempt.

# III. Constructible Falsity

A distinction is sometimes drawn between mathematical and empirical negation (Dummett, 1977, p. 337). One might therefore introduce a negation operator $\mathord\sim$ with a semantics quite different from that of $\neg$, and designed for empirical statements. If $p$ is an empirical statement, there is still a statement $\neg p$, explained somewhat as above, but it is no longer regarded as the standard negation of $p$. It may also be assumed that there is a statement $\mathord\sim p$ even if $p$ is mathematical, for at worst we can make a special stipulation as to its significance in this case. In the previous formalization of “Goldbach’s Conjecture will never be decided”, $\neg(Kp \vee K\neg p)$, the disjunction $Kp \vee K\neg p$ is empirical, whereas $p$ is mathematical. The proposal would therefore be to replace the outer but not the inner occurrence of $\neg$ by empirical negation, $\mathord\sim$. The new formalization of “Goldbach’s Conjecture will never be decided” is therefore $\mathord\sim(Kp \vee K\neg p)$.

There is a difficulty. If $\mathord\sim(Kp \vee K\neg p)$ is to be intuitionistically consistent when $\neg(Kp \vee K\neg p)$ is not, the latter must not be an intuitionistic consequence of the former; yet there is reason to believe that any alternative negation must be at least as strong as $\neg$. For if $\mathord\sim$ is to count intuitionistically as any sort of negation at all, $\mathord\sim A$ should at least be inconsistent with $A$ in the ordinary intuitionistic sense. A warrant for $A \wedge \mathord\sim A$ should be impossible. That is, we should have $\neg(A \wedge \mathord\sim A)$. By the intuitionistically valid schema $\neg(A \wedge B) \rightarrow (B \rightarrow \neg A)$, this yields:

$(2)$
$\mathord\sim A \rightarrow \neg A$

In particular, from $\mathord\sim(Kp \vee K\neg p)$ one can derive $\neg(Kp \vee K\neg p)$. Since the latter is intuitionistically inconsistent, so is the former. The appeal to empirical negation has achieved nothing.

That is not quite the end of the story. Suppose that we do after all replace the inner as well as the outer occurrence of $\neg$ in $\neg(Kp \vee K\neg p)$ by $\mathord\sim$. From $\mathord\sim(Kp \vee K\mathord\sim p)$ we can use (2) to derive $\neg(Kp \vee K\mathord\sim p)$, which is equivalent to $\neg Kp \wedge \neg K\mathord\sim p$, from which $\neg p \wedge \neg\mathord\sim p$ follows by (1). However, there is no reason to regard the last formula as a contradiction. We seem to have found a way of translating the sentence “Goldbach’s Conjecture will never be decided” into an intuitionistically consistent formula, by using the new negation throughout.

There is an obvious candidate for $\mathord\sim$: strong negation, as introduced by Nelson (1949) through the notion of constructible falsity. It is governed by the result of adding the following axiom schemas to intuitionistic propositional logic (axiomatized with modus ponens as the only rule of inference):4

$(A \wedge \mathord\sim A) \rightarrow B$

$\mathord\sim(A \wedge B) \leftrightarrow (\mathord\sim A \vee \mathord\sim B)$

$\mathord\sim(A \vee B) \leftrightarrow (\mathord\sim A \wedge \mathord\sim B)$

$\mathord\sim(A \rightarrow B) \leftrightarrow (A \wedge \mathord\sim B)$

$\mathord\sim\neg A \leftrightarrow A$

$\mathord\sim\mathord\sim A \leftrightarrow A$

(2) is derivable from the special case $(A \wedge \mathord\sim A) \rightarrow \neg A$ of the first schema.

One can adapt the Kripke semantics for intuitionistic logic to strong negation by adding to the idea of the verification of a formula at a node in the model a separate idea of its falsification there (Thomason, 1969; Rasiowa, 1974, pp. 276–310; van Dalen, 1986, pp. 301–303). Informally, the new semantics might be said to treat assertion and denial as autonomous but related speech acts.5 The condition for denial of a complex statement to have a warrant must be given in terms of warrants for its constituents, in parallel with the condition for its assertion. Formally, we start as usual with a set of nodes $\mathrm{N}$ and a reflexive partial order $\geq$ on $\mathrm{N}$. $\mathrm{\alpha} \vdash A$ means that the node $\mathrm{\alpha}$ verifies (provides a warrant to assert) the formula $A$; $\mathrm{\alpha} \dashv A$ means that $\mathrm{\alpha}$ falsifies (provides a warrant to deny) $A$. If $A$ is atomic, then we cannot have both $\mathrm{\alpha} \vdash A$ and $\mathrm{\alpha} \dashv A$; if in addition $\mathrm{\alpha} \leq \mathrm{\beta}$, then $\mathrm{\alpha} \vdash A$ implies $\mathrm{\beta} \vdash A$ and $\mathrm{\alpha} \dashv A$ implies $\mathrm{\beta} \dashv A$ (monotonicity). Verification is incompatible with falsification; if something is verified, it stays verified under all enlargements of the state of information, and if it is falsified it stays falsified. Verification and falsification are extended to complex formulas as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{lll}
\mathrm{\alpha} \vdash A \wedge B &\text{iff}& \mathrm{\alpha} \vdash A\text{ and }\mathrm{\alpha} \vdash B\\
\mathrm{\alpha} \dashv A \wedge B &\text{iff}& \mathrm{\alpha} \dashv A\text{ or }\mathrm{\alpha} \dashv B\\[3pt]
\mathrm{\alpha} \vdash A \vee B &\text{iff}& \mathrm{\alpha} \vdash A\text{ or }\mathrm{\alpha} \vdash B\\
\mathrm{\alpha} \dashv A \vee B &\text{iff}& \mathrm{\alpha} \dashv A\text{ and }\mathrm{\alpha} \dashv B\\[3pt]
\mathrm{\alpha} \vdash A \rightarrow B &\text{iff}&\text{for }\mathrm{\beta} \geq \mathrm{\alpha}\text{ if }\mathrm{\beta} \vdash A\text{ then }\mathrm{\beta} \vdash B\\
\mathrm{\alpha} \dashv A \rightarrow B &\text{iff}& \mathrm{\alpha} \vdash A\text{ and }\mathrm{\alpha} \dashv B\\[3pt]
\mathrm{\alpha} \vdash \neg A &\text{iff}&\text{for }\mathrm{\beta} \geq \mathrm{\alpha}\text{ not }\mathrm{\beta} \vdash A\\
\mathrm{\alpha} \dashv \neg A &\text{iff}& \mathrm{\alpha} \vdash A\\[3pt]
\mathrm{\alpha} \vdash \mathord\sim A &\text{iff}& \mathrm{\alpha} \dashv A\\
\mathrm{\alpha} \dashv \mathord\sim A &\text{iff}& \mathrm{\alpha} \vdash A
\end{array}
$$

As usual, $A \leftrightarrow B$ is defined as $(A \rightarrow B) \wedge (B \rightarrow A)$. $A$ is valid just in case $\mathrm{\alpha} \vdash A$ for every node $\mathrm{\alpha}$ in every model. The axiom system is sound and complete with respect to this semantics. The deletion of the clauses for $\dashv$ and $\mathord\sim$ gives a Kripke model of the usual sort; conversely, any Kripke model of the usual sort can be extended to a strong negation model by setting $\mathrm{\alpha} \dashv A$ for no node $\mathrm{\alpha}$ and atomic formula $A$ at all. It follows that the axiom system is a conservative extension of intuitionistic propositional logic.

We cannot expect a pair of formal semantic clauses for $K$ to be faithful to its intended meaning, for the latter is informal. However, for present purposes it is enough to consider a class of models in which the behaviour of $K$ can be captured formally, by the following pair of clauses:

$$
\begin{array}{lll}
\mathrm{\alpha} \vdash KA &\text{iff}& \mathrm{\alpha} \vdash A\\
\mathrm{\alpha} \dashv KA &\text{iff}&\text{for }\mathrm{\beta} \geq \mathrm{\alpha}\text{ not }\mathrm{\beta} \vdash A
\end{array}
$$

Call any strong negation model in which these clauses hold a $K$ model. Note that, as required, these clauses satisfy monotonicity, and $\mathrm{\alpha} \vdash KA$ is incompatible with $\mathrm{\alpha} \dashv KA$. They validate the following axiom schemas:

$KA \leftrightarrow A$

$\mathord\sim KA \leftrightarrow \neg A$

(1) is derivable from the first schema. It is not claimed that these schemas are correct on the intended meaning of $K$; an objection to the former of them was lodged in section I. The point is rather that the proposed formalization of “Goldbach’s Conjecture will never be decided” can be shown to be consistent even in the presence of these very strong assumptions.

Consider a $K$ model with just one node, $\mathrm{\alpha}$. Let no atomic formula be either verified or falsified at $\mathrm{\alpha}$. The proposed formalization, $\mathord\sim(Kp \vee K\mathord\sim p)$, can be shown to be verified at $\mathrm{\alpha}$ by the following argument:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{neither }\mathrm{\alpha} \dashv p\text{ nor }\mathrm{\alpha} \vdash p\\
\therefore\quad&\text{neither }\mathrm{\alpha} \dashv p\text{ nor }\mathrm{\alpha} \dashv \mathord\sim p\\
\therefore\quad&\text{for }\mathrm{\beta} \geq \mathrm{\alpha}\text{ neither }\mathrm{\beta} \dashv p\text{ nor }\mathrm{\beta} \dashv \mathord\sim p\\
\therefore\quad&\mathrm{\alpha} \dashv Kp\text{ and }\mathrm{\alpha} \dashv K\mathord\sim p\\
\therefore\quad&\mathrm{\alpha} \dashv Kp \vee K\mathord\sim p\\
\therefore\quad&\mathrm{\alpha} \vdash \mathord\sim(Kp \vee K\mathord\sim p)
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus $\mathord\sim(Kp \vee K\mathord\sim p)$ is consistent with the overly strong assumptions listed above, and a fortiori with more moderate ones.

The critical feature of $K$ models is that although verification of $A$ is equivalent to verification of $KA$, falsification of $A$ is not equivalent to falsification of $KA$. In the model above, for example, we have $\mathrm{\alpha} \dashv Kp$ but not $\mathrm{\alpha} \dashv p$. If both equivalences held, $K$ would be a redundant operator and $\mathord\sim(Kp \vee K\mathord\sim p)$ would collapse into $\mathord\sim(p \vee \mathord\sim p)$, which is equivalent to the contradiction $\mathord\sim p \wedge p$. The feature that prevents this collapse has an intuitive basis. The distinction between a warrant to assert $A$ and a warrant to assert that $A$ will be proved is a very subtle one. In contrast, the distinction between a warrant to deny $A$ and a warrant to deny that $A$ will be proved looks rather blatant. A collapse of the former distinction would not induce a collapse of the latter. If we knew that sentient life was about to be extinguished, we should have an empirical warrant to assert that various propositions would never be proved, because the requisite inquiries would never be completed, but that would not amount to a warrant to deny those propositions. The clause for $\mathrm{\alpha} \dashv KA$ in $K$ models reflects this point by being as liberal as it can be, given the twin constraints of monotonicity and incompatibility with the clause for $\mathrm{\alpha} \vdash KA$.

Since $\mathrm{\alpha} \vdash Kp$ is equivalent to $\mathrm{\alpha} \vdash p$, $Kp \leftrightarrow p$ is a theorem of the logic characteristic of $K$ models. Since $\mathrm{\alpha} \dashv Kp$ is not equivalent to $\mathrm{\alpha} \dashv p$, $\mathord\sim Kp \leftrightarrow \mathord\sim p$ is not a theorem of that logic. Thus the theoremhood of $A \leftrightarrow B$ does not in general guarantee the theoremhood of $\Sigma(A) \leftrightarrow \Sigma(B)$. This feature does not depend on the presence of $K$; for example, $\mathord\sim\neg p \leftrightarrow p$ is a theorem but $\mathord\sim\mathord\sim\neg p \leftrightarrow \mathord\sim p$ is not. The theoremhood of $A \leftrightarrow B$ requires only that $A$ and $B$ be verified at the same nodes; the theoremhood of $\Sigma(A) \leftrightarrow \Sigma(B)$ may require them to be falsified at the same nodes too. One can define a stronger conditional than $\rightarrow$ by equating $A \Rightarrow B$ with $(A \rightarrow B) \wedge (\mathord\sim B \rightarrow \mathord\sim A)$. If $A \Leftrightarrow B$ is defined as $(A \Rightarrow B) \wedge (B \Rightarrow A)$, then its theoremhood requires $A$ and $B$ to be both verified at the same nodes and falsified at the same nodes; it is therefore sufficient for the theoremhood of $\Sigma(A) \Leftrightarrow \Sigma(B)$.

The use of strong negation may point the way towards a constructivist account of statements such as “Goldbach’s Conjecture will never be decided”. What is more doubtful is that this account is available to intuitionists. It involves the replacement of $Kp \vee K\neg p$ by $Kp \vee K\mathord\sim p$ as a formalization of “Goldbach’s Conjecture will one day be decided”. Thus $K\neg p$ is treated as insufficient for a negative solution to the problem, because the stronger $K\mathord\sim p$ is treated as necessary, even though $p$ is mathematical rather than empirical.6 This seems tantamount to treating $\neg$ as not a genuine negation at all. The concomitant view of $\neg p$ is one that a classical logician might take of it, as expressing the provable unprovability of Goldbach’s Conjecture but not its falsity. Only $\mathord\sim p$ is viewed as expressing the latter. Although the resulting formal system is a conservative extension of intuitionistic logic, it is interpreted from a non-intuitionistic standpoint. Similarly, the classical modal logic S4 is a conservative extension of intuitionistic logic under a suitable mapping of formulas, but that does not make S4, as usually interpreted, available to the intuitionist. It may therefore be tentatively concluded that the strong negation account of sentences such as “Goldbach’s Conjecture will never be decided” is not available to intuitionists.

The objection generalizes from strong negation to anything recognizable as a negation operator within intuitionistic logic. Any such operator will satisfy (2), and will therefore need to replace $\neg$ within the scope of $K$ in the formalization of “Goldbach’s Conjecture will one day be decided”. As before, $K\neg p$ will be treated as insufficient for a negative solution to the problem, and therefore $\neg$ as not a genuine negation; the account will not be available to intuitionists. On its intended interpretation, intuitionistic logic leaves no room for a nonstandard negation to solve the problem.7

# IV. The Future

In the light of section III, let us revert to the intuitionistic formalization of “Goldbach’s Conjecture will never be decided” as $\neg(Kp \vee K\neg p)$. In the light of sections I and II, let us assume that this formula is indeed intuitionistically inconsistent. Thus the intuitionist denies that Goldbach’s Conjecture will never be decided. Is this denial well-motivated?

Intuitionistically, the denial that Goldbach’s Conjecture will never be decided would not commit us to the assertion that Goldbach’s Conjecture will one day be decided. Since we have no method for deciding the Conjecture, we have no intuitionistic warrant for the latter assertion. Thus the intuitionist can now assert “No one now possesses a warrant to assert that Goldbach’s Conjecture will one day be decided”. If we let $K_nA$ mean that someone now possesses a warrant to assert the statement that $A$, then the quoted assertion might be formalized by $\neg K_n(Kp \vee K\neg p)$. It is critical that $\neg$ should not commute with $K_n$. $\neg K_n(Kp \vee K\neg p)$ must not imply $K_n\neg(Kp \vee K\neg p)$, for the intuitionistic falsity of the latter formula follows from the assumed intuitionistic inconsistency of $\neg(Kp \vee K\neg p)$.

The operator $K_n$ can be given a crude semantics on Kripke trees by the condition that $\mathrm{\alpha} \vdash K_nA$ if and only if $\mathrm{\alpha}_0 \vdash A$, where $\mathrm{\alpha}_0$ is the bottom node of the tree. Thus the schema $K_nA \rightarrow A$ is valid (by monotonicity) but the schema $A \rightarrow K_nA$ is not. Also, $\mathrm{\alpha} \vdash K_n\neg A$ if and only if there is no node $\mathrm{\beta}$ such that $\mathrm{\beta} \vdash A$; $\mathrm{\alpha} \vdash \neg K_nA$ if and only if it is not the case that $\mathrm{\alpha}_0 \vdash A$. It follows from the latter clause that present warrant is decidable, in the sense that $K_nA \vee \neg K_nA$ is valid.8 Moreover, the schema $K_n\neg A \rightarrow \neg K_nA$ is valid but the schema $\neg K_nA \rightarrow K_n\neg A$ is not. For example, if $p$ is verified at some nodes on a tree but not at others, then both $\neg K_n(p \vee \neg p)$ and $\neg K_n\neg(p \vee \neg p)$ are verified everywhere on the tree. If we make the simplifying assumption that $KA$ is verified at exactly the same points on the tree as $A$, then $\neg K_n(Kp \vee K\neg p)$ and $\neg K_n\neg(Kp \vee K\neg p)$ are verified together, as required. In this model, the warrant to assert that no one now possesses a warrant to assert that Goldbach’s Conjecture will one day be decided simply reflects the warrant to assert that no one now possesses a warrant to assert that Goldbach’s Conjecture is decidable.

Nevertheless, the assertibility of “No one now possesses a warrant to assert that Goldbach’s Conjecture will one day be decided” does not completely solve the problem, for it does nothing to explain the validity of the puzzling schema (1), $\neg KA \rightarrow \neg A$. How can the falsity of “Someone will one day prove Goldbach’s Conjecture” possibly be sufficient for the falsity of Goldbach’s Conjecture itself?

Clearly, no amount of evidence about the imminent extinction of life by nuclear war can warrant the denial of Goldbach’s Conjecture. Such evidence is simply irrelevant to the mathematical question. It does not even make the negation of Goldbach’s Conjecture probable. Now, given the a priori intuitionistic argument for (1), if such evidence made “No one will ever prove Goldbach’s Conjecture” probable, it would presumably make the negation of Goldbach’s Conjecture equally probable. If (1) is a priori, then it is a priori that the statement that no one will ever prove Goldbach’s Conjecture is equivalent to its conjunction with the negation of the Conjecture. Thus the view embodied in (1) (as the conclusion of an a priori argument) must be that evidence about (e.g.) the imminent extinction of life does not even make it probable that no one will ever prove Goldbach’s Conjecture. Is that not simply absurd?

Somehow, the intuitionist must deny that the statement that no one will ever prove Goldbach’s Conjecture can merely happen to be true, and insist that the statement is true only if made so by a disproof of the Conjecture. An initial thought is that such an insistence would have to derive from the view that future tense statements are true only if their truth is logically guaranteed by present facts. The only present fact (on an informal understanding of the phrase) that could logically guarantee the truth of the statement that no one will ever prove Goldbach’s Conjecture is the unprovability of the Conjecture. If it is unprovable, then by intuitionistic standards it certainly follows that it is false.

The view that future tense statements are true only if their truth is logically guaranteed by present facts goes naturally with the conception of the future as the realm of possibility. On this conception, there is such a thing as what will happen only to the extent that it is determined by the present; otherwise there is only what might happen. The present form of the conception is particularly extreme, for since the view requires present facts to guarantee the truth of future tense statements logically rather than causally, the future is conceived as the realm of logical rather than causal possibility. If a causal guarantee were enough, the truth of the statement that no one will ever prove the Conjecture might be guaranteed by all kinds of physical condition inconsistent with the provability of the Conjecture.

The appeal to the “present facts” view of future tense statements does not withstand further scrutiny. If it worked at all, it would work just as well for the future tense statement “No one will prove Goldbach’s Conjecture tomorrow”. The only present fact that could logically guarantee that no one will prove Goldbach’s Conjecture tomorrow is the unprovability of the Conjecture. The intuitionist does not want to endorse the validity of an argument from “No one will prove Goldbach’s Conjecture tomorrow” to the negation of the Conjecture. Indeed, the considerations of section II suggest that the very notion of a future tense statement is confused. The sentence “Tomorrow will be fine” is in the future tense, but the statement that it expresses will be expressed tomorrow by the present tense sentence “Today is fine”. Moreover, the absence of present facts that logically guarantee the truth of the statement that tomorrow will be fine in no way entitles one to deny that statement.

The intuitionist seems to need a rather different line of thought: that a statement is true only if its truth can be known at some time, and that one cannot know the future. This line of thought allows an empirical statement about a finite period of the future to be true, for it can be known once that period is past. In contrast, an empirical universal generalization over all future times concerns a period that will never be wholly past; such a generalization cannot be known in that way. Indeed, the point needs to be a priori, for the argument from “No one will ever prove Goldbach’s Conjecture” to the negation of the Conjecture is an a priori one. On this view, we can know that no one will ever prove Goldbach’s Conjecture only by having a proof of its unprovability, which amounts to a proof of its intuitionistic falsity.

For the intuitionist, the future is the realm of the possible, not because any finite stretch of it is a mere potentiality, but because its infinity is a potential infinity.9 Any finite stretch of the future will eventually be past; the whole infinite future never will be. There is not even any such thing as a definite course that the infinite future might take, for one could never be in a position to assert that it would take that course. There are only definite courses that it might take over finite periods. A universal generalization about all future times cannot merely happen to be true. For any finite period of future time, the supposition that Goldbach’s Conjecture will not be proved within that period has no mathematical implications, but the supposition that it will not be proved within an infinite period entails its falsity.

The intuitionistic conception of the future is embodied in the principle of open data for lawless sequences (Kreisel and Troelstra, 1970). If a lawless sequence $\mathrm{\xi}$ has a property $\mathrm{\phi}$, then there is a (finite) initial segment $x$ of $\mathrm{\xi}$ such that every lawless sequence with $x$ as an initial segment has $\mathrm{\phi}$. At any time, mathematical knowledge of $\mathrm{\xi}$ is confined to an initial segment of $\mathrm{\xi}$, so such a segment must be sufficient for a proof that the sequence has $\mathrm{\phi}$. For example, although for any natural number $n$ the denial that 0 occurs in the first $n$ places of $\mathrm{\xi}$ is consistent, the denial that 0 occurs at all in $\mathrm{\xi}$ is inconsistent, for it cannot be warranted by any initial segment of $\mathrm{\xi}$. We cannot assert that 0 occurs in $\mathrm{\xi}$, but we can assert that it is not the case that it is not the case that 0 occurs in $\mathrm{\xi}$. Similarly, for any natural number $n$ the denial that a code for a disproof of Goldbach’s Conjecture occurs in the first $n$ places of $\mathrm{\xi}$ is consistent with the Conjecture, but the denial that such a code occurs at all in $\mathrm{\xi}$ is inconsistent with the Conjecture, for it cannot be warranted by any initial segment of $\mathrm{\xi}$ in the absence of an intuitionistic disproof of the Conjecture.

Lawless sequences are usually conceived as occurring in the notional time of an ideal mathematician, not the historical time of real human mathematicians. Statements about them are supposed to be mathematical, not empirical. On the intuitionistic view just sketched, the distinction between the mathematical and the empirical begins to dissolve in the face of universal generalizations over all times. It does so because the use of inductive evidence has not been written into the semantics of such generalizations, which provides no shortcut to an analytic justification of induction. The content of the generalizations is fixed by the condition for knowledge of their truth, and possession of inductive evidence is conceived as falling short of knowledge. If we have inductive evidence, it is the condition for such knowledge that determines what it is that we have evidence for. In this sense, knowledge is privileged with respect to other cognitive states. Its privileged status combines with a specific conception of knowledge of infinite domains to give the envisaged view.10

The envisaged view makes no provision for truths to which our only conceivable access is through inductive generalization over all times. Causal laws seem to be just such truths. According to the view, the inductive evidence may confirm the generalization as restricted to any finite period, however long, for the possibility of knowing the truth of the restriction once that period is past gives it content. The unrestricted generalization cannot gain its content in this way. Its truth must be logically determined in advance. Since inductive evidence is not evidence for truth of that kind, it does not warrant the assertion of unrestricted generalizations over all times; it does not even constitute evidence for them. To misquote Wittgenstein, an infinite period is not like a finite period, only longer.

Consider Dummett’s example of an unrestricted generalization over all times, “A city will never be built on this spot” (1978, pp. 16–17). On the envisaged view, its assertion cannot be warranted inductively, and no other kind of warrant is in prospect. Its negation is therefore intuitionistically true: it is not the case that a city will never be built on this spot. Of course, it does not follow intuitionistically that a city will at some time be built on this spot, for we have no way of specifying such a time.

Dummett (1969) has emphasized the disturbing conception of the past embodied in anti-realist views modelled on mathematical intuitionism.11 The conception of the future just sketched is equally disturbing. The aim of this paper has been to suggest that it may nevertheless be forced on the intuitionist (perhaps not on all anti-realists). If so, the reader must judge whether it constitutes a reductio ad absurdum of intuitionism.

[1] The argument in the text draws on the argument in Fitch (1963) that if all truths are knowable then all truths are known, an argument discussed in the context of intuitionistic logic in Hart (1979); Williamson (1982, 1988, 1992); Wright (1987), pp. 312–316; Percival (1990). Melia (1991) claims that the argument raises no problem for anti-realism because, although $A \wedge \neg KA$ cannot be verified, it can be falsified (when $A$ is false); this is simply irrelevant to intuitionistic forms of anti-realism, for they claim that all true propositions can be verified, not that all propositions can be either verified or falsified.

[2] Unlike Fitch’s original argument, the argument in the text does not rely on the schema $KA \rightarrow A$, from which $K(A \wedge \neg KA) \rightarrow \neg KA$ would follow, combining with $K(A \wedge \neg KA) \rightarrow KA$ to give $\neg K(A \wedge \neg KA)$; $K(A \wedge \neg KA)$ is absurd even without this mechanical reductio ad absurdum. For all the argument for $\neg p \wedge \neg\neg p$ assumes, a warrant to assert $\neg KA$ might consist of inductive evidence deductively consistent with $KA$. [3] Another difficulty for the proposal is that it requires statements to be individuated unnaturally (see Percival, 1991). The difference affects the truth-value of the statement expressed, not its identity. By treating situations on the model of times, the proposal obliterates this natural distinction.
