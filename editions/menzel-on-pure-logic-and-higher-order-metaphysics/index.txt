# Menzel on pure logic and higher-order metaphysics

Timothy Williamson

In Peter Fritz and Nicholas K. Jones (eds.), Higher-Order Metaphysics (2024), 460–471. Oxford: Oxford University Press.

In ‘Pure Logic and Higher-Order Metaphysics’ (this volume), Christopher Menzel presents and defends a type-free theory of structured propositions and of properties, based on first-order logic. He contrasts it favourably with the higher-order intensional approach I take in Modal Logic as Metaphysics (Williamson 2013). In this chapter, I will discuss some distinctive aspects of Menzel’s account, and explain problems for them, problems that do not arise for the higher-order intensional approach.

# 1. Pure Logic

From his title on, Menzel emphasizes a distinction between ‘pure logic’ and other kinds of abstract theorizing. His initial characterization is that pure logic ‘should be universal and topic neutral’ (§1.1; such references are to his chapter sections). However, the purity of pure logic turns out to be a vague matter of degree. Universality is the idea that ‘the general patterns of representation and reasoning that a logic is capable of expressing should be applicable to most any conceivable domain’ (§1.1). Menzel finds topic neutrality more elusive, but suggests glossing it as the constraint that ‘a logic shouldn’t of itself entail the existence of any particular things or particular properties; it should be indifferent to the particular identities and natures of things’, though he admits that this needs ‘a bit of qualification’ (§1.1, his italics).

Menzel’s account of ‘pure logic’ is no mere stipulation as to what he will mean by the word ‘logic’. It does methodological work for him, because he treats revision of pure logic as more disruptive and costly than revision of other theories. This is reminiscent of Quine’s attitude that we should keep revision as close to the periphery of the web of belief as we can (Quine 1951, 1970). For example, in Menzel’s assessment of the Prior-Kaplan paradox, he treats it as an advantage of his first-order account that he can treat it as a local problem, ‘specifically a paradox of truth […] as opposed to a paradox of pure logic’ (§3.1, his italics); by contrast, for me the paradox relies only on pure logic, as he notes, citing my brief discussion of it (Williamson 2016b). However, he omits my tentative conclusion there: that the paradox requires no revision of logic at all, but instead a deeper understanding of the non-transparency of our attitudes to externalist contents. I will return to that issue in the second part of these comments.

The ‘bit of qualification’ Menzel gives to his provisional gloss on topic neutrality comes in his discussion of identity. He reasonably decides that, even though the logic of identity is committed to a particular relation, identity, excluding it from pure logic would be too Draconian. Rather, he suggests, the commitments of pure logic ‘should not extend beyond those needed to think and reason within any conceivable domain’ (§1.5, his italics), which is in line with the methodological point that revising pure logic has global repercussions. Since he judges that commitment to identity passes the test of being needed to think and reason within any conceivable domain, he decides that pure logic should include the logic of identity.

Menzel takes the opposite attitude to the exemplification relation between things and properties (where properties count as things too). He judges that exemplification’s ‘properties are largely irrelevant to most areas of inquiry’ and so, by the same criterion, excludes the theory of exemplification from pure logic (§1.4). Consequently, on his view, the revision of the unrestricted comprehension principle needed to avoid the property version of Russell’s paradox counts as a revision to the local theory of property exemplification rather than to global pure logic, and so is much less disturbing.

Can identity and exemplification be kept so easily apart? In Menzel’s setting of first-order property theory, here is a natural formulation of Leibniz’s Law, the indiscernibility of identicals:

(LL)
For any things x and y and property p, if x = y then x exemplifies p if and only if y exemplifies p.

But if pure logic includes the theory of identity, which includes LL, won’t pure logic also need to include the theory of exemplification, to handle its role in LL? Thus Menzel’s differential treatment of the theory of identity and the theory of exemplification threatens to be unstable.

Formally, Menzel can confine ‘pure logic’ to standard first-order logic with identity, which has no logical constant for exemplification and replaces the universal generalization LL with a schema LL^{∗} of the usual sort, which does not mention properties; roughly:

(LL^{∗})
If x = y then φ(x) if and only if φ(y).

(I omit technical details.) However, that is less than satisfying. From the standpoint of first-order property theory, LL provides the unifying rationale for LL*’s infinitely many instances. After all, LL* is meant to be open-ended. The lack of counterexamples to it is no mere artefact of the accidentally limited expressive power of the language. As we add new atomic predicates to the language, we automatically add corresponding instances of LL*. LL is part of the rationale for doing so, since the new predicates are meant to express properties. If someone wanted to add a new predicate to be read ‘is so-called because of his size’ and then use LL* to argue that Giorgione ≠ Barbarelli, we could respond that no property has been specified for the predicate to express (the example is from Quine 1953). Of course, Quine himself would object to LL, because he regards properties as creatures of darkness. But Menzel posits properties and presumably accepts LL; he just wants to keep it out of pure logic, which will contain only LL*.

Obviously, in most domains of inquiry, non-logicians do not focus on principles like LL. But they also do not focus on principles like LL*. They just reason about cases of identity, more or less in conformity with both LL and LL*. More generally, explicit logical principles rarely figure in most domains of inquiry. They make patterns of reasoning explicit which are normally left implicit. LL takes that a step further than LL*. From this perspective, Menzel’s claim that the properties of exemplification ‘are largely irrelevant to most areas of inquiry’ looks quite dubious. Even though they are rarely the focus of inquiry, they are still relevant because they underpin patterns of reasoning employed in most areas of inquiry. Thus Menzel’s grounds for excluding the theory of exemplification from pure logic are unconvincing.

As a corollary, Menzel may be premature in his satisfaction at keeping pure logic untouched by the revisions required by various paradoxes he considers. Revisions to the theory of exemplification may be revisions to pure logic. In that respect, the advantages he claims over higher-order approaches may well be illusory. Indeed, his position is worse than that. For the restrictions on his comprehension principle for properties (§1.4) might compromise the use of LL to rationalize LL*, when no property p is exemplified by all and only those things which satisfy the predicate substituted for the schematic letter ‘φ’, though perhaps LL* could still be derived from LL in some less straightforward way—for example, by induction on the complexity of the predicate, if his property comprehension principle holds at least for the atomic predicates (but why should it?). By contrast, the higher-order approach supports an unrestricted second-order comprehension principle in place of Menzel’s restricted first-order comprehension principle for properties, which enables all instances of LL* to be smoothly derived from a second-order analogue of LL in a uniform way. Thus the higher-order perspective affords a more perspicuous view of the logic of identity.

# 2. Hyperintensionality

A second distinctive feature of Menzel’s approach is its hyperintensionality: he denies that strictly equivalent propositions and properties are always identical. For example, while agreeing that, necessarily, whatever exemplifies triangularity exemplifies trilaterality and vice versa, Menzel denies that triangularity is trilaterality: ‘they are intuitively distinct, as one concerns the number of angles of a geometric figure and the other the number of sides’ (§1.3). Perhaps in support of this, he adds that (13) ‘doesn’t seem to follow’ from (12) (his numbering):

(12)
Chelsea is pondering triangularity.
(13)
Chelsea is pondering trilaterality.

Of course, if one already regards triangularity and trilaterality as distinct, one will naturally take (13) not to follow from (12). But if one is still wondering whether ‘triangularity’ and ‘trilaterality’ refer to the same property, one should not allow the apparent gap between (12) and (13) to settle the issue. After all, to many people, (13!) doesn’t seem to follow from (12!) and the astronomical facts (which Chelsea may not know):

(12!)
Chelsea is pondering Hesperus.
(13!)
Chelsea is pondering Phosphorus.

But, for all that, Hesperus is Phosphorus. As for Menzel’s first argument, that triangularity concerns the number of angles while trilaterality does not, we should wonder what is meant by saying that a property ‘concerns’ something. ‘Concerning’ sounds suspiciously like a semantic relation of aboutness. But properties are normally assumed to be worldly items; like planets and unlike concepts, they are not about anything. We do not want to fall into a trap analogous to arguing that since Hesperus concerns the evening while Phosphorus does not, Hesperus is not Phosphorus. Thus Menzel’s opening case for hyperintensionalism should not satisfy the antecedent doubts we might have on the matter.

Admittedly, the terms ‘triangular’ and ‘trilateral’ are not always interchangeable in explanatory contexts without loss. But that is only to be expected, because explanations should be as perspicuous as possible. Substituting co-referential terms can make an explanation harder or easier to follow, and so yield explanatory loss or gain. If we have been talking about angles, the transition to using the word ‘triangular’ may be cognitively smoother than the transition to using the word ‘trilateral’, just as, if we have been talking about the evening, the transition to using the name ‘Hesperus’ may be cognitively smoother than the transition to using the name ‘Phosphorus’. In both cases, to infer a difference in reference incurs the suspicion of projecting the structure of our cognition onto the structure of the world.

To argue for the hyperintensionality of propositions, Menzel takes the example of the proposition that alligators don’t exist and the proposition that, if alligators exist, there is a largest prime, which are necessarily equivalent in truth-value (§1.6). He then asserts that ‘intuitively’, from (22) ‘we cannot reasonably infer’ (23):

(22)
Sasha believes that alligators don’t exist.
(23)
Sasha believes that, if alligators exist, there is a largest prime.

Thus, Menzel concludes, the proposition that alligators don’t exist is distinct from the proposition that, if alligators exist, there is a largest prime, because Sasha can believe the former without believing the latter.

We should be wary of that argument too. Consider the similar argument that ‘intuitively’, from (22!) and the astronomical facts (which Sasha may not know), we cannot reasonably infer (23!):

(22!)
Sasha believes that Hesperus is bright.
(23!)
Sasha believes that Phosphorus is bright.

Thus, the argument concludes, the proposition that Hesperus is bright is distinct from the proposition that Phosphorus is bright, because Sasha can believe the former without believing the latter. As Kripke has forcefully explained, the conclusion is not safe, because our practices of belief ascription are not fully consistent; they can lead us into inconsistency through over-sensitivity to the subject’s linguistic utterances (Kripke 1979). This can happen even in cases of generally accepted synonymy, as with the words ‘furze’ and ‘gorse’, which are simply two words for the same shrub, and are associated with the same non-metalinguistic descriptions. Nevertheless, if we are not sure whether Sasha is aware of the co-reference, from (22!!) and our awareness of the linguistic facts, we may feel that we cannot reasonably infer (23!!):

(22!!)
Sasha believes that furze has yellow flowers.
(23!!)
Sasha believes that gorse has yellow flowers.

Thus, the analogous argument concludes, the proposition that furze has yellow flowers is distinct from the proposition that gorse has yellow flowers, because Sasha can believe the former without believing the latter. But the conclusion is hopeless: the proposition that furze has yellow flowers is the proposition that gorse has yellow flowers, for we use the words ‘furze’ and ‘gorse’ synonymously, we use the sentences ‘Furze has yellow flowers’ and ‘Gorse has yellow flowers’ to express the same proposition, and we are the people discussing whether the proposition that furze has yellow flowers is the proposition that gorse has yellow flowers. Elsewhere, I have discussed such cases in much greater detail, and shown how the potentially inconsistent rules for belief ascription identified by Kripke can be understood as heuristics, quick and easy ways of meeting cognitive challenges which usually but not always give the correct answer (Williamson 2021b). We rely on heuristics in many different areas of cognition. On the emerging picture, the truth-conditions of propositional attitude ascriptions are much less transparent to us than they seem, and we are much less reliable in recognizing whether they obtain than we assume ourselves to be. The convoluted and inconclusive literature published over the past seventy years on the semantics of attitude ascriptions is a manifestation of the difficulties.

In the case of Menzel’s example, Sasha may of course assent to the sentence ‘Alligators don’t exist’ while too uncertain to assent to ‘If alligators exist, there is a largest prime’, but if so it does not follow that (22) is true and (23) false. Similarly, if Sasha assents to ‘Hesperus is bright’ but not to ‘Phosphorus is bright’, it does not follow that (22!) is true and (23!) false, and if Sasha assents to ‘Furze has yellow flowers’ but not to ‘Gorse has yellow flowers’, it does not follow that (22!!) is true and (23!!) false. Nothing was stipulated as to how much English (if any) Sasha knows. Of course, with the pair (22) and (23), unlike the pairs (22!) and (23!) and (22!!) and (23!!), the two embedded sentences also differ in structure and subject matter. But just what those differences imply for belief is not obvious, given the lack of consensus in the literature. We may end up having to say that Sasha believes a proposition p under the guise of the sentence ‘Alligators don’t exist’ but does not believe p under the guise of the sentence ‘If alligators exist, there is a largest prime’, even though both sentences express p. A cautious moral is that, in the present state of understanding of the semantics and heuristics of propositional attitude ascriptions, our ‘intuitive’ judgements about them are not a safe guide to the individuation of propositions. In particular, they are not decisive against a coarse-grained intensional account of propositions like that of Stalnaker (1984, 1999; see also Williamson 2021a,b, 2022).

None of this by itself constitutes an argument against hyperintensionalism about propositions and properties. It just shows that the case for hyperintensionalism is much weaker than its proponents assume. ‘Intuitively’ appealing examples are not enough. The issue will eventually have to be determined on more theoretically informed grounds. In what follows, I will make some comments towards an assessment of Menzel’s specific hyperintensional theory of propositions in its own right, putting aside his inconclusive case for hyperintensionalism in general.

According to Menzel, ‘propositions exhibit a fine-grained structure similar to (but by no means isomorphic to) the grammatical structure of the sentences that express them’ (§1.6). For some, this remark will set off alarm bells: it sounds as though the structure of language is being projected onto the structure of reality. In Modal Logic as Metaphysics, I summarily wrote: ‘Hyperintensionality arises at the level of thought and linguistic meaning, and should be explained at that level, not at the level of anything like a general theory of properties and relations’ (2013: 266). Menzel responds:

contrary to what Williamson appears to imply, ontological commitments that arise “at the level of thought and linguistic meaning” are entirely pertinent to the logic of metaphysical modality. On a realist view of thought and meaning, there are the things thought and meant. [§1.4, his italics]

Neither in the passage to which Menzel objects nor now do I disagree with the realist claim that there are the things thought and meant (though I prefer to give it a higher-order inflection). If some mental or semantic phenomenon is a counterexample to a putative law of logic, then of course that law is false. My point was quite different: although mental or linguistic representations (such as predicates) can be necessarily co-extensive yet cognitively different, we should not project that feature onto the properties or relations to which they refer.

To illustrate the point for properties, we should first note that Menzel sketches the familiar treatment of sentences and propositions as a special case of n-place predicates and n-place properties respectively, for n = 0. Thus, having introduced notation for nominalizing complex sentences and so referring to propositions in his formal object language, he writes ‘The framework developed here generalizes quite naturally to one that contains complex n-place predicates’ (footnote 24), by introducing notation for nominalizing complex predicates and so referring to properties in the object language. He later supplies some of the logical details, treating the propositional case as that for n = 0 (footnote 27). We have already seen how he carefully argues in parallel for the hyperintensional individuation of properties and the hyperintensional individuation of propositions.

If we generalize Mendel’s hyperintensional approach to the individuation of propositions to properties in the natural way, we obtain the result that being both square and square is a distinct property from being either square or square, because conjunction and disjunction are distinct operations on properties—because, in effect, being both square and square is treated as a conjunctive property, not a disjunctive one, while being either square or square is treated as a disjunctive property, not a conjunctive one. Such results might be acceptable in a theory of Fregean senses, modes of presentation of their referents. But that is not what Menzel is offering, as can be seen from his claim that the proposition that Bernie Sanders is a Democrat is determined by the property of being a Democrat and Bernie Sanders himself—not by a sense which presents Bernie Sanders (§2.2). In that respect, Menzel’s propositions and properties are worldly; his theory abstracts away from differences at the level of modes of presentation. Yet in distinguishing being both green and green or being either green or green from simply being green, his theory refuses to abstract away from other differences which to many philosophers (including me) seem blatantly to belong at the level of modes of presentation: redundant applications of a logical operator. Imagine a medical scientist discovering that what really causes lung cancer is not smoking but instead either smoking or smoking. Numerous similar examples would make the same point. By contrast, natural science robustly investigates modal distinctions: in their intended applications, dynamical systems and other mathematical structures based on phase spaces are interpreted as modelling the physically possible states of a physical system (Williamson 2016a). In short, Menzel’s rules for individuating propositions and properties have no clear unified motivation.

The implausibility of Menzel’s account is somewhat disguised in his paper because he develops it explicitly only for propositions, despite apparently regarding them as a special case of properties. But that assimilation does not fit some standard ways of thinking about propositions. In particular, propositions are often characterized as the objects of belief. In other words, they are defined by their role in mental life. That introduces a link to modes of presentation and makes the hyperintensional way in which Menzel individuates propositions much less surprising. By contrast, properties and relations are not usually defined by their role in mental life. More often, they are associated with ways in which objects resemble or differ from each other, irrespective of anyone’s mental life. That cuts the link to modes of presentation and makes the analogous hyperintensional way of individuating properties and relations much more surprising. If one wants a term for a 0-place property, ‘state of affairs’ is preferable to ‘proposition’, since the phrase ‘state of affairs’ lacks the associations with mental life which confuse the issue with the word ‘proposition’. Some states of affairs obtain, for instance Rome’s being the capital of Italy, while others do not, for instance Milan’s being the capital of Italy (for those uncomfortable with this terminology, the phrase ‘way for things to be’ may work better than ‘state of affairs’). Menzel’s approach would then imply that x’s being both square and square is a different state of affairs from x’s being either square or square, and both from x’s being square. Again, to many philosophers (including me) the differences seem blatantly to belong at the level of modes of presentation. Imagine a seismologist discovering that Taiwan has so many earthquakes not because two tectonic plates converge nearby but instead because either two tectonic plates converge nearby or two tectonic plates converge nearby. Again, the contrast with the robust role of modality in natural science is striking.

Menzel does not seem oblivious to this tension. Of the system as developed in the paper, with no complex predicates, he writes: ‘Intuitively, propositions, so represented, exhibit a level of richness and complexity that, in the framework at hand, properties do not; the latter are taken to be structurally simple’ (§1.5). After noting that ‘The framework developed here generalizes quite naturally to one that contains complex n-place predicates’, he expresses doubts as to whether the generalization would be semantically warranted (footnote 24). Yet he argues for hyperintensionalism in the two cases in parallel ways, and he does not suggest withdrawing those arguments. Presumably, unpacking ‘triangular’ and ‘trilateral’ by the complex predicates they definitionally abbreviate would make no crucial difference.

If a natural generalization of Menzel’s framework is indeed illegitimate, that hints that something may be wrong with the original framework too. His ambivalence could be interpreted as responsive to the instability of the underlying conception.

Hyperintensionalists more full-hearted than Menzel have an easier time, blithely distinguishing the property of smoking from the property of smoking or smoking because (supposedly) the former grounds the latter and not vice versa (or whatever). They treat their proliferating distinctions as a methodological benefit, giving greater flexibility, more degrees of freedom in modelling examples. Their attitude contrasts strikingly with that of modellers in the natural and social sciences, who regard increasing a model’s degree of freedom (roughly, the number of independent parameters) as a methodological cost, not a benefit, because it makes fitting the data too easy, thereby reducing explanatory power. I have developed this critique of hyperintensionalism elsewhere (Williamson 2021a), and plan to extend it further. Rather than pursue the issues here, I will briefly discuss a problem that depends only on Menzel’s fine-grained way of individuating propositions, whether or not he individuates his properties and relations in an analogously fine-grained way.

A notorious challenge faces theories such as Menzel’s, which individuate propositions in line with the syntactic structure of the sentences that express them: the Russell-Myhill paradox. It comes in various forms. I will focus on the one which most worries Menzel, because he regards it as the closest to a paradox of pure logic.

Menzel’s plural Russell-Myhill paradox starts from a standard comprehension principle PC for plurals, which says roughly that if something meets a condition, then there are the things that meet the condition (where ‘meets the condition’ is to be replaced by any open formula of the object language). Menzel describes PC as ‘arguably both ontologically innocuous and logically self-evident’, and is inclined to count it and the rest of a standard theory of plurals as ‘pure logic’ (§3.2)—though those grounds do not quite match his original criteria of universality and topic neutrality. Given his plural logic, including PC, and his fine-grained account of propositions, Menzel can then show that there are the propositions $\mathsf{rr}$ of the form $[\mathsf{zz} = \mathsf{zz}]$ that are not among the things $\mathsf{zz}$ they are about (where ‘$\mathsf{rr}$’ and ‘$\mathsf{zz}$’ are plural terms), and from there derive a contradiction in a quasi-Russellian way; $\mathsf{rr}$ is of course the analogue of the Russell set, the set of the sets which are not members of themselves.

Menzel considers two strategies for blocking the paradox. The first is to restrict the plural comprehension principle PC, as some others have done. Menzel would then sugar the pill by also denying that plural comprehension principles belong to ‘pure logic’ (contrary to his initial inclination), though quite what would be at stake in doing so is not very clear. One problem for this strategy is that the original interest of plurals was that, apparently, they satisfied the unrestricted comprehension principle PC (with the trivial exception of the empty case) without introducing any ontological mysteries, so that interpreting second-order quantification in plural terms would legitimize second-order logic with its unrestricted comprehension principle as a framework for mathematical theories (Boolos 1984). But once PC is restricted, plurals no longer properly serve that purpose. They still have some interest for the semantics of natural languages, but for mathematical purposes they are in danger of becoming useless middlemen between predicates and sets. That would deprive plurals of much of their apparent interest.

Menzel’s second strategy, which he seems to prefer to the first, is to reject the assumption that every sentence of his formal object language expresses a proposition. He sketches a hierarchical conception of propositions on which they are built up in stages from below. At no stage have all the propositions in the Russell plurality $\mathsf{rr}$ been formed, so $\mathsf{rr}$ themselves are never collectively available to be put into a proposition. The upshot is that although ‘there is the plurality $\mathsf{rr}$’, ‘there are no propositions about $\mathsf{rr}$’, as Menzel puts it (§3.2, his italics). That blocks the final stage of the paradoxical derivation by restricting propositional comprehension.

There is a problem. In stating his solution, Menzel asserts ‘there is the plurality $\mathsf{rr}$’ and ‘there are no propositions about $\mathsf{rr}$’. Do those sentences, in that context, express propositions? If they do, they express propositions about $\mathsf{rr}$, in Menzel’s sense; that is how his theory of propositions works. But then his account is wrong, because it implies that there are no propositions about $\mathsf{rr}$. So, on Menzel’s view, neither of those sentences should express a proposition. But then his attempt to state a solution to the plural Russell-Myhill paradox fails, because at the crucial point no proposition was expressed—he was just uttering empty words.

Someone might try to tell a story in which Menzel somehow gets his point across non-propositionally. But what is his point, if not a proposition? Presumably, according to him, his point is both believable and true, but how can it be believable or true if his best attempt to express it fails to express a proposition? Menzel’s propositions are in danger of becoming redundant, if everything works fine without them. What are Menzel-propositions supposed to be needed for? Just as Menzel’s contemplated restriction on plural comprehension undermines the utility of plurals, so his contemplated restriction on propositional comprehension undermines the utility of propositions.

The case is worth comparing with Menzel’s argument that ‘the entire framework of type theory teeters rather perilously on the edge of self-refutation’ (§1.3), since both concern expressive limitations. To illustrate his claim, he considers sentence (11), which he describes as ‘a metatheoretic truth of the semantics of higher-order logic’:

(11)
Every property belongs to some type.

According to Menzel, ‘It is difficult to see any other way of understanding (11) than as a general, untyped proposition […] whose quantifiers range unrestrictedly over a universe that contains all properties and all types’. Naturally, type-theorists deny that such untyped quantification is possible. They need not deny that (11) is meaningful; they can understand the quantifiers ‘every property’ and ‘some type’ as ordinary first-order quantifiers, so that (11) may have a meaning, just not one that connects with the intended semantics of higher-order logic in any useful way. Contrary to Menzel’s claim, (11) is not a metatheoretic truth of the semantics of higher-order logic; it is a Trojan horse, an unwanted gift he offers to type-theorists, who must learn from the Trojans’ example. By contrast, the sentences about $\mathsf{rr}$ above were written by Menzel, not pressed on him by his opponents; they are his statement of his tentative answer to an objection to his view of plural logic. In committing itself to their not expressing propositions, the answer is not so much teetering on the edge of self-refutation as marching right over it.

In a footnote at the end of his paper, Menzel mentions with apparent sympathy the attempted potentialist solution in Yu (2017) to the Russell-Myhill paradox for plural propositions. Since he does not elaborate on the idea, nor will I.

In support of his type-free approach, Menzel also offers a variety of more everyday sentences of English involving nominalizations (§ 1.2–3), such as his (6) and (7):

(6)
Charles loves David.
(7)
Charles loves wisdom.

As Russellian paradoxes teach us, nominalization cannot in general work as we want it to. An unrestricted first-order comprehension principle for properties is inconsistent. That does not imply that nominalization is meaningless, but for many theoretical purposes we do better to avoid its combinatorial limitations by working in a typed language and using type-raising rather than type-lowering devices. For example, rather than applying a type-lowering device to the predicate ‘is wise’ to get ‘wisdom’, as in (7), we can apply a suitable type-raising device to the two-place predicate ‘loves’ to get a new two-place predicate, higher-order in its second argument place, which can combine with ‘Charles’ and ‘is wise’ to make a meaningful sentence.

If we are condemned to work in an untyped framework, we can learn to live with its problems, under some or other ad hoc restrictions to maintain consistency. But we are not so condemned. We can work in higher-order intensional logic with its unrestricted comprehension principles, if we like both for plurals and for propositions. Menzel’s troubles stem from his ultra-fine-grained hyperintensional approach. His initial case against intensionalism turned out to be flimsy. That would not have mattered too much if his positive theory had proved to be explanatorily powerful. But it did not. In its struggle for consistency, it had drastically to restrict the comprehension principles needed for explanatory power. By any reasonable abductive criteria of simplicity, strength, elegance, and capacity to unify, higher-order intensional logic is by far the better theory.
