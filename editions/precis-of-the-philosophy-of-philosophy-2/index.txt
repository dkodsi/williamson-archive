# Précis of The Philosophy of Philosophy

Timothy Williamson

Philosophy and Phenomenological Research 82 (2) (2011): 470–471.

What is the epistemology of the sorts of armchair knowledge we sometimes achieve in philosophy? We cannot explain such knowledge by reinterpreting philosophical questions as questions about words or concepts. Although some philosophical questions are about words or concepts, most are not: they are, as they seem to be, about the things, typically independent of us, to which the words or concepts refer. Nor is philosophical knowledge grounded in linguistic or conceptual competence, except in the general way in which most non-philosophical knowledge also depends on linguistic or conceptual competence. Notions of analyticity are explanatorily impotent in epistemology. Nevertheless, the linguistic turn did enhance our ability to distinguish between valid and invalid arguments. The truth-preservingness of an argument depends on the truth-conditional semantic structure of its premises and conclusion, of which we now have better theories than ever before.

Philosophy is much more like other disciplines than many philosophers pretend. Armchair knowledge requires no special faculty of intuition. We achieve it in philosophy by painstaking applications of cognitive faculties whose main role is to provide mundane sorts of knowledge. For example, cognitive capacities that enable us to evaluate ordinary contingent counterfactual conditionals enable us, as an accidental by-product, to evaluate modal claims too. We can evaluate offline in imagination, under counterfactual suppositions, propositions we can evaluate online in perception. Both abilities involve skills in applying terms well beyond mere possession of the terms or linguistic competence. Offline employment of cognitive skills grounds armchair philosophical knowledge. Since experience helps hone such skills even when it plays no strictly evidential role, the distinction between a priori and a posteriori knowledge cuts much less deep than often thought. Since philosophical thought experiments are typically valid modal arguments, they can be understood similarly. The imagination plays its ordinary role in them when we evaluate the modal premises.

Good philosophy is evidence-based. As elsewhere in epistemology, the nature of evidence has been mischaracterized, psychologized under sceptical pressure in vain attempts to make its status uncontroversial. This has made the evidence look irrelevant to the non-psychological questions philosophers ask. Theories of intuitions are artefacts of this error. Even characterizations of philosophical method in terms of reflective equilibrium involve unwarranted epistemological internalism. Our evidence in philosophy is whatever we know; controversy over what our evidence comprises is inevitable. A knowledge-maximizing principle of charity provides qualified reassurance.
