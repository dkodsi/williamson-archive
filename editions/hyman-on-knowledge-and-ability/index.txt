# Hyman on knowledge and ability

Timothy Williamson

Philosophy and Phenomenological Research 97 (1) (2018): 243–248.

John Hyman’s fresh, perceptive, and acutely argued work on knowledge and action, culminating in his recent book (Hyman 2015), merits close attention. In particular, the massive industry of theorizing about reasons has much to learn from his powerful case that knowing that A is necessary for acting for the reason that A. His approach makes a far better starting-point for the study of reasons than the crude and misleading dichotomy between objective and subjective reasons. The latter is like an account of gold starting: ‘There are two kinds of gold, real gold and apparent gold’. Of course, the connection between knowledge and reasons for action is controversial; it is unnecessary to rehearse or expand Hyman’s arguments for it here (but see also Hawthorne and Magidor 2018).

Predictably, this note focuses on a disagreement between Hyman and me, not the large areas of agreement. In particular, it concerns Hyman’s central claim that knowing that A just is being able to do things for the reason that A, in brief, to do things because A (on the relevant reading of ‘because’, used henceforth). I deny Hyman’s equation of knowledge with an ability.

Hyman originally argued for his account of knowledge as an ability in much earlier work (1999). I briefly responded in a footnote to Knowledge and its Limits (Williamson 2000, p. 64 n. 1). Here is what I said there:

Hyman 1999 argues plausibly for another connection between knowledge and action: one knows that A if and only if one’s reason for doing something can be that A. But it does not follow that one can explain knowing that A as being able to do things for the reason that A (as Hyman wishes to do). Someone in a Gettier case who believes truly that A without knowing that A cannot do $\mathrm{X}_1$ for the reason that A, and cannot do $\mathrm{X}_2$ for the reason that A, …. But a single failure to know explains all these incapacities. If the incapacities constituted the failure to know, the correlation between the incapacities would be an unexplained coincidence.

In his book, Hyman replies to this objection, and to a similar one by Kieran Setiya (2013). After quoting the last two sentences of my footnote, he writes (Hyman 2015, pp. 181-2):

The objection is surely unconvincing. Of course, explanations vary depending on the context in which they are given and the purpose for which they are required, with what is assumed to be common knowledge, and so on. But it simply is not true that if someone could not do X because $p$, could not do Y because $p$, etc., and these incapacities constituted her failure to know, the correlation between them would be an unexplained coincidence. Clearly, it would be explained by whatever explains her failure to know, such as the fact that she did not see this morning’s paper.

To assess Hyman’s response, suppose that Mary cannot go for a bicycle ride because North Korea tested a missile yesterday, cannot give a lecture in Turkish because North Korea tested a missile yesterday, and so on. Why does she lack all those abilities? Of course, she might lack the ability to ride a bicycle, which would explain why she lacks the ability to go for a bicycle ride because North Korea tested a missile yesterday, and she might lack the ability to speak Turkish, which would explain why she lacks the ability to give a lecture in Turkish because North Korea tested a missile yesterday. The question does not automatically have a unified answer. However, we may assume that there are things she is able to do, but not able to do because North Korea tested a missile yesterday. A different sort of explanation is needed for those cases, although not even that guarantees a unified answer.

Hyman’s envisaged answer ‘Because she did not see this morning’s paper’ does not bear its relevance to the question ‘Why does Mary lack all those abilities?’ on its face. It assumes background knowledge of some mediating connection between this morning’s paper and her inabilities. If news of the missile were too recent to make this morning’s paper, the explanation would be no good. The salient candidate for such a mediating connection is this conditional: if Mary did not see this morning’s paper, she does not know that North Korea tested another missile yesterday. Given that connection, the answer ‘Because she did not see this morning’s paper’ implies a unified explicitly relevant answer to the question ‘Why does Mary lack all abilities to do things because North Korea tested a missile yesterday?’ The answer is: she lacks all those abilities because she does not know that North Korea tested a missile yesterday, as a result of not seeing this morning’s paper. But if not knowing that North Korea tested a missile yesterday just is lacking all those abilities, then giving that answer is tantamount to saying: she lacks all those abilities because she lacks all those abilities, as a result of not seeing this morning’s paper. The first part of that answer, ‘She lacks all those abilities because she lacks all those abilities’, is trivially circular; the second part, ‘She lacks all those abilities as a result of not seeing this morning’s paper’ still fails to explain why a single omission should deprive her of all those different abilities. Separately and together, they fail to do the work of unification.

Hyman supports his response with an analogy (2015, p. 182):

Similarly, if the inability to do crawl, or breaststroke, or doggy paddle, … constitutes the inability to swim, it does not follow that someone’s inability to do crawl, or breaststroke, or doggy paddle, … is an unexplained coincidence. It is explained by whatever explains her inability to swim.

This presupposes that her inability to swim has a unified explanation. Of course, it may have (Hyman’s ‘it does not follow’ is right). For instance, she may be unable to swim because she is afraid of the water and so never took swimming lessons, or because a bomb blew off her legs (those occurrences of ‘because’ have a different reading from earlier ones; they are broadly causal and do not require her knowledge). Equally, her inability to swim may lack a unified explanation. Her inability to do crawl may be explained by the incompetence of the crawl teacher, her inability to do breaststroke by a timetable clash involving the breaststroke lessons, her inability to do doggy paddle by the fact that she never had seaside holidays as a little girl, and so on. In such a case, her inability to swim is an unexplained coincidence.

In my original footnote, I used the word ‘unexplained’ rather than ‘inexplicable’ because the point was that the ability account of knowledge faces a so far unmet explanatory challenge: to unify the explanations of the inabilities. As we have seen, just citing a prior condition such as ‘She did not see this morning’s paper’ only appears to achieve such unification when we forget to apply the ability account to the role of knowledge in the background assumptions.

Hyman concludes his treatment of my objection by commenting on my statement that ‘a single failure to know explains all these incapacities’ (2015, p. 182):

And in a sense it does. It explains them by including them, in the way that a person’s inability to ride a bicycle explains why she cannot cycle from X to Y, from Y to Z, from Z to W, and so on.

The standard reason for being unable to ride a bicycle is not knowing how to ride a bicycle. However, on the view of know how I defend (Stanley and Williamson 2001), on which Hyman stays neutral (2015, p. 160), knowing how is just a special kind of knowing that, and so raises all the same issues again. It is therefore simpler to imagine a different cause of her inability to ride a bicycle, such as the fact that she has lost her legs. That clearly gives a unified explanation for her inability to ride from X to Y, her inability to ride from Y to Z, her inability to ride from Z to W, and so on. But not all explanations by inclusion are equally good. For instance, consider a random list of a thousand miscellaneous things I cannot do. My general inability to do any of those things implies my particular inability to do any given one of them, but it does not well explain that particular inability, because the general inability is too disunified. The trouble is that the ability account of knowledge makes the state of not knowing that A look too disunified to well explain the inability to do something because A.

Thus I do not consider Hyman to have met the explanatory challenge to his ability account of knowledge. However, issues about what makes explanations good or bad are notoriously tricky to handle, so I do not want to rest everything on the explanatory challenge. I will therefore raise some other issues for the ability account.

A preliminary question concerns the intended meaning of the equation between knowing that A and being able to do things because A. In my original objection, I said that Hyman ‘argues plausibly’ for the biconditional ‘one knows that A if and only if one’s reason for doing something can be that A’. For the sake of argument, I then implicitly conceded the truth and even necessity of the biconditional, in order to argue for an explanatory asymmetry between its two sides. Nevertheless, that concession might be sufficient for the identity of knowledge with an ability, on a coarse-grained intensional conception of properties. For if the knowledge that A is just the property of knowing that A, the ability to do things because A is just the property of being able to do things because A, and necessarily coextensive properties are identical, then if it is necessary that all and only those who know that A are able to do things because A, it follows that the knowledge that A is identical with the ability to do things because A. Of course, as linguistic expressions, the right-hand side might still explain the left-hand side, just as in a verbal definition the definiens explains the definiendum, even though they refer to the same property.

However, Hyman seems not to conceive the matter in those thin intensional terms. He takes Setiya and me to be denying that the knowledge is identical with the ability (2015, p. 181). He does not suggest that, in conceding the biconditional (for the sake of argument), I may in effect be conceding the very point at issue. Thus Hyman seems to have in mind a thicker conception of abilities, and so of knowledge too.

On a thicker conception of abilities, they are not all or nothing. Diachronically, one may retain the ability to do something even through changes in one’s ability to do it. For instance, many young children have the ability to speak English, but their ability to speak English will improve as they grow up. Someone who can only do breaststroke already has the ability to swim, but her ability to swim will improve when she learns crawl too. Synchronically, two people may share the ability to do something, while also differing in that very ability. The young child’s ability to speak English is more limited than the parent’s. Someone who can do both breaststroke and crawl and someone who can only do breaststroke share the ability to swim while differing in their ability to swim.

Such comparisons also make sense for the abilities with which Hyman identifies knowledge. However, they do not match the corresponding comparisons of knowledge. For instance, consider someone who knows that King Richard III was killed at the battle of Bosworth. He has the ability to do things because Richard was killed at Bosworth. He may take a walk around Bosworth for that reason. Unfortunately, as a result of his passion for collecting military memorabilia, his legs are later blown off by a bomb. His ability to do things is drastically curtailed. He can no longer walk. In particular, his ability to do things because Richard was killed at Bosworth is drastically curtailed. He can no longer take a walk around Bosworth because Richard was killed at Bosworth. But his knowledge is not curtailed. He still knows that Richard was killed at Bosworth. He still has some ability to do things because Richard was killed at Bosworth. He can still collect pictures of Bosworth for that reason. Nevertheless, his ability has changed while his knowledge has stayed the same. Similarly, he differs from two-legged enthusiasts for military history in the ability to do things because Richard was killed at Bosworth, without differing from them in the knowledge that Richard was killed at Bosworth. On this rather natural conception of abilities, not all differences in the ability to do things because A are differences in the knowledge that A, so the ability is distinct from the knowledge.

So far, I have been questioning the equation of the knowledge that A with the ability to do things because A, while granting for the sake of argument that being able to do things because A is both necessary and sufficient for knowing that A. But further reflection suggests that being able to do things because A is neither always sufficient nor always necessary for knowing that A, as I will now argue. As the reader will note, my arguments cast no doubt on Hyman’s important claim that knowing that A is necessary (but not sufficient) for doing things because A.

Is the knowledge necessary for the ability? Currently, my eyes are open. However, I am still able to do various things with my eyes shut. For instance, I can find my away about the room with my eyes shut. It is just that, to exercise that ability, I have to shut my eyes. Amongst the things I can do with my eyes shut are various things I do because my eyes are shut. For instance, when my eyes are shut, I move cautiously because my eyes are shut. Right now, with my eyes open, I have the ability to move cautiously because my eyes are shut. It is just that, to exercise that ability, I have to shut my eyes. Therefore, with my eyes open, I have the general ability to do things because my eyes are shut. But, with my eyes open, I do not know that my eyes are shut; knowledge is veridical. Thus knowing that my eyes are shut is not necessary for being able to do things because my eyes are shut. It is only necessary for doing things because my eyes are shut. Thus the knowledge is not in general necessary for the ability.

Is the knowledge sufficient for the ability? An uncontentious starting-point is that people have many abilities they never exercise. For instance, most people have, but never exercise, the ability to read a telephone directory from cover to cover while holding a banana. I have that ability, and I know that I will never exercise it. Similarly, one may know that A, and have the ability to do things for the reason that A, but never exercise that ability. No occasion for exercising it may ever arise. One might even know that one will never exercise the ability.

A criminal being led out to execution might know the conjunction that the money is hidden in the cellar and he will never do anything even partly because the money is hidden in the cellar; such a conjunction is clearly knowable in principle. Consequently, on Hyman’s account, the criminal is able to do things because the money is hidden in the cellar and he will never do anything even partly because the money is hidden in the cellar. But is it possible for him to exercise that putative ability, perhaps in completely different circumstances? To make the structure of the argument easier to track, I will abbreviate ‘the money is hidden in the cellar’ as ‘M’. To exercise the putative ability would be to do something because M and he will never do anything even partly because M. In view of the first conjunct of that reason (‘M’), to do that would be to do something partly because M. But that contradicts the second conjunct of the reason (‘he will never do anything even partly because M’). Hence the task is impossible, for on Hyman’s view, as on mine, reasons are veridical: if (in counterfactual circumstances) the criminal does something because M and he will never do anything even partly because M, then he knows that M and he will never do anything even partly because M, so M and he never will do anything even partly because M.1 Thus it is impossible for him to do anything for that conjunctive reason. Consequently, he lacks the ability to do something because the conjunction holds. Yet he knows that the conjunction holds. Thus the knowledge is not in general sufficient for the ability.

Here is another example. Let s be the sentence ‘Everything is as it actually is’, on the reading formalized in modal logic as $\forall \mathrm{p} (\mathrm{p} \leftrightarrow @\mathrm{p})$ with quantification into sentence position and the rigidifying ‘actually’ operator @. As uttered in any possible world w, s expresses a proposition $p_w$ true in w and in no other world (compare ‘The time is now’). Since s is guaranteed to express a truth, it is trivial, though not very useful, to know the truth s expresses under the guise of s. Of course, which truth one knows depends on which world one is in. As usual, in some possible worlds one acts on the knowledge, for instance in reasoning, in other possible worlds one does not. Consider a possible world w in which someone knows $p_w$ under the guise of s but never acts on the knowledge. We may assume that, in w, she does not know $p_w$ under any other guise. On Hyman’s account, she still has the (unexercised) ability to do things for the reason $p_w$. But that proposition is true only in w; in any other world, $p_w$ is false and so is not a reason for which she can do anything. Thus she does not do anything for the reason $p_w$ in any possible world. Perhaps, in many other possible worlds, she does something for a reason she would there express with s, but there s expresses a different proposition, and so a different reason. Thus it is metaphysically impossible for her to do anything for the reason $p_w$. Since one has the ability to do something only if it is metaphysically possible for one to do it, even in w she lacks the ability to do things for the reason $p_w$. Nevertheless, in w, she knows $p_w$. The conclusion is as before: the knowledge is not in general sufficient for the ability.

We have seen counterexamples in both directions to Hyman’s equation of knowledge that A with the ability to do things because A. Although genuine counterexamples, they all look like rather special cases. It may well be that normally one knows that A if and only if one is able to do things because A (on the relevant understanding of ‘because’). That strong and far from accidental correlation highlights the central role of knowledge in intelligent life. But it does not make knowledge an ability. Rather, knowledge of reasons is a crucial precondition for acting for those reasons, and a crucial role for our knowledge is to give us our stock of reasons for action. Those points adequately explain the normal correlation between knowing that A and being able to do things because A. The value of Hyman’s salutary emphasis on the relation between knowledge and action does not depend on his equation of knowledge with an ability. His book is a major contribution to the growing realization that knowledge can be properly understood only in relation to action, and that action can be properly understood only in relation to knowledge.2
