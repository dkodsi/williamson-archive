Gettier cases in epistemic logic

Timothy Williamson

Inquiry 56 (1) (2013): 1–14.

Abstract The possibility of justified true belief without knowledge is normally motivated by informally classified examples. This paper shows that it can also be motivated more formally, by a natural class of epistemic models in which both knowledge and justified belief (in the relevant sense) are represented. The models involve a distinction between appearance and reality. Gettier cases arise because the agent’s ignorance increases as the gap between appearance and reality widens. The models also exhibit an epistemic asymmetry between good and bad cases that sceptics seem to ignore or deny.

I.

Thought experiments are a source of unmysterious philosophical knowledge. I have defended that statement elsewhere.1 Nevertheless, when conclusions so reached are subsequently corroborated by other forms of argument, the latter need not be redundant. We are hardly good enough at philosophy to dispense with independent checks on our working. Moreover, less case-oriented methods of argument promise deeper theoretical insight into phenomena whose sheer occurrence has already been recognized.

Take Gettier cases. Since the publication of Gettier’s 1963 paper,2 there has been a consensus in epistemology that cases such as he presents are counterexamples to the view that justified true belief is equivalent to, or at least sufficient for, knowledge. The consensus survives, despite experiments that have been claimed to show that it depends on the ethnicity or gender of those who evaluate the cases.3 More recent experiments suggest that the threat to the consensus may have been a false alarm, by calling into question the robust replicability of the results.4 Nevertheless, it would be reassuring to have some independent way of checking that in Gettier cases the subject does not know after all. Even granted that reflective verdicts on such cases do not vary significantly with ethnicity or gender, one can worry that those verdicts might still reflect some oddity of the human cognitive system for ascribing ‘knowledge’, rather than a genuine feature of the underlying epistemological kind to which the term refers.5 An argument cast within a more general theoretical framework has a fair chance of bypassing any such putative oddity.

This paper uses the more general theoretical framework of epistemic and doxastic logic, in the tradition going back to Hintikka.6 Models will be constructed in which justified true belief in the relevant sense can be compared with knowledge; cases of the former without the latter will be shown to arise under reasonable conditions.

We must take care about methodology. If we simply introduce three primitive operators for justification, belief and knowledge respectively and permit them to vary independently of each other, then modelling justified true belief without knowledge is trivial. By the same token, such models constitute no serious evidence for the genuine possibility of that combination. The point is instead to minimize the number of moving parts in the models, using reasonable idealizations and other well-motivated constraints, for if we can show that all models of the type so characterized contain cases of the required combination, that is significant evidence for its genuine possibility. Relaxing the constraints will not make the combination less possible. What we seek from the model-building exercise is an independent test, not conclusive proof, of whether justified true belief implies knowledge. As in the natural sciences, the proper use of formal models involves an ineliminable informal element of good judgement. Readers who dislike the models in this paper can try to build better ones, to be judged by the same standard.

The models described below turn out to have other uses too. For example, they exhibit epistemic asymmetries of a sort that sceptical arguments have been accused of ignoring, and so provide independent evidence for an obstacle to scepticism.

II.

For present purposes, an epistemic model is simply an ordered pair ⟨W,R⟩\langle \mathrm{W},\mathrm{R}\rangle, where W\mathrm{W} is any non-empty set and R\mathrm{R} is any binary relation (in extension) over W\mathrm{W}, that is, a set of ordered pairs of members of W\mathrm{W}.

Informally, we think of the members of W\mathrm{W} as worlds, or maximally specific states, although that informal interpretation is no part of the formal definition of what it is to be an epistemic model. The propositions in the model are just the subsets of W\mathrm{W}. Thus propositions are identified with sets of worlds. The variables ww, xx, . . . will be used to range over worlds, and the variables pp, qq, . . . to range over propositions. A proposition pp is true in a world ww if and only if ww belongs to pp; otherwise pp is false in ww. Consequently, a proposition pp entails a proposition qq if and only if pp is a subset of qq: qq is true in every world in which pp is true. Hence propositions are identical if and only if they are mutually entailing, true in the same worlds.

The model treats propositions as coarse-grained, without internal structure corresponding to the semantic structure of sentences that express them. Of course, such a treatment looks like an obstacle to understanding epistemic puzzles where agents seem to take different attitudes to the propositions expressed by ‘Hesperus is bright’ and ‘Phosphorus is bright’, or by ‘2 + 2 = 4’ and ‘There is no largest prime number’—although it is doubtful that even a finer-grained Russellian or Fregean treatment of propositions will solve all such puzzles by itself. In any case, even if the coarse-grained treatment is merely an idealization, it is a harmlessly simplifying one for our purposes. For given a model of justified true belief without knowledge for coarse-grained propositions, we can turn it into a model of justified true belief without knowledge for fine-grained propositions by associating in some uniform manner each coarse-grained proposition in the model with one of the fine-grained propositions true at the same worlds. The distinction between coarse-grained and fine-grained propositions is not what does the work in the models below. Gettier cases and Frege cases raise very different problems.

All the epistemology in an epistemic model ⟨W,R⟩\langle \mathrm{W},\mathrm{R}\rangle is encoded in its second element, R\mathrm{R}. Informally, we think of R\mathrm{R} as the relation of epistemic accessibility for a given agent at a given time. In other words, a world xx is accessible from a world ww (wRxw\mathrm{R}x) if and only if whatever the agent then knows in ww is true in xx: for all the agent knows in ww, she is in xx (xx is epistemically possible in ww). For any proposition pp, let Kp\mathrm{K}p be the proposition true in a world ww if and only if pp is true in every world accessible from ww: Kp={w:∀x(wRx⇒x∈p)}\mathrm{K}p=\{w:\forall x\,(w\mathrm{R}x\Rightarrow x\in p)\}. Informally, Kp\mathrm{K}p is identified with the proposition that the agent knows pp. Thus the agent counts as knowing whatever is true in all the worlds that for all she knows she is in. This is in no deep sense an analysis of knowledge in terms of epistemic accessibility, but simply the imagined decoding of the epistemic information encoded in the relation R\mathrm{R}.

The treatment of K\mathrm{K} as a knowledge operator according to the definition above enforces a form of logical omniscience for knowledge beyond that already implicit in the coarse-grained treatment of propositions. Specifically, if the agent knows each premise of a valid deductive argument, then the agent ipso facto knows the conclusion. For since the argument is valid, the conjunction of the premises entails the conclusion; in other words, the intersection of the premises is a subset of the conclusion. Hence, if the agent knows each premise in a world ww, and a world xx is accessible from ww, then each premise is true in xx, so the conclusion is true in xx; thus, the conclusion is true in every world accessible from ww, so she knows the conclusion in ww. This logical omniscience too can be regarded as a harmlessly simplifying idealization of the models. If even perfect logicians are susceptible to Gettier cases, humans with more limited logical skills should not expect to be immune.

A convenient feature of epistemic models is that in each world ww there is a strongest proposition R(w)\mathrm{R}(w) known by the agent. It is simply the set of accessible worlds: R(w)={x:wRx}\mathrm{R}(w)=\{x:w\mathrm{R}x\}. For, unpacking the definition of K\mathrm{K}, she knows R(w)\mathrm{R}(w) in ww, and for any proposition pp, if she knows pp in ww then R(w)\mathrm{R}(w) entails pp. In terms of membership, R(w)\mathrm{R}(w) is the smallest proposition the agent knows in ww. More simply, R(w)\mathrm{R}(w) is what she knows in ww. It is the horizon of open epistemic possibility.

Knowledge is factive: whatever is known is true. That uncontentious principle requires the relation R\mathrm{R} to be reflexive, for if some world ww were not accessible from itself, Kp\mathrm{K}p would be true but pp false in ww, where pp is the proposition containing all worlds except ww. Conversely, suppose that R\mathrm{R} is reflexive; let qq be any proposition and xx any world. Then Kq\mathrm{K}q is true in xx only if qq is true in every world accessible from xx; since xx is accessible from itself, qq is true in xx. Thus K\mathrm{K} is factive. All the models considered below have a reflexive accessibility relation.

III.

To make progress, we must consider a more specific class of epistemic models. We do so by introducing a very simple form of the distinction between reality and appearance. Imagine the agent gaining perceptual knowledge of some environmental parameter (such as temperature) that takes values in a set E\mathrm{E}. For simplicity, imagine further that the parameter always appears to her in a maximally specific way, in the sense that exactly one member of E\mathrm{E} appears to her to be the value of the parameter (e.g., the temperature appears to be exactly 30 degrees Celsius). For present purposes it is unnecessary to build in the complications of unspecific appearances. There is no limit in principle to how far the apparent value of the parameter can diverge from its real value at a given time. As another idealization, we identify worlds with ordered pairs of members of E\mathrm{E} (W=E2\mathrm{W}=\mathrm{E}^2). Informally, the first member of an ordered pair represents the real value of the parameter; the second member represents its apparent value to the agent. Such impoverished worlds contain enough information to be adequate for present purposes; everything else can be ignored.

We treat present appearances to the agent as transparent to her: she knows all about them, in the sense that no world where the parameter has a different apparent value is epistemically possible for her. Formally, if ⟨e,f⟩R⟨e*,f*⟩\langle e,f\rangle \mathrm{R}\langle e^{*},f^{*}\rangle then f=f*f=f^{*}. Although appearances are not in fact so epistemically privileged,7 treating them as such is yet another harmlessly simplifying concession for present purposes. If even agents to whom appearances are transparent suffer from Gettier cases, humans with more limited knowledge of their appearances should not expect to be immune.

A mathematical temptation is to make the stronger biconditional stipulation that if f=f*f=f^{*} then ⟨e,f⟩R⟨e*,f*⟩\langle e,f\rangle \mathrm{R}\langle e^{*},f^{*}\rangle as well as vice versa. That temptation must be resisted, for it is tantamount to scepticism about the external world. It implies that the agent has no non-trivial knowledge at all of the real value of the parameter, for example of the external temperature. For let pp be a non-trivial proposition about the real value. It is non-trivial in the sense that it is false in at least one world ⟨g,h⟩\langle g,h\rangle. It is about the real value in the sense that its truth-value depends only on that value: pp has the same truth-value in any two worlds with the same real value. Consider the agent in a world ⟨e,f⟩\langle e,f\rangle. Since the real value is the same in ⟨g,h⟩\langle g,h\rangle and ⟨g,f⟩\langle g,f\rangle, and pp is false in ⟨g,h⟩\langle g,h\rangle, pp is also false in ⟨g,f⟩\langle g,f\rangle. But the envisaged converse stipulation implies that ⟨e,f⟩R⟨g,f⟩\langle e,f\rangle \mathrm{R}\langle g,f\rangle, since the apparent value is the same in the two worlds. Thus pp is false in a world epistemically accessible from ⟨e,f⟩\langle e,f\rangle, so in ⟨e,f⟩\langle e,f\rangle the agent does not know pp: if the agent knows only appearances, for all she knows in ⟨e,f⟩\langle e,f\rangle she is in ⟨g,f⟩\langle g,f\rangle. The stronger, biconditional stipulation makes her perfectly knowledgeable about appearances but perfectly ignorant of the corresponding realities, even though we started with a scenario in which she was gaining ordinary perceptual knowledge of her environment. To avoid such crass scepticism, we must permit some worlds to be inaccessible from others with the same appearance.

A better picture is this. When the real value matches the apparent value, the agent knows something non-trivial about the real value. For example, when the temperature both is and appears to be 30 degrees, she knows that it is not zero. Some world with a different real value but the same apparent value is not epistemically possible for her. However, she still does not know everything about the real value, for her perceptual apparatus is not perfectly discriminating. For example, when the temperature is 30.0006 degrees, she does not know that it is not 30.0007 degrees. Some world with a different real value but the same apparent value is epistemically possible for her. The latter world is distinct from the one she is in, which is of course epistemically accessible from itself by factiveness. Formally, for any value ff in E\mathrm{E}, the worlds accessible from the world ⟨f,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle with identical real and apparent values are ⟨f,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle itself (trivially, by reflexivity) and at least one other world:

{⟨f,f⟩}⊂R(⟨f,f⟩)\{\langle f,f\rangle\}\subset \mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle)(#1)

To complete the picture, a natural postulate is that as the real value diverges more and more from the given apparent value, the agent knows less and less about the real value. To state the relation formally, we postulate a metric on E\mathrm{E}, in the mathematical sense of the term, to measure distance in the space of values. The metric is a function d\mathrm{d} from pairs of values in E\mathrm{E} to real numbers; d(e,f)\mathrm{d}(e,f) measures the difference in value between ee and ff. The standard mathematical definition of a metric also requires d\mathrm{d} to have the following properties, for all e,f,ge,f,g in E\mathrm{E}: 0≤d(e,f)0\le \mathrm{d}(e,f) (no distance is negative); d(e,f)=0\mathrm{d}(e,f)=0 if and only if e=fe=f (no value is as close to any other as any value is to itself); d(e,f)=d(f,e)\mathrm{d}(e,f)=\mathrm{d}(f,e) (distance is symmetric); d(e,g)≤d(e,f)+d(f,g)\mathrm{d}(e,g)\le \mathrm{d}(e,f)+\mathrm{d}(f,g) (the triangle inequality: metaphorically, the shortest journey in quality space from ee to gg is no longer than the journey from ee to ff followed by that from ff to gg).

We can now state the constraint on the accessibility relation R\mathrm{R} that ignorance grows with the distance of the real value from the apparent value:

d(e,f)≤d(e*,f)if and only ifR(⟨e,f⟩)⊆R(⟨e*,f⟩)\mathrm{d}(e,f)\le \mathrm{d}(e^{*},f)\quad\text{if and only if}\quad \mathrm{R}(\langle e,f\rangle)\subseteq \mathrm{R}(\langle e^{*},f\rangle)(#2)

In other words, if the real value diverges no more from the given apparent value in the world ⟨e,f⟩\langle e,f\rangle than it does in the world ⟨e*,f⟩\langle e^{*},f\rangle, then the agent knows at least as much in the former world as in the latter; every world she can exclude in the latter she can already exclude in the former. Conversely, the agent knows at least as much in one world as in another only if the gap between appearance and reality is no wider in the former than in the latter.

The rationale for constraint (#2) is that any increase in the gap between appearance and reality has an epistemic cost for the agent: more knowledge is lost. However, (#2) is not claimed to be an exceptionless law of epistemology. Rather, it is a natural idealization of a wide range of cases.

We check that our stipulations are reasonable by constructing a natural model that verifies them. Let E\mathrm{E} be the set of real numbers, so a world is an ordered pair of a real-valued real value and a real-valued apparent value. For real numbers ee and ff, let d(e,f)\mathrm{d}(e,f) be the absolute distance |e−f||e-f|, so d\mathrm{d} is a metric. Let cc be a positive real number; the epistemic structure of the model will not depend on the particular value of cc. We define the relation R\mathrm{R} thus: ⟨e,f⟩R⟨e*,f*⟩\langle e,f\rangle \mathrm{R}\langle e^{*},f^{*}\rangle if and only if |e*−f*|≤|e−f|+c|e^{*}-f^{*}|\le |e-f|+c and of course f=f*f=f^{*}. Thus the worlds accessible from a given world are those where the apparent value is exactly the same and the gap between it and the real value exceeds the gap in the given world by at most the constant cc. Obviously R\mathrm{R} is reflexive. In any world, what the agent knows about the real value is that it falls within some margin for error of the apparent value; she does not know exactly what that margin is, since it varies across the accessible worlds.

Note that R(⟨e,f⟩)={(⟨g,f⟩:|g−f|≤|e−f|+c}\mathrm{R}(\langle e,f\rangle)=\{(\langle g,f\rangle:|g-f|\le |e-f|+c\}. In particular, therefore, R(⟨f,f⟩)={(⟨g,f⟩:|g−f|≤c}\mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle)=\{(\langle g,f\rangle:|g-f|\le c\}. Thus ⟨f,f⟩R⟨f+c,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle \mathrm{R}\langle f+c,f\rangle, so (#1) holds. To check (#2), first suppose that |e−f|≤|e*−f||e-f|\le |e^{*}-f|. Then R(⟨e,f⟩)={(⟨g,f⟩:|g−f|≤|e−f|+c}⊆{(⟨g,f⟩:|g−f|≤|e*−f|+c}=R(⟨e*,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle e,f\rangle)=\{(\langle g,f\rangle:|g-f|\le |e-f|+c\}\subseteq\{(\langle g,f\rangle:|g-f|\le |e^{*}-f|+c\}=\mathrm{R}(\langle e^{*},f\rangle). Conversely, suppose that R(⟨e,f⟩)⊆R(⟨e*,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle e,f\rangle)\subseteq \mathrm{R}(\langle e^{*},f\rangle). Let g=f+|e−f|+cg=f+|e-f|+c, so |g−f|=|e−f|+c|g-f|=|e-f|+c. Hence ⟨g,f⟩∈R(⟨e,f⟩)\langle g,f\rangle\in \mathrm{R}(\langle e,f\rangle), so ⟨g,f⟩∈R(⟨e*,f⟩)\langle g,f\rangle\in \mathrm{R}(\langle e^{*},f\rangle), so |e−f|+c=|g−f|≤|e*−f|+c|e-f|+c=|g-f|\le |e^{*}-f|+c, so |e−f|≤|e*−f||e-f|\le |e^{*}-f|, as required. Thus the model verifies both constraints, (#1) and (#2).

In the rest of the paper, we explore some epistemic consequences of the constraints (#1) and (#2) themselves. For the sake of generality, we avoid reliance on the model just sketched. Nevertheless, the model may help the reader visualize the arguments below more clearly.8

IV.

One obvious consequence of the constraints is that whatever is epistemically possible in a world in which appearance matches reality is epistemically possible in any world with the same appearance. For (#2) implies that since d(f,f)=0≤d(e,f)\mathrm{d}(f,f)=0\le \mathrm{d}(e,f) by the axioms for a metric, R(⟨f,f⟩)⊆R(⟨e,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle)\subseteq \mathrm{R}(\langle e,f\rangle). In particular, since the world in which appearance matches reality is accessible from itself, it is accessible from any world with the same appearance; ⟨f,f⟩∈R(⟨e,f⟩)\langle f,f\rangle\in \mathrm{R}(\langle e,f\rangle).

We can go further. By (#1), R(⟨f,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle) contains some world ⟨f#,f⟩\langle f^{\#},f\rangle other than ⟨f,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle, so f#≠ff^{\#}\ne f. Although appearance in fact matches reality perfectly, and the agent knows the appearance perfectly, she does not know the reality perfectly: for all she knows, it is f#f^{\#} rather than ff. Now suppose that R(⟨f#,f⟩)⊆R(⟨f,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle f^{\#},f\rangle)\subseteq \mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle). Then by (#2), d(f#,f)≤d(f,f)=0\mathrm{d}(f^{\#},f)\le \mathrm{d}(f,f)=0, so f#=ff^{\#}=f by the axioms for a metric, contrary to hypothesis. Thus R(⟨f#,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle f^{\#},f\rangle) contains some world ⟨f##,f⟩\langle f^{\#\#},f\rangle not in R(⟨f,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle). Consequently, the accessibility relation is non-transitive. For ⟨f,f⟩R⟨f#,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle \mathrm{R}\langle f^{\#},f\rangle and ⟨f#,f⟩R⟨f##,f⟩\langle f^{\#},f\rangle \mathrm{R}\langle f^{\#\#},f\rangle, but not ⟨f,f⟩R⟨f##,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle \mathrm{R}\langle f^{\#\#},f\rangle. A natural interpretation of this result is that when appearance matches reality, for all the agent knows there is a small gap between them, and when there is a small gap between appearance and reality, for all she knows there is a larger gap, but when appearance matches reality, the agent does know that there is not the larger gap between appearance and reality (scepticism is false). As always in epistemic logic, the non-transitivity of accessibility yields failures of the KK principle, also known as positive introspection and the 4 axiom of modal logic, that if one knows pp then one knows that one knows pp.9 Specifically, in the world ⟨f,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle the agent knows R(⟨f,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle), but she does not know that she knows R(⟨f,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle), because she does not know R(⟨f,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle) (even though it is true) in the accessible world ⟨f#,f⟩\langle f^{\#},f\rangle. There is no need to labour the failure of the KK principle here.

A more interesting consequence of the constraints is that accessibility is also non-symmetric, for ⟨f##,f⟩R⟨f,f⟩\langle f^{\#\#},f\rangle \mathrm{R}\langle f,f\rangle but not vice versa. A natural interpretation of this result is that there is an epistemic asymmetry between the good (anti-sceptical) scenario ⟨f,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle, where appearance matches reality, and the bad (sceptical) scenario ⟨f##,f⟩\langle f^{\#\#},f\rangle, where appearance diverges widely from reality. In the bad case, for all the agent knows she is in the good case; by contrast, in the good case, the agent knows that she is not in the bad case. Sceptics often neglect the possibility of such epistemic asymmetries.10 As always in epistemic logic, the non-symmetry of accessibility yields failures of the ‘Brouwerian’ axiom B that if pp is false then one knows that one does not know pp. Specifically, in the bad case ⟨f##,f⟩\langle f^{\#\#},f\rangle the proposition R(⟨f,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle) is false, but the agent does not know that she does not know R(⟨f,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle), because for all she knows she is in the good case ⟨f,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle, in which she does know R(⟨f,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle). The general failure of the B axiom for knowledge was pointed out by Hintikka.11 It immediately yields failures of the ‘negative introspection’ principle (known as axiom 5 or E in modal logic) that if one does not know pp then one knows that one does not know pp, for by factiveness if pp is false, then one does not know pp. Although many researchers in epistemic logic continue to treat the accessibility relation for knowledge as symmetric and transitive as well as reflexive, and the assumptions are often harmless idealizations for their purposes, they are not harmless for purposes of serious epistemology.

We can also extract more positive consequences from the constraints. For example, we can show that the only world in which the agent knows R(⟨f,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle) is ⟨f,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle itself. For suppose that the agent knows R(⟨f,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle) in a world ⟨e,g⟩\langle e,g\rangle. By the structure of R\mathrm{R}, g=fg=f. Thus KR(⟨f,f⟩)\mathrm{KR}(\langle f,f\rangle) is true in ⟨e,f⟩\langle e,f\rangle. Since R(⟨e,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle e,f\rangle) is what she knows in ⟨e,f⟩\langle e,f\rangle, R(⟨e,f⟩)⊆R(⟨f,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle e,f\rangle)\subseteq \mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle). Therefore, by (#2), d(e,f)≤d(f,f)=0\mathrm{d}(e,f)\le \mathrm{d}(f,f)=0, so e=fe=f. Thus ⟨e,g⟩=⟨f,f⟩\langle e,g\rangle=\langle f,f\rangle, as required. Elsewhere, I have shown how such propositions known in only one world generate cases where the agent knows a truth pp even though it is virtually certain on her own current evidence that she does not know pp. Details are omitted here, because they involve the introduction of an apparatus of evidential probabilities.12

The consequences just drawn from our constraints have a strongly externalist feel. However, the constraints themselves were not motivated by externalism, but only by a preference for simplicity over complexity, and for anti-scepticism over scepticism. In particular, the agent was granted a knowledge of appearances as complete as internalists could desire. That makes the externalist upshot all the more significant.

V.

To discuss Gettier cases, we must interpret belief and justification over the models. That is the task of this section.

In epistemic logic, the normal way to interpret belief is by adding another binary relation S\mathrm{S} between worlds as a further constituent of the model, to represent doxastic accessibility for the given agent at the given time. A world xx is doxastically accessible from a world ww (wSxw\mathrm{S}x) if and only if whatever the agent believes in ww is true in xx: xx is doxastically possible in ww. For any proposition pp, let Bp\mathrm{B}p be the proposition true in a world ww if and only if pp is true in every world doxastically accessible from ww: Bp={w:∀x(wSx⇒x∈p)}\mathrm{B}p=\{w:\forall x\,(w\mathrm{S}x\Rightarrow x\in p)\}. Informally, Bp\mathrm{B}p is identified with the proposition that the agent believes pp. Thus the agent counts as believing whatever is true in all the worlds that for all she believes she is in. The treatment of B\mathrm{B} as a belief operator enforces a form of logical omniscience for belief beyond that already implicit in the coarse-grained treatment of propositions. Specifically, if the agent believes each premise of a valid deductive argument, then the agent ipso facto believes the conclusion, by the same reasoning as for K\mathrm{K}. As before, this logical omniscience, too, can be regarded as a harmlessly simplifying idealization of the models.

Since belief is not factive—some falsehoods are believed—the relation S is not required to be reflexive, unlike R. However, S may be required to be serial, in the sense that every world has S to at least one world. For if no world were doxastically accessible from a world w, then every proposition would count vacuously as believed in w, making the agent inconsistent. Although one can be irrational and have inconsistent beliefs, it is doubtful that one can have inconsistent justified beliefs.13 Conversely, if from each world at least one world in the model is accessible, then neither Bp&B¬p\mathrm{B}p\mathbin{\&}\mathrm{B}\neg p nor B(p&¬p)\mathrm{B}(p\mathbin{\&}\neg p) is true at any world: the agent never believes a contradiction, either in the sense of believing both of a pair of contradictories or in the sense of believing their conjunction.

A natural assumption is that, if one knows p, one believes p. It is equivalent to the constraint that doxastic accessibility implies epistemic accessibility. For suppose that a world x is not epistemically accessible from a world w. Then in w the agent knows the proposition p containing all worlds except x. So, if knowledge requires belief, in w the agent believes p, so x is not doxastically accessible from w. Conversely, if doxastic accessibility implies epistemic accessibility, then whatever is true in all epistemically accessible worlds is true in all doxastically accessible worlds, so knowledge requires belief. Let us accept that requirement.

In epistemic models of the sort introduced above, we can define the doxastic accessibility relation S\mathrm{S} well enough for present purposes without adding it as a separate component. To motivate the definition, let us make the simplifying but natural assumption that what the agent believes, unlike what she knows, depends only on the apparent value of the relevant parameter: for any values ee, e*e^{*}, and ff, exactly the same worlds are doxastically accessible from ⟨e,f⟩\langle e,f\rangle as from ⟨e*,f⟩\langle e^{*},f\rangle. Although an agent’s beliefs could be sensitive to factors other than appearance, we focus on agents who do not base their beliefs on such factors. Since the agent has the same beliefs in a world ⟨e,f⟩\langle e,f\rangle as in the world ⟨f,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle, all that remains is to determine what the agent believes in a world where appearance matches reality. Since knowledge requires belief, whatever she knows in ⟨f,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle she believes in ⟨f,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle. Conversely, there is no relevant reason to attribute to her beliefs that fail to constitute knowledge in ⟨f,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle, the good case where appearances match reality perfectly. Thus we may assume that whatever she believes in ⟨f,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle she knows in ⟨f,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle. Consequently, she believes pp in ⟨e,f⟩\langle e,f\rangle if and only if she knows pp in ⟨f,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle. Thus doxastic accessibility is definable in terms of epistemic accessibility in our models: ⟨e,f⟩S⟨e*,f*⟩\langle e,f\rangle \mathrm{S}\langle e^{*},f^{*}\rangle if and only if ⟨f,f⟩R⟨e*,f*⟩\langle f,f\rangle \mathrm{R}\langle e^{*},f^{*}\rangle (so f=f*f=f^{*}). What the agent believes in a case is what she knows in the corresponding good case. As with (#1) and (#2), this is not claimed to be an exceptionless law of epistemology; rather, it is a natural idealization of a wide range of cases.

One consequence of our definition is that S\mathrm{S} is serial, for ⟨e,f⟩S⟨f,f⟩\langle e,f\rangle \mathrm{S}\langle f,f\rangle since ⟨f,f⟩R⟨f,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle \mathrm{R}\langle f,f\rangle. Thus the agent’s beliefs are always consistent. Moreover, a perfect match between appearance and reality is always doxastically possible.

For knowledge to imply belief, doxastic accessibility must imply epistemic accessibility. Our definition of the former in terms of the latter yields that result, given our constraints. For suppose that ⟨e,f⟩S⟨e*,f*⟩\langle e,f\rangle \mathrm{S}\langle e^{*},f^{*}\rangle. By the definition of S\mathrm{S}, ⟨f,f⟩R⟨e*,f*⟩\langle f,f\rangle \mathrm{R}\langle e^{*},f^{*}\rangle, so f=f*f=f^{*}. But by constraint (#2) R(⟨f,f⟩)⊆R(⟨e,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle)\subseteq \mathrm{R}(\langle e,f\rangle). Since ⟨e*,f⟩∈R(⟨f,f⟩)\langle e^{*},f\rangle\in \mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle), ⟨e*,f*⟩=⟨e*,\langle e^{*},f^{*}\rangle=\langle e^{*}, f⟩∈R(⟨e,f⟩)f\rangle\in \mathrm{R}(\langle e,f\rangle), so ⟨e,f⟩R⟨e*,f*⟩\langle e,f\rangle \mathrm{R}\langle e^{*},f^{*}\rangle, as required. That is some evidence that our definition of doxastic accessibility is a reasonable one.

The agent in our models is omniscient about her own current beliefs, in the sense that, if she believes pp, then she knows that she believes pp, and if she does not believe pp, then she knows that she does not believe pp. For what she believes supervenes in the model on appearances to her, and she is omniscient about those appearances. More precisely, the definition of S\mathrm{S} ensures that for all ee, e*e^{*} and ff in E\mathrm{E}, Bp\mathrm{B}p is true in ⟨e,f⟩\langle e,f\rangle if and only if it is true in ⟨e*,f⟩\langle e^{*},f\rangle. Consequently, if Bp\mathrm{B}p is true in ⟨e,f⟩\langle e,f\rangle, then it is true in every world epistemically accessible from ⟨e,f⟩\langle e,f\rangle, so KBp\mathrm{KB}p is true in ⟨e,f⟩\langle e,f\rangle. Similarly, if Bp\mathrm{B}p is false in ⟨e,f⟩\langle e,f\rangle, then it is false in every world epistemically accessible from ⟨e,f⟩\langle e,f\rangle, so K¬Bp\mathrm{K}\neg \mathrm{B}p is true in ⟨e,f⟩\langle e,f\rangle.

The agent does not satisfy the principle that if she believes pp then she also believes that she knows pp.14 For example, in ⟨f,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle, she believes, and indeed knows, R(⟨f,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle), but we noted in Section IV that she fails to know R(⟨f,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle) in the other worlds epistemically accessible from ⟨f,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle; since those worlds are also doxastically accessible from ⟨f,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle, she neither knows nor believes in ⟨f,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle that she knows R(⟨f,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle). This is an admirable form of epistemic modesty on her part. In the model, she cannot know that she knows R(⟨f,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle); she avoids believing that she knows R(⟨f,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle) because in the model such a belief cannot constitute knowledge.

VI.

We have modelled an internally rational agent whose beliefs depend only on appearances. Whatever she believes in a world, she knows in some world with the same appearance. In any world in such a model, all her beliefs are justified in an internalist sense. In some worlds, she has justified false beliefs. We saw in Section IV that the world ⟨f##,f⟩\langle f^{\#\#},f\rangle is epistemically inaccessible from the world ⟨f,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle: in the good case, the agent has non-trivial knowledge of the real value of the parameter. By the definition of S\mathrm{S}, ⟨f##,f⟩\langle f^{\#\#},f\rangle is doxastically inaccessible from itself. Thus in ⟨f##,f⟩\langle f^{\#\#},f\rangle the agent has a justified false belief that she is not ⟨f##,f⟩\langle f^{\#\#},f\rangle. In an internalist sense, she is justified in believing the gap between appearance and reality to be narrower than it really is. Similarly, internalists often say that a brain in a vat is justified in believing that it is not a brain in a vat.

In a strongly externalist sense, a belief is fully justified only if it constitutes knowledge, and is therefore true. Although one may have a cast-iron excuse for a false belief, that excuse does not amount to a justification. Similarly, consider a competent and non-negligent surgeon who causes the death of a patient because a lab technician with a grievance switched the labels on two bottles: he has a cast-iron excuse for killing the patient, but it does not amount to a justification for killing the patient.15 Trivially, in that externalist sense, there are no cases of justified true belief without knowledge; indeed, there are no cases of justified belief without knowledge. The interest of Gettier cases concerns a more internalist sense of ‘justified’, closer to excusability than to genuine justification. Gettier himself seems to have had such a reading in mind; he emphasizes that someone can be justified in believing a false proposition.16 After all, a sense of ‘justification’ explained in terms of ‘knowledge’ would not help the project of analysing knowledge in terms of justification and other factors. The internalist sense of ‘justified’, in which the beliefs of the agent in the model are justified even when false, is much more relevant to the debate over Gettier cases.

Every model of the kind we have described also contains cases of justified true belief without knowledge: Gettier cases. For recall the scenario derived in Section IV from our constraints: a world ⟨f##,f⟩\langle f^{\#\#},f\rangle is epistemically accessible from a world ⟨f#,f⟩\langle f^{\#},f\rangle, which is in turn epistemically accessible from ⟨f,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle, although ⟨f##,f⟩\langle f^{\#\#},f\rangle is not itself epistemically accessible from ⟨f,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle. The proposition R(⟨f,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle) is true in ⟨f#,f⟩\langle f^{\#},f\rangle. Moreover, the agent believes R(⟨f,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle) in ⟨f#,f⟩\langle f^{\#},f\rangle, for by the definition of S\mathrm{S} what she believes in ⟨f#,f⟩\langle f^{\#},f\rangle is what she knows in ⟨f,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle, and she knows R(⟨f,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle) in ⟨f,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle. We have just seen that in the relevant models the agent’s beliefs count as justified in the relevant sense. Thus in ⟨f#,f⟩\langle f^{\#},f\rangle the agent has a justified true belief in R(⟨f,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle). However, in ⟨f#,f⟩\langle f^{\#},f\rangle she does not know R(⟨f,f⟩\mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle, for R(⟨f,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle) is false in the epistemically accessible world ⟨f##,f⟩\langle f^{\#\#},f\rangle. Thus in ⟨f#,f⟩\langle f^{\#},f\rangle with respect to R(⟨f,f⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle f,f\rangle), the agent has a justified true belief that is not knowledge. Our constraints predict Gettier cases in epistemic logic.

The Gettier cases just described are not of the traditional type, in which the agent derives her justified true belief from a justified false belief in something else. Our Gettier cases arise in ⟨f#,f⟩\langle f^{\#},f\rangle, a world in which the agent lacks false beliefs: for if she believes pp in ⟨f#,f⟩\langle f^{\#},f\rangle then she knows pp in ⟨f,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle, so pp is true in ⟨f#,f⟩\langle f^{\#},f\rangle because the latter is epistemically accessible from ⟨f,f⟩\langle f,f\rangle. The cases above are more like ‘fake barn’ Gettier cases,17 in which the agent may lack relevant false beliefs but still the circumstances are not favourable enough for knowledge of the given truth. In our case, the unfavourable circumstance is the gap between appearance and reality, even though the agent does not doxastically endorse the appearance specifically enough to produce falsity.

Some but not all of our models also contain Gettier cases of a more traditional type. For example, in the real-valued models described in Section III, the agent in the world ⟨2c,0⟩\langle 2c,0\rangle has a justified belief in the false proposition R(⟨0,0⟩)\mathrm{R}(\langle 0,0\rangle), which is true just in worlds of the form ⟨e,0⟩\langle e,0\rangle where −c≤e≤c-c\le e\le c. Since her beliefs are closed under entailment, in ⟨2c,0⟩\langle 2c,0\rangle she also believes the ‘disjunctive’ proposition R(⟨0,0⟩)∨{⟨2c,0⟩}\mathrm{R}(\langle 0,0\rangle)\vee\{\langle 2c,0\rangle\}. Her belief in the disjunction is justified in ⟨2c,0⟩\langle 2c,0\rangle because she has a justified belief in its left disjunct, although no belief in its right disjunct. Moreover, in ⟨2c,0⟩\langle 2c,0\rangle the disjunction is true because its right disjunct is true, although its left disjunct is false. But in ⟨2c,0⟩\langle 2c,0\rangle she does not know the disjunction, for it is false in the world ⟨3c,0⟩\langle 3c,0\rangle, which is epistemically (though not doxastically) accessible from ⟨2c,0⟩\langle 2c,0\rangle. That is much closer to Gettier’s original cases.18

With respect to Gettier cases, the informal judgements of epistemologists and a more formal model-building methodology in epistemic logic converge. One can arrive at the same conclusion either way, and each method lends support to the other. The Gettier effect is robust.
