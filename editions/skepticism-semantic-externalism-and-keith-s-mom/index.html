Skepticism, semantic externalism, and Keith’s Mom

Timothy Williamson

The Southern Journal of Philosophy 38 (Supplement) (2000): 149–158.

A canonical argument for skepticism about our ordinary knowledge of the external world is this: we have ordinary knowledge of the external world only if we know that we are not brains-in-vats (BIVs); we do not know that we are not BIVs; therefore, we do not have ordinary knowledge of the external world. Hilary Putnam (1981) famously suggested that semantic externalism undermines the skeptical argument. The semantic externalist postulates necessary connections between the content of our thinking and the external world. According to Putnam, those necessary connections preclude BIVs from entertaining the proposition that they are BIVs. If so, there is a valid argument from semantic externalism and the plausible premise that we do entertain the proposition that we are BIVs to the conclusion that we are not BIVs.

One standard objection to Putnam’s argument is that for some of the less radical BIV scenarios, no plausible form of semantic externalism precludes BIVs from entertaining the proposition that they are BIVs. Perhaps you lack the requisite connections with the external world if you have always been a BIV, but surely you still have them if you were envatted only last night. Thus, as Keith DeRose notes in his illuminating paper, the semantic externalist argument against skepticism looks insufficiently general. DeRose also raises a new problem for the semantic externalist strategy. That is the main topic of this response.

Consider senses of “semantic externalism” and “BIV” in which semantic externalism does entail that BIVs cannot entertain the proposition that they are BIVs. Suppose that semantic externalism is true and that we do entertain the proposition that we are BIVs. Then the argument from semantic externalism and the auxiliary premise that we entertain the proposition that we are BIVs to the conclusion that we are not BIVs is not just valid but sound; its premises and conclusion are true. We might be tempted to assume that a sound argument to the conclusion that we are not BIVs constitutes an effective reply to the skeptic who denies that we know that we are not BIVs. As DeRose shows, that assumption is unwarranted. He forcefully presses the question: even if the argument is sound, how would it support our putative knowledge that we are not BIVs? After all, if we are not in fact BIVs, then the argument “We are not BIVs; therefore we are not BIVs” is a sound argument to the conclusion that we are not BIVs, but no one supposes that it would support our knowledge that we are not BIVs.

The obvious way for a valid argument to help us know its conclusion is this: we start by knowing its premises, and then by following the argument, come to know its conclusion. For that way to work, deduction must be a way to extend one’s knowledge, a closure assumption denied by some epistemologists, such as Robert Nozick (1981). However, both DeRose and I endorse the closure assumption. DeRose has argued elsewhere (1995) that apparent counterexamples to it cheat by changing the context in which the word “know” is used halfway through. However, DeRose points out, to come to know that one is not a BIV in the obvious way, by following the argument to the conclusion that one is not a BIV from semantic externalism and the premise that one entertains the proposition that one is a BIV, one must first know the premises of the argument. If one is indeed entertaining the proposition that one is a BIV, then it is perhaps not very difficult to know that one is, but one must also know the truth of semantic externalism. If, although semantic externalism is true, one does not know that it is, then the obvious way of using the argument to come to know that one is not a BIV is not open to one. DeRose notes that since most people in the world have never even heard of semantic externalism, they have no chance of coming to know in that way that they are not BIVs, even if they have heard of BIVs. Should we make the teaching of semantic externalism compulsory in schools, to enable future generations to know that they are not BIVs? If DeRose is correct in claiming that his Mom has not encountered Putnam’s argument, and she knows that she is not a BIV, then she knows it in some other way.

As DeRose argues, a further problem faces those of us who have heard of semantic externalism, for its epistemic status is far from secure. Although thought experiments provide strong support for the minimal claim that the content of one’s thinking does not supervene on one’s internal physical states, that minimal form of semantic externalism by itself has little impact on problems of skepticism, for it is not strong enough to preclude BIVs from entertaining the proposition that they are BIVs. Precluding that requires a specific kind of causal constraint on reference. Since we find it very hard to state such a constraint without immediate confrontation by counterexamples, probably no one yet knows the truth of any such constraint. Probably, therefore, no one has ever come to know that he or she is not a BIV in the “obvious” way, by following the semantic externalist argument to the conclusion that he or she is not a BIV.

Suppose that although the semantic externalist argument is sound, semantic externalism is not known to be true. Is there some less obvious way in which the argument would support our putative knowledge that we are not BIVs—even if we have never heard of semantic externalism? I will suggest that the semantic externalist has more scope for maneuver than DeRose is inclined to allow, and that DeRose’s own work provides such scope. I will show how semantic externalism is relevant to skeptical issues in a way quite different from the one DeRose considers, on at least some conceptions of knowledge. I will begin with a drastically oversimplified form of reliabilism, and then allow it to become more sophisticated in the direction of DeRose’s own account of knowledge attributions.

One infallibly believes pp if and only if one believes pp and, necessarily, if one believes pp then pp is true. All infallible belief is of course true belief. A drastically oversimplified form of reliablism says that knowledge is infallible belief:

(1)
For all pp, one knows pp if and only if one infallibly believes pp.

Now suppose that semantic externalism entails that, necessarily, no BIV entertains the proposition that it is a BIV (for given senses of “semantic externalism” and “BIV”). But, necessarily, in believing that one is not a BIV, one thereby entertains the proposition that one is a BIV, in order to deny it. Hence, necessarily, if one believes that one is not a BIV, then one is not a BIV, so one’s belief is true. That yields this principle:

(2)
If one believes that one is not a BIV, then one infallibly believes that one is not a BIV.

Given the right-to-left direction of (1), (2) entails:

(3)
If one believes that one is not a BIV, then one knows that one is not a BIV.

Thus semantic externalism and simpleminded reliablism entail that one knows that one is not a BIV, provided that one believes that one is not a BIV, even if one has never heard of semantic externalism, and a fortiori even if one does not know the truth of semantic externalism. The argument does not even require one to know that one entertains the proposition that one is a BIV.

The obvious objection to (1) is that, although it may permit one to know that one is not a BIV (courtesy of semantic externalism), it permits one to know very little else. For almost every proposition pp such that one is ordinarily said to know pp (for example, that one had breakfast), one can believe pp falsely. Thus, granted semantic externalism, (1) has the opposite effect to that of Nozick’s account of knowledge: (1) gives one knowledge that one is not a BIV but not ordinary knowledge, whereas Nozick’s account gives one ordinary knowledge but not knowledge that one is not a BIV.

The skeptical consequences of (1) just noted flow from its left-to-right direction; its antiskeptical consequences such as (3)—given semantic externalism—flow from its right-to-left direction. Thus if we replace “infallibly believes” in (1) by one of its weaker consequences, we might avoid the skeptical consequences while preserving the argument to (3). The simplest way of doing that would be to weaken the interpretation of “necessarily” in the definition of “infallibly believes.” In the new sense, “S\mathrm{S} infallibly believes pp” is true as uttered in a context c if and only if in every possible world relevant to c in which S\mathrm{S} believes pp, pp is true. Not every metaphysically possible world is relevant to every context. “S\mathrm{S} knows that she has had breakfast” is true as uttered in cc if every world in which S\mathrm{S} believes falsely that she has had breakfast is irrelevant to cc. Since contextually relevant possible worlds are still possible worlds, this reinterpretation of (1) preserves the argument to (3).

An alternative weakening of “believes infallibly” replaces the strict conditional by a counterfactual conditional. One sensitively believes pp if and only if one believes pp and if pp were not true one would not believe pp. All sensitive belief is true belief, because a counterfactual conditional entails the corresponding material conditional. In particular, “If pp were not true, S\mathrm{S} would not believe pp” entails the truth-functional “If pp is not true then S\mathrm{S} does not believe pp.” Moreover, all infallible belief is sensitive belief (on the strict original reading of “infallible”), for a strict conditional entails the corresponding counterfactual conditional. In particular, “Necessarily, if S\mathrm{S} believes pp, then pp is true” entails “If pp were not true, S\mathrm{S} would not believe pp” because it entails “Necessarily, if pp is not true, then S\mathrm{S} does not believe pp.” But not all sensitive belief is infallible belief, for one can believe sensitively but not infallibly that one has had breakfast. Thus we may consider this form of reliabilism:

(1*)
For all pp, one knows pp if and only if one sensitively believes pp.

Unlike (1) (on the original strict reading), (1*) does not imply that we lack ordinary knowledge. Now, since infallible belief entails sensitive belief, (2) entails:

(2*)
If one believes that one is not a BIV, then one sensitively believes that one is not a BIV.

Given the right-to-left direction of (1*), (2*) entails the original (3). Since the source of (2) and therefore of (2*) was semantic externalism, (1*) preserves the relevance of semantic externalism to BIV skepticism.

Nozick’s initial analysis of knowledge, before he introduces the complications of method, is close to (1*). He conjoins sensitive belief with the fourth condition “If pp were true, S\mathrm{S} would believe pp,” interpreted to mean that if pp were true but things were slightly different in some other way, S\mathrm{S} would still believe pp. The fourth condition interferes with the argument to (3), because it does not follow from infallible belief. Nevertheless, it is independently plausible that the fourth condition holds in ordinary cases when pp is the proposition that one is not a BIV, so the argument to (3) can go through with that extra premise. Nozick’s account preserves the relevance of semantic externalism to BIV skepticism, even when the complications of method are introduced.

DeRose’s account (1995, 1996) contains elements both of the contextualized (1) and of (1*). He does not accept (1*) as a true generalization. It conflicts with the reasonable closure principle that if one knows pp and believes qq on the basis of competent deduction from pp then one knows qq. For example, if bb is the proposition that I had breakfast this morning and ff is the proposition that I falsely believe that I had breakfast this morning as a result of confusing memories of today and yesterday, then I sensitively believe bb, and I believe ∼f\mathord{\sim} f on the basis of competent deduction from bb, but I do not sensitively believe ∼f\mathord{\sim} f. For DeRose, closure holds within contexts but not across them. He treats the truth-condition for knowledge ascriptions as similar in spirit to the contextualized version of (1), but postulates a conversational effect by which utterances of the form “S\mathrm{S} knows pp” tend to make the closest worlds in which pp is false contextually relevant, so that instances of (1) for contextually salient ascriptions of “knowledge” tend to be true. My utterance “I know that I had breakfast this morning” makes contextually relevant the closest worlds in which I did not have breakfast this morning; in those worlds my memory is not confused and I do not falsely believe that I had breakfast this morning. I sensitively believe bb and in an ordinary context “I know bb” expresses a truth. But when I deduce ∼f\mathord{\sim} f, my utterance “I know that I do not falsely believe that I had breakfast this morning as a result of confusing memories of today and yesterday” adds to the contextually relevant worlds the closest worlds in which I do falsely believe that I had breakfast this morning as a result of confusing memories of yesterday and today. I do not sensitively believe ∼f\mathord{\sim} f and “I know ∼f\mathord{\sim} f” expresses a falsehood. But in this new context “I know bb” expresses a different and false proposition. My belief in bb may be sensitive, but it is not infallible, even in the contextualized sense; I falsely believe bb in some contextually relevant worlds. The details of the conversational mechanisms do not concern us here. What matters is that, on DeRose’s account, the truth of the proposition expressed by the skeptic’s utterance “S\mathrm{S} does not know pp” in its context typically depends on the skeptic’s ability to make worlds in which S\mathrm{S} falsely believes pp contextually relevant. But if one infallibly believes pp, then there are no such worlds, and a version of the argument to (3) still goes through. Thus DeRose’s own account appears to suggest that at least some forms of semantic externalism falsify at least some forms of BIV skepticism.

At this point DeRose is entitled to remind us that, in many less radical senses of “BIV,” no plausible form of semantic externalism precludes BIVs from entertaining the proposition that they are BIVs. But if we do not know that we are not BIVs in one of those less radical senses, and the closure principle holds, then the skeptical consequences are still devastating. However, the picture changes when we consider a modification of the account forced on DeRose by some recalcitrant examples.

Let pp be the proposition that I don’t falsely believe that I have hands. If pp were false, I would falsely believe that I have hands, and would therefore believe something from which pp follows, and would therefore believe pp (we may assume). Thus my belief in pp is not sensitive. Intuitively, however, this example hardly threatens my knowledge that I don’t falsely believe that I have hands. In this case, the left-to-right direction of (1*) fails. The same goes for my knowledge that I’m not an intelligent dog who’s always incorrectly thinking that it has hands (1995, 22).

In response, DeRose proposes a rough qualification of (1):

We don’t … judge ourselves ignorant of pp where ∼p\mathord{\sim} p implies something we take ourselves to know to be false, without providing an explanation of how we came to falsely believe this thing we think we know. Thus, I falsely believe that I have hands implies that I don’t have hands. Since I do take myself to know that I have hands (this belief isn’t insensitive), and since the above italicized proposition doesn’t explain how I went wrong with respect to my having hands, I’ll judge that I do know that proposition to be false. (1995, 23; symbolism altered).

The passage suggests that the consequence of ∼p\mathord{\sim} p we take ourselves to know to be false (“I don’t have hands”) is falsely believed to be false if pp is false, and that ∼p\mathord{\sim} p does not explain why. From a third-personal perspective, we presumably judge S\mathrm{S} to know pp in these cases only if S\mathrm{S} believes pp at least in part because ∼p\mathord{\sim} p has the consequence we take S\mathrm{S} to know to be false; S\mathrm{S} does not know pp if S\mathrm{S} believes pp only on different and bad grounds. This reference to the grounds on which S\mathrm{S} believes is a step in the direction of Nozick’s methods, but not far enough to block the account’s partially skeptical consequences. The negation of the proposition that I am not a BIV induced to appear to itself to have hands does explain how I could falsely believe that I have hands, because that skeptical scenario has enough detail to be explanatory.

DeRose’s account as just stated is insufficiently general. For example, let A\mathrm{A} be a case in which I am climbing a mountain and appear to myself to be climbing a mountain; things appear to me as they are. In case B\mathrm{B}, I am a BIV, but things appear to me just as they do in A\mathrm{A}. If I were in B\mathrm{B}, I would believe that I was not in B\mathrm{B}. If pp is the proposition that I am not in B\mathrm{B}, then I do not sensitively believe pp. Nevertheless, in my actual present case, I know that I am not in B\mathrm{B}, because I am in neither A\mathrm{A} nor B\mathrm{B}; things do not appear to me at all as they would in B\mathrm{B}. I appear to myself to be sitting in front of a computer screen in my office. I do not appear to myself to be climbing a mountain, as I would in B\mathrm{B}.1 No matter what my situation, I cannot sensitively believe pp. This is a counterexample to (1*). Moreover, my false beliefs when I am in B\mathrm{B} are just as explicable as in other skeptical scenarios, while the example does not involve false beliefs when I am in either A\mathrm{A} or my actual case. Thus the example does not involve unexplained false belief at all, unlike the apparent suggestion in the quoted passage.

A small modification of DeRose’s suggestion does handle the example. Let qq be the proposition that I do not appear to myself to be climbing a mountain. Thus qq entails pp (that I am not in B\mathrm{B}), so ∼p\mathord{\sim} p entails ∼q\mathord{\sim} q; if I am in B\mathrm{B}, then I appear to myself to be climbing a mountain. I take myself to know qq; it is a belief about how things appear to me. I am mistaken about qq in none of the relevant cases. I sensitively believe qq, for if I appeared to myself to be climbing a mountain I would not believe that I did not appear to myself to be climbing a mountain. Moreover, in B\mathrm{B} I do not falsely believe qq; I believe ∼q\mathord{\sim} q, truly. Thus ∼p\mathord{\sim} p does not explain how I could falsely believe qq. One might, therefore, modify DeRose’s suggestion thus: when we judge that S\mathrm{S} knows pp without sensitively believing pp, we do so because S\mathrm{S} sensitively believes a proposition qq that entails pp, and ∼p\mathord{\sim} p does not explain how S\mathrm{S} could falsely believe qq. When S\mathrm{S} does sensitively believe pp, pp is itself such a proposition qq, granted that ∼p\mathord{\sim} p does not explain how one could falsely believe pp when one would not believe pp if pp were false. We might therefore consider the following modification of (1*):

(1**)
For all propositions pp, one knows pp if and only if for some proposition qq: qq entails pp, one sensitively believes qq and ∼p\mathord{\sim} p does not explain how one could falsely believe qq.

Further modifications could be made. We might require that S\mathrm{S} believes pp because S\mathrm{S} believes qq. We might allow the link between qq and pp to be looser than entailment. The discussion below will not depend on these details.2 The contextualist motivation for replacing (1*) by (1**) is that when we evaluate “S\mathrm{S} knows pp” we need not consider cases in which S\mathrm{S} falsely believes qq, because we have no contextually relevant explanation of how S\mathrm{S} could do so. Of course, DeRose is not committed to (1**) as a true universal generalization about knowledge but as a principle contextually salient instances of which tend to express truths. But let us consider the effect of (1**) on skeptical claims in those cases in which it does hold.

We are interested in the skeptical hypothesis that I am a BIV in a less radical sense of “BIV” in which no plausible form of semantic externalism precludes a BIV from entertaining the proposition that it is a BIV. Let the good case be one in which everything appears to me just as it actually does, and as far as possible is that way. Let the bad case be one in which I am a brain in a vat and everything appears to me as far as possible as it actually does. Let us reserve the term “BIV” for a brain in a vat in that bad case and assume that, semantic externalism notwithstanding, if I were a BIV, I would believe that I was not a BIV. If pp is the proposition that I am not a BIV, then I do not sensitively believe pp, although I may believe pp truly. According to (1**), do I know pp? Trivially, if I am a BIV, then I do not know pp, because pp is false. The question is whether I know pp in the good case, when I believe pp truly.

In the good case I may believe sensitively a proposition qq from which I deduce pp, and semantic externalism may prevent the BIV from entertaining qq, even though it does not prevent the BIV from entertaining the proposition that it is a BIV. For example, in the good case I am looking at a cloud in the sky. If I were a BIV, I could not see this cloud and would never have had any contact with it, however indirect. In the bad case, the BIV does not think about this cloud, although the BIV might think about clouds in general, and perhaps even about a cloud-image. In the bad case, I might understand the linguistic meaning of the words “I see this cloud,” but they would not express the proposition that I see this cloud, and I would be unable to entertain that proposition. Even if the BIV could have designated the cloud by some elaborate description, it does not do so, because I deploy no such description in the good case, and the bad case is relevantly similar. Let pp be the proposition that I am not a BIV, and qq the proposition that I see this cloud. Given what has been built into the current definition of “BIV,” qq entails pp. Since I cannot believe qq when pp is false, ∼p\mathord{\sim} p does not explain how I could falsely believe qq. In the good case, I sensitively believe that I see this cloud. If I did not see it, I would presumably be in some other ordinary situation and would not believe that I saw it. Even if I would or might be a BIV if qq were false, I would still not believe qq. In the good case, I competently deduce pp from qq, and believe pp on that basis. If I were a BIV, I would still have the premise token “I see this cloud,” and go through a process superficially like deduction, but my premise token would lack content. Thus, in the good case, the right-hand-side of (1**) is verified for the specified value of “pp” by the specified value of “qq.” Consequently, the effect of (1**) is that I know that I am not a BIV, even in a sense of “BIV” in which a BIV can entertain the proposition that it is a BIV. The result depends on semantic externalism, but not on the very strong causal constraints on reference that Putnam’s argument requires. Rather, it depends on a less contentious externalist conception of singular propositions on which one cannot think about a particular without some kind of special link to it, a link which I have to the cloud in the good case but not in the bad case.

In his earlier work, DeRose aimed to explain the appeal of skepticism by showing how skeptical claims express truths in the contexts in which they are made. Once his account is modified in the direction of (1**), semantic externalism jeopardizes that explanation.

Could DeRose handle the problem by modifying (1**) to speak of quasi-propositions rather than propositions, where quasi-propositions are like propositions except for being individuated in a semantically internalist manner? That is easier said than done, for we need to be told which narrow criterion is to individuate quasi-propositions; which internal differences are consistent with thinking the same quasi-proposition? Furthermore, the appeal to quasi-propositions looks suspiciously ad hoc. It needs some independent motivation if it is not to be a mere device for rigging (1**) in favor of the skeptic. Such a regression to internalist modes of thought also looks foreign to the otherwise externalist spirit of DeRose’s account.

A more general concern is that the simple forms of reliabilism considered have all been proposition-specific, in that they imply that infallibly believing pp is sufficient for knowing pp. That implication is false, of course. If one believes a mathematical truth, for example, then one believes it infallibly, for all mathematical truths are necessary truths, but one can believe a mathematical truth without knowing it, if one believes it on the basis of a thoroughly fallacious proof.3

It has become clear that on at least some accounts of knowledge, including versions of DeRose’s own account, there is a way of drawing antiskeptical consequences from semantic externalism not closed off by the arguments of DeRose’s paper. The crucial questions are left open here. Is one of those accounts of knowledge correct? Are the antiskeptical consequences as extensive as we desire? Could they be derived in no other way?

One methodological point is worth making. Semantic externalism is a doctrine whose implications are often surprising at first sight, although satisfying on further thought. We should not expect them to be intuitively obvious. Therefore, we should not revise our account of knowledge merely so that semantic externalism does not generate antiskeptical consequences that sound overweening when we first hear them. They might be true.
