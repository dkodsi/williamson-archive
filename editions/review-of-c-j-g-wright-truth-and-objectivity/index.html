Review of C.J.G. Wright, Truth and Objectivity

Timothy Williamson

International Journal of Philosophical Studies 2 (1) (1994): 130–144.

I. Realism and Truth

Faced with a discourse, many philosophers begin with the question: does it involve the making of true or false statements? In trying to understand moral discourse, for example, they ask whether a sentence like ‘Rape is wrong’ is used to say, truly or falsely, that something is the case, or merely to express an emotional attitude, without propositional content, to rape. Moral realism, in one sense of the term, is the claim that moral discourse does involve the making of true or false statements. Parallel forms of realism can be identified with respect to other discourses. This use of the term ‘realism’ presents the applicability of the notions of truth and falsity as an issue central to the status of the discourses in question. In his new book, Crispin Wright argues that the issue does not deserve that central place, which he reassigns to a bundle of other issues. For Wright, the existence of moral truths should not be particularly contentious; what we ought to be arguing about is their objectivity. Not that he supposes the word ‘objectivity’ to stand for anything clear: the book works hard to construct various precise notions of objectivity with which to capture what should be at stake in the debates labelled ‘realism v. anti-realism’.

Wright’s earlier work proceeded from a conception of such debates due to Michael Dummett. That conception assigns the central place to the relation between truth and knowledge. Dummett’s realist asserts, while his anti-realist denies, that it is possible for a statement made within a discourse to be true yet incapable of being known to be true by participants in the discourse. A realist theory of meaning for the discourse explains statements by giving necessary and sufficient conditions for them to be true, without any reference to the cognitive capacities of participants as such. These conditions (it assumes) either obtain or fail to obtain, even if participants cannot recognize which. According to the realist about mathematical discourse, for example, a conjecture is bivalent (either true or false), even if we can neither prove nor refute it. In the latter case, either it is an unknowable truth or its negation is. By contrast, an anti-realist theory of meaning explains statements by giving necessary and sufficient conditions for us to be warranted in asserting them. These conditions (it assumes) are either recognizable by participants as obtaining or recognizable by them as failing to obtain. According to the anti-realist about mathematical discourse, our practice entitles us to regard a conjecture as bivalent only if we can in principle either prove or refute it.

Dummett’s conception does not always capture what is at stake in debates labelled ‘realism v. anti-realism’. For example, a moral realist in Dummett’s sense would be someone who thought that there could be unknowable moral truths. But that is not the point at issue in the dispute about moral discourse. Those who hold that ‘Rape is wrong’ expresses a moral truth are also likely to hold that it expresses moral knowledge. Although a willingness to assert that there are unknowable moral truths may be sufficient to qualify one as a moral realist in the original intuitive sense, it hardly seems necessary. A broader conception than Dummett’s is therefore required of the geography of possible realisms and anti-realisms. The book is intended as a contribution to the required broadening.

Wright surveys a labyrinth of positions, most of them occupied by at least one philosopher. The goal of the book is not primarily to adjudicate between them, but rather to clarify the issues that such an adjudication would need to address. Only a selection of these issues can be discussed here. §II argues that the notion of truth is not quite as minimal as Wright suggests. §III considers his notion of superassertibility. To hold that superassertibility plays the role of truth with respect to a discourse is to be one kind of anti-realist with respect to that discourse. A difficulty will be raised for this kind of anti-realism. §IV considers Wright’s notion of Cognitive Command. To hold that a discourse fails to exert Cognitive Command is to be a different kind of anti-realist with respect to that discourse (although someone might be an anti-realist of both kinds). It will be argued that Cognitive Command is not defined clearly enough to resist the charge that it is satisfied trivially. §V questions the notion of a discourse in terms of which Wright frames his discussion.

II. Deflationism

Wright conceives the notion of truth as a superficial one. Its applicability to a discourse requires, on his view, only that the latter possess certain manifest features: its sentences should have both the syntactic capacities of declaratives and accepted standards for their proper use. In moral discourse, for example, superficial linguistic appearances favour moral truth. ‘Rape is wrong’ is a well-formed declarative sentence, grammatically isomorphic to ‘Philately is laborious’. It has a negation, and occurs as the antecedent and as the consequent of conditionals.1 One can therefore state a truth-condition for it homophonically: ‘Rape is wrong’ is true if and only if rape is wrong. Moreover, there are public standards for the use of such sentences, according to which moral utterances may be warranted or unwarranted. Wright suggests that we should not argue with these appearances. If a discourse appears to permit the making of true or false statements, then it really does so: not because the appearances are an infallible sign of something deeper, but because they constitute the capacity of the discourse for truth or falsity. Indeed, since the language of the discourse contains negation and the negation of a falsehood is a truth, the discourse includes truths if it includes falsehoods, so the appearances constitute the capacity of the discourse for truth. Truth is not a deep notion. If we want to ask a deep question about moral discourse, then we should ask whether it is objective.

Wright’s argument may prompt some unease. Take a miniature case: ‘Eldorado’ appeared to name a city, but did not really name anything at all. On one respectable view, no statement is then made by use of a sentence like ‘Eldorado is crowded’; it says nothing that is even a candidate for truth or falsity. It has, in Wright’s phrase, no assertoric content. Nevertheless, it appears capable of truth and falsity. It is a well-formed declarative sentence, grammatically isomorphic to sentences that are used to make true or false statements. It has a negation, and occurs as the antecedent or consequent of conditionals. One can therefore appear to state its truth-condition homophonically, by saying ‘“Eldorado is crowded” is true if and only if Eldorado is crowded’. Moreover, there appear to be public standards for the use of such sentences, according to which ‘Eldorado’-utterances are warranted or unwarranted. Wright gives us no reason to argue with these appearances.2 Yet one does not really state a truth-condition by saying ‘“Eldorado is crowded” is true if and only if Eldorado is crowded’, for what follows ‘if and only if’ has no assertoric content. Why should moral discourse not be in the same plight?

Wright does allow that, in special circumstances, we might be mistaken in supposing a discourse to have assertoric content (pp. 140–1). However, this is not to concede that the linguistic appearances are insufficient for assertoric content; the cases envisaged are ones in which we are merely over-hasty in assessing the linguistic appearances. Now it could be denied that there really is a distinction between warranted and unwarranted ‘Eldorado’-utterances. But if that denial is right, then the linguistic appearances are insufficient for a genuine standard of warrant, and a fortiori insufficient for assertoric content.

The sentence ‘Eldorado is crowded’ aspires to assertoric content. That aspiration may be constituted more generally by the linguistic appearances to which Wright appeals; it does not follow that they constitute the achievement of assertoric content. Suppose that moral discourse aspires to assertoric content without achieving it, perhaps because moral terms fail to refer, there being no properties for them to refer to of the required sort. Then moral discourse rests on an error. Wright briefly discusses such a view, as held by John Mackie (pp. 9–10, 86–7). The situation once recognized, Wright plausibly suggests, one could engage in moral discourse only in bad faith. Although Mackie’s view may be implausible, it is not obviously incoherent. If moral discourse does achieve assertoric content, its doing so is not conceptually guaranteed by the linguistic appearances.

It might be claimed that the failed aspiration to assertoric content is essentially a local matter. It can vitiate discourse involving a particular would-be name, but not something on the scale of moral discourse. However, Wright does not make this claim, and there does not seem to be any good argument for it. We cannot rely on some general guarantee that we shall never have reason to give up an extensive discourse as vitiated by reference failure. Nevertheless, Wright may be correct to this extent: if a discourse displays the linguistic appearances to which he points, then we can engage in it in good faith only if we are prepared to regard it as achieving assertoric content, and therefore as permitting us to make true statements.

If a discourse aspires to truth in virtue of displaying the appropriate linguistic appearances, how substantial a notion of truth is at issue? On a deflationist view, it is very insubstantial. According to one form of deflationism, ‘true’ is simply a device for endorsing assertions. That cannot be quite right as it stands, for it neglects unasserted occurrences of ‘true’, as in the antecedent of a conditional. However, not all forms of deflationism make that mistake. The more general view is that the content of ‘true’ is exhausted by disquotational equivalences such as: ‘Grass is green’ is true if and only if grass is green.

Wright interprets the deflationist as denying that ‘true’ introduces a discourse-governing norm over and above the norm of warranted assertibility. He argues, against this, that the linguistic appearances manifest a norm of truth distinct from the norm of warranted assertibility (pp. 19–21).3 For if the norm of truth coincided with the norm of warranted assertibility, then to criticize the assertion ‘PP’ as not warranted would be tantamount to criticizing it as not true, which would in turn be tantamount to asserting the negation of ‘PP’, given the very disquotational equivalences on which the deflationist relies.4 Yet, by accepted standards of warrant, the former criticism is often apt when the latter is not. An assertion made on the basis of a lucky guess may be true but unwarranted. In almost any discourse there will be situations in which there is neither warrant to assert ‘PP’ nor warrant to assert ‘It is not the case that PP’; the speaker may be in the dark. If truth were equivalent to warranted assertibility, these would be situations in which neither ‘PP’ nor ‘It is not the case that PP’ was true. By the disquotational equivalences, these are situations in which it is not the case that PP and it is not the case that it is not the case that PP. But that is a contradiction; there are no such situations. Thus truth is not equivalent to warranted assertibility.

Wright is correct in arguing that, if there is a norm of truth, it is distinct from the norm of warranted assertibility. Will the deflationist concede that there is a norm of truth? Assertion of ‘Grass is green’ is governed by a norm that is satisfied if and only if grass is green, and assertion of ‘Snow is white’ is governed by a norm that is satisfied if and only if snow is white; why suppose these to be the same norm? Wright considers the question, and answers that the norm is the same because all the disquotational equivalences can be gathered into a single schema, giving a uniform account of ‘true’. An utterance uu is true if and only if, for some PP, uu says that PP and PP (p. 31).5 This will not satisfy all deflationists. In effect, the truth of uu is equated with an infinite disjunction: either uu says that grass is green, and grass is green, or uu says that snow is white, and snow is white, or . . . Some deflationists may deny that such a disjunction of norms is itself a further norm. After all, it would be a mistake to argue that all games have the same set of rules, given by the infinite disjunction: either one is playing cricket and [the rules of cricket], or one is playing chess and [the rules of chess], or . . . Each game is governed by its own rules; there is no need for an extra universal rule that one should obey the rules of the game one is playing (if such a meta-rule were needed to make one obey the specific rules, a meta-meta-rule would be needed to make one obey the meta-rule, and so ad infinitum). According to this deflationist, the notion of obedience to the rules simply collects together the norms specific to particular games, and the notion of truth simply collects together the norms specific to particular utterances.

The denial that any one norm is satisfied by all true utterances may be interpreted as a corollary of another denial, issued by many deflationists, that any one property is common to all true utterances (pp. 14–15). Their denial can seem utterly baffling, for the ascription of truth to all those utterances seems precisely to be the ascription of a common property. However, things are not so straightforward. There is a distinction between predicates and properties, and a sense in which not every predicate stands for a property. For example, one may predicate ‘is white or green’ of both snow and grass, but in doing so one does not ascribe a common property to them, in a rich sense of the word ‘property’. Snow and grass do not resemble each other in both being white or green. In this sense, to predicate ‘is true’ of various utterances is not automatically to ascribe a common property to them. If the predicate ‘is true’ does not stand for a property, that would give point to the denial that it stands for a separate norm. Of course, one would like some elucidation of this rich notion of a property. Must a predicate figure in a scientific theory in order to stand for a property? If so, what entitles science to this privileged status? The challenge to these deflationists is to say what it is that truth is not.

A further difficulty for the idea that ‘true’ expresses different norms for different sentences arises from the possibility of combining different sentences into more complex ones, whose truth-conditions are determined by the truth-conditions of their constituents. ‘Snow is white or grass is green’ is true if and only if ‘Snow is white’ is true or ‘Grass is green’ is true. This threatens the analogy between sentences and games. There is no game of which both chess and cricket are constituents. If we did have a systematic way of combining any two games into a third game, it might require us to think of winning in a more unitary way. This point will be extended in §V.

Wright’s argument has at least the merit of emphasizing that explanations of ‘true’ as merely a device for endorsing assertions make no sense of our use of ‘not true’. One may add that the problem is not solved by the amended explanation that ‘true’ is merely a device for endorsing or denying assertions. The amendment still makes no sense of occurrences of ‘true’ in forms such as ‘If uu is true then QQ’; the antecedent is neither asserted nor denied in the assertion of the conditional. ‘Speech act’ analyses of ‘true’ are best forgotten. What remains is disquotation.

The slogan ‘Truth is disquotational’ can mean many things. Suppose that we are interested in the truth or falsity of an utterance uu. The disquotationality of truth amounts to at least the following pair of schemas, which formalize Aristotle’s dictum ‘To say of what is that it is not, or of what is not that it is, is false, while to say of what is that it is, or of what is not that it is not, is true’ (Metaphysics Γ 7.27):

(T)
If uu says that PP, then uu is true if and only if PP.
(F)
If uu says that PP, then uu is false if and only if not PP.

Strictly, ‘uu says’ here abbreviates ‘the utterer of uu says by uttering uu’, and ‘not PP’, ‘it is not the case that PP’. ‘PP’ is a schematic letter for which declarative sentences may be substituted. Of course, instances of (T) and (F) will say something (have assertoric content) in a given context only if the declarative sentence substituted for ‘PP’ says something in that context; thus (T) and (F) rely on a pre-given standard of saying. Not even the most extreme realist need find anything to quarrel with in (T) and (F). They may be regarded as simply the most fundamental principles governing the concepts of truth and falsity.

The slogan ‘Truth is disquotational’ may also be read as claiming that there is nothing more to the notions of truth and falsity than (T) and (F). What does this claim amount to? It may simply be the claim that (T) and (F) uniquely characterize the notions of truth and falsity. This may be made precise, as follows. Suppose that ‘true1’ and ‘true2’ both express notions of truth. Each therefore obeys (T) in place of ‘true’. But then one can argue that ‘true1’ and ‘true2’ are equivalent. For suppose that an utterance uu says that PP. By (T) for ‘true1’, uu is true1 if and only if PP. By (T) for ‘true2’, uu is true2 if and only if PP. Since ‘if and only if’ is symmetric and transitive, uu is true1 if and only if uu is true2. The only remaining case is that in which for no possible value of ‘PP’ does uu say that PP; but then uu says nothing, and therefore satisfies no genuine notion of truth; once again, albeit vacuously, uu is true1 if and only if uu is true2. Thus ‘true1’ and ‘true2’ are equivalent. In exactly parallel fashion, one can argue by appeal to (F) that if ‘false1’ and ‘false2’ both express notions of falsity, then they are equivalent. In this sense, the notions of truth and falsity are uniquely characterized by (T) and (F).6 Again, there is nothing here with which the most extreme realist need quarrel.

There is a different way of understanding the claim that there is nothing more to the notions of truth and falsity than (T) and (F), on which it is inconsistent with some forms of realism. For it may be claimed that our grasp of the notions of truth and falsity extends only as far as our grasp of the relevant instances of (T) and (F).7 This claim affronts the realist thought that we can coherently suppose there to be creatures with conceptual capacities vastly more powerful than our own, some of whose utterances express truths untranslatable into any language that humans can learn.8 If uu is such an utterance, uu does not say that PP for any value of ‘PP’ intelligible to us. The realist will insist that we can coherently apply our notions of truth and falsity to uu, because we grasp (T) and (F) as open-ended schemas; it is sufficient that they have instances whose antecedent is satisfied by uu, and not necessary that we grasp those instances. This is just what is denied by the claim that our grasp of the notions of truth and falsity extends only as far as our grasp of the relevant instances of (T) and (F). On the latter view, no true or false utterance involves concepts unavailable in principle to us. One may suspect this claim of depending on a neglect of the open-ended nature of schemas. After all, when we appreciate the validity of a logical law such as modus ponens, it is natural to suppose that we do not simply appreciate that all its instances in the language we happen to speak are valid. We appreciate the validity of the form, and therefore of an indefinite variety of its instances in extensions of our language, irrespective of whether those extensions are humanly learnable. Be that as it may, the contested claim provides a way of understanding the disquotationality of truth with anti-realist consequences. However, it is not a way discussed by Wright.

What Wright emphasizes is the philosophical neutrality of principles such as (T) and (F). Taken by themselves, they neither entail that truth and falsity are mind-dependent nor entail that they are not (in any sense of ‘mind-dependent’). They constitute common knowledge for realists and anti-realists. Nevertheless, Wright takes the philosophical neutrality of the notion of truth to have a bias: truth should be assumed anti-realist until proved realist. More precisely, minimalism about a discourse is the anti-realist claim that its truth predicate has no intuitively realist features beyond those required for it to qualify as a truth predicate at all. ‘Because of its unassuming character, this minimalism’, Wright suggests, ‘should always be viewed as the “default” stance, from which we have to be shown that we ought to move’ (p. 174). Unfortunately, Wright does not make it clear why it is more ‘unassuming’ to assume that the truth predicate has no intuitively realist extra features than to assume that it has no intuitively anti-realist extra features (nor why we need make either assumption prior to investigation).9 Moreover, minimalism is supposed to be inappropriate to at least some discourses (e.g. astronomy), which have at least some intuitively realist extra features. The idea that minimalism remains the default option therefore rests on the problematic idea that different discourses are governed by nonequivalent truth predicates (see §V).

The concepts of truth and falsity may be very simple ones. To acquire them, one may need to learn little more than (T) or (F). That point gives no support to any kind of anti-realism. Realists of various kinds require truth and falsity to be mind-independent in various ways. They do not require mind-independence to be written into the concepts, to be read off by their possessors. Realist views merely require mind-dependence not to be written into the concepts. There is, quite generally, no presumption that we can think of something mind-independent only by thinking of it as mind-independent. Dogs who want water (if there are such dogs) think of a mind-independent natural substance, but are presumably incapable of thinking of it either as mind-independent or as mind-dependent. To say that truth is mind-independent is not to say that everyone who thinks of truth thinks of it as mind-independent.

III. Superassertibility

The fundamental principle governing the notion of truth, (T), does not by itself make truth independent of the standards of warranted assertion operative in a discourse. No doubt truth cannot actually be warranted assertibility, for the reason rehearsed in §II, but, on the face of it, (T) permits truth to be a construction out of warranted assertibility. Wright introduces the notion of superassertibility as just such a construction. It is intended to be the nearest thing to warranted assertibility with a reasonable chance of satisfying (T). An item is superassertible if and only if:

it is, or can be, warranted and some warrant for it would survive arbitrarily close scrutiny of its pedigree and arbitrarily extensive increments to or other forms of improvement of our information (p. 48).10

Wright’s argument for distinguishing between truth and warranted assertibility does not automatically extend to an argument for distinguishing between truth and superassertibility. It is more obvious that we are actually in the dark on some matters than that we cannot escape from it however hard we try. An anti-realist interpretation of a discourse might therefore equate truth in it with superassertibility. The anti-realist thereby makes the large assumption that ‘superassertibility’ can be substituted for ‘true’ in (T), giving for all utterances uu in the discourse:

(S)
If uu says that PP, then uu is superassertible if and only if PP.

As Wright admits, his definition of superassertibility is vague in a number of important respects. For example, over what possibilities does the word ‘can’ range in the phrase ‘is, or can be warranted’? They must not include possibilities in which ‘the facts are different’ (as to whether PP), otherwise (S) will certainly fail, but these possibilities must be excluded without use of a truth predicate for the discourse, since that is what the anti-realist is trying to construct. However, such difficulties will not be pressed here. The notion of superassertibility is clear enough for a different problem to be raised, concerning the idea that truth might be equivalent to superassertibility in a discourse whose vocabulary is not tightly restricted.

Suppose that truth coincides with superassertibility in a discourse D\mathrm{D}. Let ‘QQ’ go proxy for a declarative sentence in D\mathrm{D} that can be used to say something in this context. If D\mathrm{D} has enough vocabulary, the following conjunction should also be expressible in D\mathrm{D}:11

(*)
QQ and no-one ever has a warrant to assert that QQ.

Let uu be an utterance expressive of (*) in D\mathrm{D}. Thus uu says that: QQ and no-one ever has a warrant to assert that QQ. By substituting (*) for ‘PP’ in (S) and using reasoning of a kind employed by Wright, we can deduce:

(1)
If uu is not superassertible, then not: QQ and no-one ever has a warrant to assert that QQ.

Now uu is superassertible only if some state of information provides a warrant to assert that: QQ and no-one ever has a warrant to assert that QQ. But a warrant to assert that conjunction is impossible, for it would involve a warrant to assert the first conjunct, that QQ, and the existence of such a warrant is contradicted by the second conjunct, that no-one ever has a warrant to assert that QQ. Thus uu is not superassertible. Given (1), this follows:

(2)
Not: QQ and no-one ever has a warrant to assert that QQ.

If we use classical logic, from (2) we can deduce:

(3)
If QQ then someone at some time has a warrant to assert that QQ.

Now (2) and (3) speak not of the existence or availability in principle of a warrant, but of the actual possession of one by a particular person at a particular time. Wright’s argument for distinguishing between truth and warranted assertibility requires that conditionals of the form ‘If QQ then I now have a warrant to assert that QQ’ can fail in the discourse. (3) is of course a weaker conditional, but it looks open to the same sort of objection. If, given that QQ, one has a warrant to assert that QQ only if one gets into the right position, what guarantee is there that anyone will ever actually happen to get into the right position? The claim that QQ only if someone at some time happens to get into the right position looks like an extreme form of subjective idealism. (2) and (3) can fail. Since they are the outcome of the assumed coincidence of truth and superassertibility in D\mathrm{D}, that coincidence can fail. Truth and superassertibility are not equivalent in D\mathrm{D}.

The argument leaves it open that superassertibility might be equivalent to truth in a discourse whose vocabulary is too limited to permit one to talk about warrants for assertion. If it is a moral question whether QQ, for example, it does not immediately follow that it is a moral question whether anyone will ever have a warrant to assert that QQ. Now there are general grounds for doubting the equivalence of truth and superassertibility even with respect to limited discourses.12 Suppose, however, that the two are equivalent within a discourse D\mathrm{D}. The significance of this point will depend on whether truth in D\mathrm{D} is simply the restriction of truth in general to D\mathrm{D}. If it is, then the point reveals something about the nature of D\mathrm{D}, not about the nature of truth; D\mathrm{D} is simply too limited to exemplify the general divergence between truth and superassertibility. If the point is to reveal more than that, truth in D\mathrm{D} must somehow be independent of truth in other discourses. §V will question the possibility of such independence.

IV. Cognitive Command

If I think that rhubarb is disgusting and you think that it is delicious, the idea that one of us is right and the other wrong may seem unattractive to a third party. It is tempting to say that deliciousness and disgustingness are relative to the observer – whatever that means. Again, many have been tempted by the appearance of irresoluble moral disagreements between societies to say that moral goodness and badness are relative to the society – whatever that means. Such relativism is one kind of anti-realism. Philosophers have sometimes been reluctant to discuss it, for it is notoriously hard to formulate in coherent terms, but its wide appeal makes it hard to ignore.

Wright uses the concept of Cognitive Command to put relativism (although he does not call it that) on his map as a coherent option. According to his definition, a discourse exerts Cognitive Command if and only it is a priori that differences of opinion formulated within the discourse, unless excusable as a result of vagueness in a disputed statement, or in the standards of acceptability, or variation in personal evidence thresholds, will involve something which may properly be regarded as a cognitive shortcoming (p. 144). Relativism with respect to a discourse becomes the denial that it exerts Cognitive Command. For example, the relativist might insist that it is not ruled out a priori that, when I say that rhubarb is disgusting and you say that it is delicious, neither of us may properly be accused of a cognitive shortcoming, even though our difference of opinion is not a result of vagueness in our statements, or in our standards of acceptability, or variation in our personal evidence thresholds.

Wright’s relativist is willing to apply a minimal notion of truth to the utterances at issue, for they display the required linguistic appearances. This creates a prima facie problem for relativism. Suppose that my opinion ‘Rhubarb is disgusting’ and your opinion ‘Rhubarb is delicious’ are both true. Then, by the disquotational property of minimal truth, rhubarb is disgusting and rhubarb is delicious. That, however, is impossible; ‘disgusting’ and ‘delicious’ are contraries (the point is even more obvious in the case in which you merely opine ‘Rhubarb is not disgusting’). It follows that our opinions are not both true. Thus it seems to be a priori that our difference of opinion does involve something which may properly be regarded as a cognitive shortcoming. The threat is that every discourse to which a minimal notion of truth applies will trivially exert Cognitive Command, in which case Wright has failed to capture a coherent relativist option.

Much depends on what is meant by ‘cognitive shortcoming’. If the phrase refers to the operation of those faculties by which we gain and process information about the world, then it is not obvious that an untrue opinion need constitute a cognitive shortcoming. We have an adequate supply of information only because our methods of gathering information are fallible; they could be infallible only by being cautious to the point of uselessness. Thus the healthy operation of our faculties occasionally results in the acquisition of untrue opinions without cognitive shortcomings in a restricted sense, when external conditions are misleading. However, this restricted notion of cognitive shortcoming is unsuitable for Wright’s purposes. A strict realist may recognize differences of opinion in which one opinion is true and the other is false, but no cognitive shortcoming in the restricted sense is involved, nor is the difference excusable as a result of vagueness in a disputed statement, or in the standards of acceptability, or variation in personal evidence thresholds.

Wright takes the threatened trivialization seriously, and tries to ward it off. His strategy is to argue that those who think that the discourse exerts Cognitive Command must regard truth in the discourse either as always recognizable by the participants or as sometimes transcending their powers of recognition, and that each possibility can be substantiated only by hard philosophical work (pp. 148–56). However, this seems off the point. The objector did not claim that, once it was conceded that the discourse exerted Cognitive Command, there could be no further philosophical disputes about it, such as that between realists and anti-realists in Dummett’s sense. Cognitive Command alone is threatened with trivialization. What Wright needs to meet the threat is a way of blocking the original trivializing argument. The only promising way of doing so is by a clarification of the notion of a cognitive shortcoming suitable to his purposes that would enable him to deny that the holding of untrue opinions must involve a cognitive shortcoming. No such clarification is supplied by Wright’s complex argument. Without it, Cognitive Command is too obscure to do philosophical work. A coherent relativism remains an elusive option.

V. Discourses

Wright treats discourse as divided into a variety of separate discourses. It is crucial to his argument that a notion of truth appropriate to one discourse may be inappropriate to another. There is moral discourse, mathematical discourse, discourse about the past, discourse about the funny, discourse about pain and so on. Wright is by no means the originator of this conception (Wittgenstein on language-games is one ancestor). At first sight it looks entirely natural. After all, we can separate the sentences in which a past tense is used from all others, and so on.

Doubt begins to seep in when one notes that the discourses are not disjoint from each other. The sentence ‘Yesterday only five of the twelve torturers inflicted pain, but the other seven who amused them with funny stories were equally bad’ concerns morality, mathematics, the past, the funny and pain together. More generally, suppose that the discourses D1\mathrm{D}_{1} and D2\mathrm{D}_{2} are both conducted in English, and meet Wright’s conditions for the applicability of a notion of truth. Let ‘A1A_1’ and ‘A2A_2’ be declarative sentences in D1\mathrm{D}_{1} and D2\mathrm{D}_{2} respectively. Thus ‘Either A1A_1 or A2A_2’ is also a declarative sentence of English. Some notion of truth is applicable to both the disjunction and its disjuncts, for otherwise the platitude that ‘Either A1A_1 or A2A_2’ is true if and only if either ‘A1A_1’ is true or ‘A2A_2’ is true would be vitiated by equivocation.13 Thus at least one notion of truth is applicable to both ‘A1A_1’ and ‘A2A_2’, and to longer sentences in which they are embedded. There is no need for a notion of truth applicable to ‘A1A_1’ but not to ‘A2A_2’, or vice versa. By the argument for unique characterization in §II, such a restricted notion would anyway be equivalent within its domain to the more general one.

Nothing just said militates against the possibility that interesting generalizations can be made about truth for restricted classes of utterances, generalizations not applicable to all utterances saying that something is so. The mistake is to claim that the generalizations involve different notions of truth, as though the word meant something different in the phrases ‘mathematical truth’ and ‘moral truth’. A natural language is strongly unified in syntax and semantics. Any finite set of its words can be combined within the unity of a sentence. The notion of truth must respect and reflect this integrity. Truths are many; truth is one.14

The unity of a natural language shows that we end with a single notion of truth for that language. It does not immediately show that we begin with one. Might we begin with many separate notions of truth, and gradually paste them together? Such an account corresponds to nothing in the process by which we learn the language. We do not first understand phrases such as ‘mathematical truth’ and ‘moral truth’ as unstructured idioms. We learn them in the obvious way, just as we learn the phrases ‘mathematical confusion’ and ‘moral confusion’, by first understanding their component words. ‘Truth’ comes before ‘mathematical truth’ and ‘moral truth’. The latter merely restrict the former to mathematical and moral discourse respectively. Our general notion of truth allows us to ask whether an utterance of an unfamiliar kind is true, without learning a new notion of truth for it. The existence of different notions of truth for different discourses is a myth.

The unity of truth threatens Wright’s piecemeal approach. He assumes that the central question at issue between realists and anti-realists with respect to a discourse D\mathrm{D} is ‘What is the appropriate notion of truth for D\mathrm{D}?’, and that the answer is likely to be quite different from one discourse to another. The aim is to construct a taxonomy of notions of truth, and therefore a taxonomy of discourses. If there is only one notion of truth, this divide-and-conquer strategy fails, for no genuine division is possible. The central question has the same answer in every case.

The unity of truth does not threaten all piecemeal approaches. It is not necessary to assume that every question at issue between realists and anti-realists does concern the appropriate notion of truth for a discourse. For example, we can ask whether the meaning of ‘wrong’ (or the property of wrongness) is such that something is wrong if and only if it is superassertible that it is wrong, and that is quite different from asking whether the meaning of ‘wet’ (or the property of wetness) is such that something is wet if and only if it is superassertible that it is wet. Some of Wright’s discussion could be recast in such terms. Even here, however, ‘wrong’ and ‘wet’ cannot be treated in isolation. Superassertibility that it is wrong or that it is wet depends on the available ways of referring to it; consideration of these ways will take one back towards the language as a whole. If one is to work with a single notion of truth, then sooner or later one must ask how far it is a realist one. For example: can there be unknowable truths? That question is not best approached piecemeal. Indeed, Wright sketches a perfectly general argument for a negative answer to it from Wittgenstein’s rule-following considerations, but does not press the matter (p. 228).15

Truth and Objectivity is a strikingly resourceful and serious book, imbued with respect for the difficulty of philosophical problems and a readiness to probe them with all the conceptual instruments of contemporary analytic philosophy. The richness of Wright’s discussion has only been hinted at in this review. What is less obvious is that the problems are best tackled by the proliferation of complexity. Some of the main issues are, after all, quite stark. The quality of the details in Truth and Objectivity is so high that one is ungratefully disappointed when one discovers that their sum is not a new or compelling vision of the issues as a whole. It is not obvious that such a vision is possible; it is not obvious that it is not.
