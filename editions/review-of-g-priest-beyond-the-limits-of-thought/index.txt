# Review of G. Priest, Beyond the Limits of Thought

Timothy Williamson

British Journal for the Philosophy of Science 47 (2) (1996): 331–334.

Graham Priest is a dialetheist: he believes that some contradictions are true. It is notoriously hard to pin down a fallacy in the reasoning that yields the semantic and set-theoretic paradoxes; Priest’s explanation is that there is no fallacy. In classical logic, any proposition whatsoever can be deduced from a contradiction, so contradictions induce trivialization. Priest therefore rejects classical logic in favour of paraconsistent logic, in which a contradiction lacks such global consequences. This book, however, makes only passing mention of logical issues, for which Priest refers the reader to his In Contradiction (Dordrecht, Kluwer, 1987). Rather, it sets the paradoxes within a wider context. According to Priest, thought runs into true contradictions when it runs up against its own limits, and the book attempts to trace this pattern through the history of philosophy. Dialetheism must hope to gather from such an exercise support independent of that which it claims to receive from the semantic and set-theoretic paradoxes themselves.

Priest identifies four kinds of limit to thought: limits of expression (he discusses Plato on the need for something unchanging as a precondition of meaning; Aristotle on prime matter; Cusanus on the ineffability of God); limits of iteration (infinite regresses in Zeno, Aristotle, and Leibniz); limits of cognition (Sextus’ skepticism and Protagoras’ relativism); limits of conception (Anselm’s ontological argument and Berkeley’s inconceivability argument for idealism). Kant and Hegel are credited with having recognized the underlying pattern, although only Hegel regarded the contradictions as unavoidable. Hegel on the contradictory nature of the true infinite leads naturally to the paradoxes of set theory, and the analogous semantic paradoxes, the standard solutions to which Priest attacks. In particular, he accuses them of violating Cantor’s principle that for every potential infinity there is a corresponding actual infinity, a principle which he defends as a precondition for the meaningfulness of thought about the infinite. The final part of the book extends the argument to the limits of language: Frege’s claim that the concept horse is not a concept; the saying of what cannot be said in Wittgenstein’s Tractatus; Quine and Davidson’s application of the supposed indeterminacy of reference to the theorist’s own language; Kripke’s Wittgenstein and skepticism about meaning; Derrida’s use of ‘différance’ to express the inexpressible precondition of all expressing.

Unfortunately for dialetheism, Priest’s admirably eclectic survey does little to extend its evidential base. He is too good a logician to ignore the flaws in the paradoxical arguments he considers up to and including Kant’s antinomies. The nearest miss is Berkeley’s inconceivability argument. In response to the objection that ‘I can conceive that something is such that no one can conceive it’ does not entail ‘something is such that I can conceive that no one can conceive it’, Priest uses Hilbert’s $\varepsilon$-operator in an attempt to bridge the gap, via the intermediate step ‘I can conceive that no one can conceive $\epsilon\mathrm{x}$ (no one can conceive $\mathrm{x}$)’. Edo Pivčević anticipated this line of thought in The Concept of Reality (London, Duckworth, 1986). Unlike Pivčević, Priest uses it for dialetheist rather than idealist ends because he thinks that we can conceive truly that something is such that no one can conceive it. The result of his modifications, although intriguing, is but a variation on König’s Paradox about the least undefinable ordinal (which has just been defined); as such, it lends no support to dialetheism independent of the semantic and set-theoretic paradoxes. As for the contradictions at the limits of language, they are too loosely presented to carry conviction. For example, the arguments about rule-following and the indeterminacy of reference appear to rest on reductionist assumptions about meaning for which Priest offers no support. The case for dialetheism has not been extended beyond the usual paradoxes.

Priest emphasizes the Principle of Uniform Solution: same kind of paradox, same kind of solution. Only dialetheism satisfies it, he claims. The Principle itself is hardly contentious: as he acknowledges, the difficulty is in knowing when two paradoxes are of the same kind. Priest classifies the central paradoxes in the book as of one kind by a formal criterion. They all instantiate his Inclosure Schema, a generalization of one stated by Russell in ‘On Some Difficulties in the Theory of Transfinite Numbers and Order Types’. An instance of Inclosure consists of properties $\phi$ and $\psi$ and a function $\delta$ such that:

(1)
$\Omega = \{\mathrm{y} : \phi(\mathrm{y})\}$ exists, and $\psi(\Omega)$
(2)
$\begin{aligned}\text{if }\mathrm{x} \subseteq \Omega\text{ and }\psi(\mathrm{x})\text{ then}\quad &\text{(a) }\delta(\mathrm{x}) \notin \mathrm{x}\\ &\text{(b) }\delta(\mathrm{x}) \in \Omega.\end{aligned}$

Substitution of ‘$\Omega$’ for ‘$\mathrm{x}$’ in (2) yields a contradiction with (1). For example, in König’s Paradox $\phi$ is the property of being a definable ordinal, $\psi$ the property of being definable, and $\delta(\mathrm{x})$ the least ordinal not in $\mathrm{x}$ (given a reading of ‘definable’ on which not all ordinals are definable). Priest admits that any paradox whatsoever can be dressed up as an instance of Inclosure, but argues that only some paradoxes can be explained as instances of it; they are supposed to form the kind.

Priest may have drawn the boundaries of the kind too narrowly. Consider the Liar:

(L)
$\neg\operatorname{True}(\mathrm{L})$

Following Curry, one can form a variant paradox by using a conditional $\to$ and an absurdity $\bot$ that entails everything (e.g. ‘Everything is true’; Priest allows $\bot$, merely denying that every contradiction behaves like it):

(CL)
$\operatorname{True}(\mathrm{CL}) \to \bot$

One half of a disquotational principle about truth gives $\operatorname{True}(\mathrm{CL}) \to (\operatorname{True}(\mathrm{CL}) \to \bot)$; a principle of Absorption gives $\operatorname{True}(\mathrm{CL}) \to \bot$; the other half of the disquotational principle gives $(\operatorname{True}(\mathrm{CL}) \to \bot) \to \operatorname{True}(\mathrm{CL})$; two steps of detachment give $\operatorname{True}(\mathrm{CL})$ and then $\bot$; $\bot$ gives everything. Is this an instance of Inclosure? According to Priest, it depends on what $\to$ is. If $\to$ is a material conditional, then $\operatorname{True}(\mathrm{CL}) \to \bot$ is equivalent to $\neg\operatorname{True}(\mathrm{CL})$, the paradox is Liar-like and does instantiate Inclosure. On other readings of $\to$, however, $\operatorname{True}(\mathrm{CL}) \to \bot$ is not equivalent to $\neg\operatorname{True}(\mathrm{CL})$. For Priest, the paradox then belongs

to a quite different family. Such paradoxes do not involve negation and, a fortiori, contradiction. They therefore have nothing to do with contradictions at the limits of thought (p. 186).

This is dubious. Suppose, for example, that $\to$ is strict implication. Then CL is equivalent to something like ‘This statement is necessarily false’. A treatment of ‘This statement is false’ that does not generalize to ‘This statement is necessarily false’, and is forced to consign the latter to some quite different kind, appears to violate the Principle of Uniform Solution. Contradictions are a symptom of the underlying condition; they are not the condition itself.

Priest’s unconvincing discussion of Curry paradoxes hints at deeper problems for dialetheism. Its strength is supposed to be its ability to preserve intuitively compelling inferences in semantics and set theory, by preventing them from trivializing. Curry paradoxes show that this strategy does not generalize, because they trivialize even on dialetheist terms. Dialetheism must therefore reject those intuitively compelling arguments. For example, it rejects detachment for material conditionals (Priest sometimes uses detachment without comment; he must have some other reading of $\to$ in mind). These moves look as ad hoc, as lacking in independent motivation, as many of the proposed solutions to which Priest objects. The exposition largely obscures this point (there are occasional warning signs). Although the suppression of logical issues is supposed to make the book accessible to logically innocent readers, it prevents them from assessing the costs of dialetheism.

Nevertheless, the book has many virtues. It is clever, resourceful, undogmatic, unpretentious, often sensible and usually clear over a wide range of issues. If one abjures contradictions, it is certainly harder to think about the limits of thought; but that extra difficulty may in the end produce greater depth.
