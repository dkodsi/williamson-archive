# Review of T.E. Burke, The Philosophy of Popper

Timothy Williamson

Hermathena 140 (1986): 114–115.

Sir Karl Popper holds that no aspect of human theory or practice is above criticism. Dr Burke is more concerned with this fallibilist principle as such than with the details of its application to politics or science. He offers a thoughtful critique of the canon, thorough, untechnical, unfussy and wisely free from paraphrase, since Popper is an easy philosopher to read. Burke argues the plausible case that, whatever Popper says, his fallibilism is ill-placed to withstand two of its intended victims, relativism and scepticism. In effect, Popper tries to leave criticism above criticism: he writes as though falsification, but not verification, were conclusive. Given that criticisms too can be mistaken, he cannot explain where our admittedly tenuous grip on reality comes from. However, Burke accepts Popper’s dilemma, either complete certainty for some statements or complete uncertainty for all; neither properly considers the alternative, that all statements are more or less probable or improbable. Some assumptions are in more urgent need of criticism than others; it does not follow that some are in no need of criticism at all.

Dr Burke thinks that, to avoid relativism, he must rule that any difference in how we investigate makes a difference in what we investigate (p. 103). Thus if a table looks perfectly smooth, physics cannot tell us that it is not (p. 111); my dream-thought ‘I am not dreaming’ is not falsified when I wake up (p. 113); what I praise as ‘killing for the sake of honour’ cannot be what you condemn as ‘murder’ (p. 200). Burke does not mention that the main detailed semantics based on these verificationist principles is the intuitionist one for mathematics; it rejects the law of excluded middle and revises classical theorems wholesale. Such a cure (an ‘innocuous relativism’, p. 119) is as painful as the disease. Fortunately, we need not choose between Burke’s alternatives, since we can reject his assumption, for which I could find no support, that unargued choices cannot be erroneous (p. 86). Indeed, they need not even be unjustified: he points out that we cannot adopt our first criteria for valid arguments on the basis of argument, but ignores Quine’s point that these criteria have evolved under the demands of an inhospitable environment. Nor does Burke consider the standard charge that relativism is self-refuting: it claims that all criteria are equally good because there is no neutral standpoint from which to judge them, yet this claim itself seems to presuppose a neutral standpoint, for without one, as Quine argues, we must judge from our own standpoint, which rejects many criteria as worse than ours and may be able to accept others as improvements.

Dr Burke concedes less to scepticism than to relativism. He says that if we always miscounted, ‘count’ would be meaningless; could not the sceptic agree, happy to throw his language into question along with everything else? Against Popper’s final defence, from the concept of approximation to the truth, Burke understates his case: Popper has had to abandon both the quoted definition (p. 127), for its untoward technical consequences, and his well-publicized abstention from inductive inference, a feat on which the fallibilism had been supposed to depend. These facts, however, will not surprise the reader of this unfashionable but attractively serious work.
